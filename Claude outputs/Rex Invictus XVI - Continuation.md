**NEW ROME**

The trouble with her Varlet's body, Minerva decided about four seconds into the chase, was that it had been built for a war she did not know how to fight.

She knew how to run. Running was mechanics: force, angle, friction, timing. She had run his body through the ruins of Olympus for fourteen thousand years and through the Kilns the night before, and she could make it move faster than any legacy alive. At the end of the alley she scooped Verrill up onto her back without breaking stride. Verrill yelped and locked her arms around Minerva's neck. Minerva hit the main boulevard at a speed that turned the rain on her face into needles.

The trouble was the water.

It was *everywhere*. It was falling out of the copper sky in sheets. It was running down the gutters and pooling in the potholes and roaring through the storm drains under the street. It was hanging in the air as mist and steam and the breath of ten thousand people. Every drop of it knew exactly who she was. Every drop of it was turning toward her like a flower toward the sun, *wanting* something, and she had absolutely no idea what.

Her Varlet had never had to think about it. For him, the sea was a limb. He reached, and it came. He pushed, and it moved. He had tried to explain it to her once, a very long time ago, on the ruined slopes of Olympus. *It's not math, Minerva. You just ask it nicely.*

Minerva did not know how to ask anything nicely. She only knew how to calculate.

She calculated now. She needed more speed. The rain on the boulevard was ankle-deep. If she could get the water beneath her feet to push instead of drag, it would be like running on a conveyor belt. She reached for it with his instinct and her precision, both at once.

The boulevard exploded.

"I don't know how!"

She hadn't been aiming at the pigeon. She hadn't been aiming at anything at all.

They leapt the gap between two towers so wide that Verrill stopped screaming and simply held on in silence. Then they crossed the Colosseum Gigantus.

They went right over the top of it, in the middle of a live broadcast. Minerva came down on the rim of the arena and ran along it, and forty thousand people looked up from the sand where a Megafighter Mark III was trying to kill a T-rex. They saw a boy with a girl on his back sprinting along the edge of the stands. A pillar of lightning with an angry teenage girl in the middle of it was coming up behind them. The T-rex looked up too. It opened its enormous jaws, roared, and snapped at Thalia as she passed overhead.

Minerva was learning the water. She was learning it fast, in the way she learned everything, by making mistakes and cataloguing them. She still overcorrected. Every time she reached for the rain, she got too much of it. It came in walls and waves and geysers, it blew out windows and flooded gutters, and once it floated an entire parked car. But she was beginning to feel the shape of it, the way her Varlet had described. It wasn't math. It was something closer to a conversation. It was something she had never once in her existence had with anything.

It was *terrifying*.

It was also, she thought, wiping rain out of her Varlet's eyes as she vaulted a rooftop air-conditioner at twice the speed of sound, the most interesting thing that had happened to her in a very long time.

Behind her, Thalia was losing ground.

Thalia shouldn't have been. She was faster than Minerva. Lightning was faster than water, and it always had been. On a straight line she would have caught them in thirty seconds.

But she kept stopping.

She stopped when the boy vaulted a railing one-handed, the exact way Percy used to vault the fence at the strawberry fields. She stopped when the girl on his back shouted something to him and he shouted back, and she heard the rhythm of it. It wasn't the words, just the rhythm of two people who argued with each other the way other people breathed. She had heard that rhythm for years on a hill on Long Island, a very long time ago. She stopped when they passed over the Via Heroum, and she saw the Monument of the Two lit gold beneath her, and the two figures running across the roof above it. For one dizzy moment, she honestly could not tell which of them was the statue.

And she stopped every time a civilian got in the way. That happened a great deal, because it was ten o'clock at night in New Rome and the city was *full*. Thalia was a Hunter of Artemis. The Hunt protected the innocent before anything else. That was the oath, fourteen thousand years old and never once broken. Every time a family stepped out of a restaurant into the path of the lightning, every time a drunk wandered onto a rooftop, every time a car skidded in the flood the boy left behind him, Thalia had to break off, pull up, catch them, set them down and start again.

The boy never had to stop. The water got out of the way for him. Everyone else just got wet.

"*Lieutenant!*" Naomi again. "*Lieutenant, they're headed for the Needle!*"

Thalia looked up.

---

The Themis Needle was the tallest building in the Voss System.

Solon Themis had built it in the second year of the war, when the Princes still believed they could win. It was a single spire of black glass and steel two and a half kilometers tall, rising out of the center of New Rome like a spear driven into the earth. At its peak was a broadcast mast. Below that was an observation deck, and below that the offices of the Sovereignty's planetary government. Below those were three hundred and sixty floors of corporate headquarters, embassies and very expensive apartments. Aircraft kept their distance from it. Weather broke around it. From its summit, on a clear night, a person could see the whole basin and the mountains beyond it. Straight up, they could see the golden strings of Dominion lanterns climbing toward the Yamamoto Relay, and the lights of the orbital traffic lanes.

Minerva went straight up the side of it.

She didn't take the stairs or the elevators. She went up the *outside*, the black glass sheer and slick with rain. Glass was hard to climb. Water, on the other hand, went wherever she told it to, more or less. She ran at the base of the Needle and asked the rain on its surface, very nicely, to hold her.

The rain held her.

It was not graceful. The water went up the side of the tower in a column, with her in the middle of it, and more or less *threw* her upward. It went in bursts and lurches and one terrifying moment when it forgot what it was doing and let go entirely. Minerva slid thirty meters back down the glass on her Varlet's stomach with Verrill shrieking on her back before she caught it again. But it went *up*. Floor after floor after floor streamed past, offices and apartments and a startled ambassador in a bathrobe. The city dropped away below them, and the wind rose to a howl.

"*I hate this!*" Verrill shouted in her ear. "*I hate this so much! I've never hated anything this much in my whole life!*"

"You're doing very well!"

"*I'm not doing anything!*"

"That's what you're doing very well!"

Below them, the lightning hit the base of the tower.

Thalia came up the Needle in a series of jagged leaps, bolt to bolt, from window ledge to maintenance gantry to antenna array. Each strike blew out a ring of windows and left a scorch mark on the black glass. She was *fast*. She was faster than the water, and she was gaining with every leap. Minerva glanced down, and her Varlet's stomach dropped. Thalia was two hundred meters below. Then a hundred and fifty. Then a hundred.

Then Thalia slowed down.

She didn't stop, and she didn't turn back. But the leaps got shorter, and the gaps between them got longer. Every time she landed, she landed pressed flat against the glass, with her fingers splayed and her face very pale. Minerva had fourteen thousand years of her Varlet's memories. It took her about a thousandth of a second to find the one she needed.

*Thalia Grace, daughter of the sky, was afraid of heights.*

She always had been. She'd been terrified in the back of Apollo's sun-chariot and terrified on the Hoover Dam. She'd held on to a railing on the side of Mount Tamalpais so hard that her knuckles went white. Percy had teased her about it, very gently, for years.

Minerva felt a stab of something she didn't have a name for. She filed it away to think about later.

Then she reached the top of the Needle.

**THE THEMIS NEEDLE, SUMMIT**

**16231.4.4.2 | 22:31**

The summit of the Themis Needle was a narrow platform of steel grating around the base of the broadcast mast, forty meters across, ringed by a waist-high railing. There was nothing above it but the mast and the sky. Rain blew across it sideways in the howling wind. New Rome lay spread out below it, two and a half kilometers down, a sea of copper lights stretching to the mountains.

Minerva set Verrill down on the grating. Verrill immediately sat down, grabbed the railing with both hands and closed her eyes.

"Tell me when it's over."

"Soon."

Minerva stood at the center of the platform, closed her Varlet's eyes, and opened every door she had.

It had taken her eleven seconds to pull her viruses out of the Bakuto. It took her four-tenths of a second to put them back into the whole of New Rome at once.

She went out through the broadcast mast, the tallest antenna in the Voss System. It was the one that carried every signal in the basin, and she went into all of them. Traffic grids. Maglev controls. Public screens. Security cameras. Every networked lock and light and lift in eleven kilometers of city. Every vehicle with a transponder. She poured through them like water through a broken dam, and for four-tenths of a second, Minerva *was* New Rome. She was every screen and every light and every signal. She could see the whole city at once, the way her Varlet could see the whole sea.

She saw Naomi and the other Hunters converging on the base of the Needle, with Columbina leading the pack. She saw the pit boss at the Bakuto, still holding the Greek copy of the policy. She saw the grey mutt behind the coat-check counter, with its head on its paws, looking sad. She saw the bouncer at the weapons check turning a small black cube over and over in his gauntlets, looking thoughtful.

She saw the skyport.

It was on the eastern edge of the basin: nine launch pads, and on pad four, an Astralnet orbital shuttle, cleared for departure, engines hot. It was a long, sleek thing of silver and blue with the Astralnet crest on its tail, and its registry read *SCHEDULED SERVICE 118: NEW ROME TO VOSS HIGH ANCHORAGE*. It carried two hundred passengers. It was forty seconds from lift-off.

Minerva went into its flight computer.

It was an Astralnet flight computer, which meant it was very good and very proud and utterly convinced that it knew the safest route to orbit. It argued with her for nearly a thousandth of a second. Then she rewrote its flight plan. She didn't change much. She only moved its departure corridor eleven kilometers west, so that it would climb out of the basin not over the mountains but directly over the center of New Rome. Its ascent path would clip the top of the tallest building in the Voss System with a clearance of exactly four meters.

She also, while she was in there, told every public screen in New Rome to play the same image. It was the T-rex from the Colosseum Gigantus, sitting in the sand with a very surprised expression and a small scorch mark on its nose. She thought Thalia deserved that.

Then she opened her Varlet's eyes.

Thalia came over the edge of the platform on her hands and knees.

She had made the last fifty meters without lightning. She'd climbed hand over hand up the maintenance ladder on the side of the mast housing, with the wind trying to tear her off it and the city two and a half kilometers down, and her face was the color of paper. She pulled herself up onto the grating and stayed there on all fours, breathing hard. Then she looked up.

The boy was standing at the center of the platform, ten meters away, with the rain streaming down his face and the city lights behind him.

The girl was sitting against the railing with her eyes closed.

For a long moment, none of them said anything.

"Percy," said Thalia.

Her voice cracked. She didn't try to stop it.

The boy looked at her. His sea-green eyes were very, very sad.

"No," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm really not."

"You..."

"I know. I have his face. I have his hands. I even have his terrible habit of running away from you." He smiled, a small crooked smile, the exact smile. "But I'm not him, Thalia. He isn't here. He hasn't been here for a very long time. I'm just the thing he left behind."

Thalia got to her feet.

It took her two tries. The wind was trying to push her over, and her legs didn't want to hold her, and she wouldn't look down. She stood up anyway and nocked an arrow and drew it back to her cheek. The point was aimed straight at the boy's heart.

Her hands were shaking so badly that the arrowhead was drawing little circles in the air.

"Then what are you?" she said.

Behind the boy, in the eastern sky, a light was rising.

It came up out of the skyport on a column of blue-white fire, climbing fast. It was a long silver shape, banking over the basin, and it was not going where it should. It wasn't heading for the mountains. It was coming west, toward the center of the city, toward the Needle. It was climbing and climbing, the roar of its engines rising under the howl of the wind, until it was a scream that shook the grating under their feet.

Thalia didn't look at it. She was looking at the boy.

"Minerva," said the girl against the railing, without opening her eyes. "Minerva, what did you do?"

"Called us a cab."

"That's not a cab. That's a *spaceship*."

"It's a very large cab."

The shuttle came over the edge of the Needle four meters above the railing, filling the whole sky. It was a wall of silver hull and blue running-lights, its engines deafening, the wash of its thrusters hitting the platform like a hurricane. Along its flank, a service gantry ran the length of the hull, with a bright yellow safety railing just above the level of the platform.

Minerva moved.

She had Verrill off the grating and onto her back before Verrill had finished opening her eyes. She took three running steps across the platform, right past Thalia, close enough to touch. She leapt.

Thalia moved too.

She dropped the bow. She didn't draw the arrow and she didn't call the lightning. She simply threw herself across the platform with both hands outstretched, reaching. She was reaching for his coat, his arm, anything at all, the way she had reached for him once in a burning city, fourteen thousand years ago. Her fingertips came within a hand's breadth of the boy's sleeve.

Less than a meter.

"*Sorry, Pinecone Face*," said Minerva.

It was the name. It was only a name, a stupid nickname from a hill on Long Island, from a boy who had never once in his life called her anything else when he wanted to annoy her. Nobody had said it to her in fourteen thousand years. Nobody alive still knew it.

It hit Thalia Grace harder than any blow she had ever taken.

She faltered, only for an instant. It was an instant too long.

The thruster wash of the shuttle took her full in the side. It picked her up off the grating like a leaf and slammed her into the base of the broadcast mast. By the time she had rolled over and dragged herself upright, gasping, with her head ringing, the boy's hand had closed around the yellow railing of the shuttle's service gantry.

He hung there for a moment, one-handed, with a blonde girl clinging to his back, silhouetted against the blue running-lights and the burning copper sky. He looked back at her across the gap.

Then the shuttle climbed away into the dark, and took them with it.

Thalia stood alone at the summit of the tallest building in the Voss System, in the rain, with her bow at her feet.

She watched the running-lights dwindle up into the clouds until they were only one star among many, climbing toward the golden strings of the Dominion lanterns and the Yamamoto Relay beyond. All across New Rome, two and a half kilometers below her, every screen in the city was showing a picture of a very surprised dinosaur.

Thalia's knees gave out. She sat down on the wet steel grating with her back against the mast, pulled her knees up to her chest and put her face in her arms.

She was still sitting there when the rest of the Hunt found her, forty minutes later.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

"Lieutenant," said Celyn at last, very gently, kneeling beside her. "Lieutenant. What do we tell the Director?"

Thalia lifted her head. Her eyes were red. Her face was wet, and not all of it was rain.

"The truth," she said.

The Hunters looked at each other.

"All of it?" asked Naomi.

Thalia looked up at the sky, at the place where the shuttle's lights had disappeared.

"He called me Pinecone Face," she said, very quietly. "Nobody's called me that in fourteen thousand years." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand and got to her feet. "Tell her *all* of it."
