**OVER THE LAURENTIAN HIGHLANDS, QUEBEC**

**T+2 HOURS AFTER DEPARTURE**

---

Five suits of Neodamode heavy armor crossed the Saint Lawrence at dawn, thirty meters above the water, and nobody in Quebec saw them go.

They flew in a loose wedge. Each suit stood close to three meters tall in its plating and was scarred at every joint, and each one carried its squad the way a truck carries lumber, with people strapped and clipped and clinging wherever there was room. Grav-stamp plates in the soles of the suits pushed against the ground far below them, a low pulse every half second that Percy could feel in his teeth. In the Empire the riders called it *hitching*. The Neodamodes called it *freight work*, and they did not mean it kindly.

Centurion Ashmead had the point.

He had a Psilos on his back, a Trophimos on his left shoulder and an Ekdromos keeping station off his right hip under his own power, and he talked the entire time.

"*Freight Lead to all freight.*" His voice came over the squad net like gravel being poured into a brass bucket. "*Spacing is sloppy. Two-Freight, you are drifting. Three-Freight, you are drifting the other way. If the two of you want to hold hands, do it on your own time, and do it after the Wall has your names on it.*"

"*Two-Freight, copy,*" said Quillon. He was nineteen. He sounded nineteen.

"*Three-Freight, copy,*" said Dray, who sounded like a man who had been yelled at by Ashmead for eleven years and planned to be yelled at for eleven more.

The Psilos on Ashmead's back was a long, thin woman named Cerelli. She sat in a sling harness on his shoulder plates with a rifle case longer than she was strapped across her knees, and as far as Percy could tell she had not spoken since they left Long Island. Technically she was still under arrest. Article Three of the Accord did not have a clause for a marksman who had disobeyed her Handler on the Director's own authority, so Corvin had settled the matter by handing her a rifle and telling her not to make him regret it.

The Trophimos on Ashmead's shoulder was Hrafn. He rode with one hand hooked through a tow loop and the other resting on the haft of his axe, and he watched the sky. Twice in the first hour a raven had come up out of the spruce below and flown beside him for a while before peeling away.

Straban flew off the centurion's hip with his arms folded, the wind holding him up the way water holds a swimmer who has stopped trying. Out of all of them, he looked the least impressed by the morning.

The other three heavy squads looked much the same. Quillon carried a Psilos, a sullen, sharp-eyed Apollonian legacy named Doros, and the Aztec-blooded Trophimos whose whip of blue fire had burned the Big House lawn. Her name was Xochitl and she preferred Xochi. Neumond rode Quillon's back plate with his refitted bronze wings folded tight, saving their charge, and complained about it. Dray carried Iane, a Psilos named Lisandra who chewed something resinous without stopping, and an Egyptian-blooded Trophimos named Khay, who slept, upright, in his harness. The fourth heavy was a quiet, slab-sided woman named Vasko. She carried Ione, a Psilos called Ardal, and a Trophimos named Wen Shu, who had cultivator blood three hundred generations back on her mother's side and who had been very quiet since the briefing.

The fifth squad was Merek's.

He had been offered a Neodamode suit at the camp and had looked at it the way a man looks at a borrowed shoe. Then he had gone and fetched his own, which Beckendorf and the Hephaestus cabin had spent two days and most of a third pulling back into shape after Eleni caved it in. It still had a dent in the chest plate the size of a dinner plate. Merek said it gave him character.

He carried the three camp counselors.

Annabeth rode his back plate, harnessed in with a tablet strapped to her forearm and her Yankees cap jammed down over a wool hat. Beckendorf rode the left shoulder because he had asked to, so he could watch the servos work. Katie Gardner rode the right shoulder because nobody else would, and she had both arms wrapped around the shoulder rail and her eyes squeezed half shut against the wind.

And off the formation's left flank, some way out from everybody and a little above them, Percy Jackson was floating.

He hadn't really decided to do it that way. When the suits had lifted off the beach in a spray of sand, Ashmead had offered him a tow loop, very formally, the way you'd offer a seat on a bus to a bishop. Percy had looked at the loop. Then he had reached for the water in the air instead. There was a lot of it over the Sound in the morning, hanging in the haze. It came to him the way it always came. And when the suits went up, he went up with them, sitting on nothing, with his hands in his jacket pockets and his knees drawn up a little, like a kid on a dock.

He had stayed that way for two hours.

Every few seconds, somebody in the formation looked at him.

He didn't notice. That was the thing they would talk about later, in mess halls and on the long nights at the Salient, when they told the story. They would say the Lord Imperator had crossed half a continent sitting on the morning fog, and that he had done it with his eyes half closed, and that he hadn't looked at any of them once.

He was thinking about a dock.

---

She had not been glowing.

That was what he kept coming back to. Every other time he had seen her, she had carried her light with her, even when she was trying to be small. It was in her hair and her skin and the edges of her, a moon that did not know how to turn itself down. Last night on the dock she had been tired, and the light had gone out of her, and she had sat down next to him close enough that their shoulders touched. He'd taken her hand. She had let him.

That was all. They had sat there and listened to the lake.

He could still feel the exact temperature of her fingers. Cool, a little colder than his. Calluses at the tips from the bowstring. She had held on harder than he expected when Minerva spoke up about Alaska, and then she had let go and stood up and become the Director again, all in about a second, and he had liked watching her do that more than he could explain.

*Varlet.*

"Mm."

*Your heart rate has been elevated for one hour and fifty-one minutes. You are not exerting yourself. Your altitude control is excellent. Your core temperature is stable. I have ruled out every medical cause.*

"Okay."

*I am telling you this so you know that I know.*

"Okay," Percy said again, and kept looking at nothing.

Minerva made a noise in the back of his skull that he had come to recognize as the sound of an artificial intelligence choosing not to say something.

Below him, the spruce went by in a dark, endless carpet, broken by lakes that were still mostly ice. The sun came up behind them and laid their shadows out on the forest, five big ones and one small one, racing northwest.

---

"*He's doing it again,*" Quillon said on the squad net.

"*Doing what?*" said Doros, from his back.

"*Not holding onto anything.*"

"*He's been not holding onto anything since Long Island.*"

"*I know. I keep checking.*" A pause. Quillon's helmet turned a few degrees left, toward the small figure hanging in the air off the flank, and then snapped back to the front. "*My grandmother had a mural of him. Over the stove. The Tidefather on the Wall of Obscurus with the ice behind him. She used to touch it before she cooked.*"

"*Everybody's grandmother had that mural.*"

"*Yeah, but he's right there.*"

"*Eyes front, Two-Freight,*" said Ashmead, who had been looking too.

On Dray's back, Lisandra worked her jaw slowly around whatever she was chewing and said, to no one in particular, "He's younger than my little brother."

Nobody answered her.

Ahead of the wedge and a little below it, Straban rolled lazily onto his back in the wind with his arms still folded and looked up at Percy for a while. He had been shot down by this boy once already, in the place they were going back to. His lungs still ached on cold mornings because of it. He studied Percy's face for a long time. Then he rolled back over.

"*He's not even awake,*" Straban said. "*He's daydreaming.*"

"*About what?*" said Neumond.

There was a short silence on the net. All of them knew. Every one of them had been in the Big House hallway when the Director came down the stairs. Every one of them had seen her face when she looked at him.

"*Not our business, buddy,*" said Merek.

---

On Ashmead's shoulder, Hrafn had stopped watching the sky. He was watching Percy instead.

"*I hath long dreamt of serving in a holy crusade,*" the big Trophimos said, on the squad net, to no one in particular. His voice was low and slow, the way it always was. "*Since I was a boy in the foundling hall. I dreamt of marching behind the Tidefather himself. Now the hour lieth at hand, and I findeth it somehow…*" He searched for the word. "*Lacking.*"

Ashmead grunted. "*It's a tow job, Hrafn. Tow jobs are always lacking.*"

"*Nay. 'Tis not the task. 'Tis me.*"

For a while nobody answered him. The grav-stamps pulsed. The spruce went by.

Then Cerelli spoke. It was the first thing she had said since Long Island, and her voice was so quiet that the net almost didn't carry it.

"*Yours is a soul ever yearning for fulfillment,*" she said. "*But purpose can't come from without. Only within.*"

Hrafn turned his head to look at her, slung across the centurion's back with the rifle case on her knees.

"*Not so,*" he said, very gently, as if correcting a child. "*Purpose cometh from the Lord alone.*" His eyes went back across the empty air to the boy sitting on the fog. "*From him.*"

Cerelli was quiet for a moment.

"*By our service we reflect the Lord Imperator's purpose,*" she said. "*But only we can choose to embrace it.*"

Hrafn sat with that for a long time. A raven came up out of the trees below and flew beside him for a while, and he watched it without seeing it.

"*Yes,*" he said at last. "*Yes… of course.*" Something in his broad, bearded face eased. "*I perceiveth thy meaning now. If dimly.*"

"*Dimly's fine,*" said Ashmead. "*Dimly's how the rest of us do it. Spacing, Two-Freight.*"

**OVER HUDSON BAY**

**T+4 HOURS**

---

The shore fell away under them, and then there was nothing but ice.

It went to the edge of the world in every direction, white and broken, pressure ridges like stitched scars running through it in long crooked lines. Leads of black water opened here and there between the floes and steamed in the cold. Katie Gardner opened her eyes, looked down, and closed them again.

"There's nothing," she said into her mic. "Not even lichen. There's nothing growing down there at all."

"It's sea ice," Annabeth said. "Nothing grows on sea ice."

"I *know* that. It just feels weird. I've been feeling plants under me since Long Island. Spruce, birch, moss, lichen, all the way up. It's like somebody keeps talking to me under the floor, and then they just stopped." Katie's arms tightened on the shoulder rail. "How are you guys even doing this? Doesn't the cold get into the suits?"

"Everything gets into the suit," Merek said. "My baby's got more gizmos than some ships."

On the other shoulder, Beckendorf had his gloved hand pressed flat on a seam in Merek's pauldron, feeling the actuators move beneath the plating. He had been doing it for four hours. "These aren't hydraulic," he said, not for the first time.

"Nope."

"They aren't electric either. I took the forearm apart on Tuesday. There's no motor in there. There's just this lattice, like a woven bronze, and when the current goes through it, it contracts like muscle tissue."

"It's grown," Merek said. "They grow it in vats."

"From what?"

Merek thought about it. "From money, mostly."

Beckendorf laughed out loud. It was the first time anyone had heard him laugh since the pyre.

Annabeth had not been listening. For the last ten minutes she had been looking at a small hexagonal port in the plating between Merek's shoulder blades, half hidden under a flap of armored mesh, about a hand's width from her knee. It had a bronze collar and six gold contacts arranged in a ring. She had looked at it for long enough to decide that it was almost certainly a maintenance interface. Then she had looked at the cable that came with her tablet, which Beckendorf had machined for her on Tuesday night out of a scavenged Imperial data lead and a USB-C plug, and which had a six-pin bronze collar on the far end.

It fit perfectly. She had suspected it would.

She pushed it in.

Merek made a noise.

It was a very small, very high noise, like a kettle that had just been surprised. It did not sound like it could have come out of an eight-foot Roman legacy in gold power armor. Every head on the squad net turned at once.

Then the suit dropped out from under them.

For one long, horrible second, the grav-stamps simply stopped. The pulse in Percy's teeth from across the formation stuttered and died, and Merek's suit fell like a dropped anvil, with three screaming demigods attached to it. Katie's scream went up an octave. Beckendorf grabbed the pauldron rail with both hands. The armor's outline shimmered and went *visible*, all at once, gold and bronze and dented against the white sky, flickering in and out like a bad television signal. A thruster on the left hip coughed out a jet of blue fire, then another one on the right, out of rhythm, and the suit began to tumble.

"*Merek!*" Ashmead roared on the net. "*Five-Freight, report!*"

Merek did not report. Merek was busy.

His arms snapped out wide. His back arched. Somewhere inside the plating, something whined up through three octaves and caught, and the grav-stamps hit the ice below with a single enormous *thud* that Percy felt from fifty meters away. The tumble stopped. The suit lurched, steadied, sputtered once more and then climbed back up into the wedge on a ragged blue jet of thrust. The shimmer closed back over the armor like water over a stone, and Merek was gone again from every sky but theirs.

The whole thing had taken perhaps four seconds.

There was a silence on the squad net that lasted considerably longer.

"Lady Chase," Merek said at last. His voice was very calm and very faint, and it sounded the way a man sounds after he has been punched in the stomach and is trying hard not to show it. "High-Lord. Grand Architect. With the greatest possible respect."

"Yes," said Annabeth, in a small voice. She was holding the tablet very still.

"Please warn me." He breathed out. "Next time. That port goes into my spine. The suit is wired into my nervous system. When you open a line into the suit, you open a line into me. It felt like you stuck a fork in my brain stem and then asked it a question."

"I'm sorry," Annabeth said. She meant it. Her face had gone red under the wool hat, all the way to the ears. "I'm really, really sorry. I thought it was a diagnostic port."

"It is a diagnostic port."

"I thought it was a *passive* diagnostic port."

"It's the *only* diagnostic port." Merek's helmet tipped forward a fraction, as if he was looking down at the ice and thinking about how close it had come. "It's fine. I've had worse. Somebody stuck a QVARTUS round through my knee once. That was worse." A pause. "Not by a lot."

"*Five-Freight,*" said Ashmead, with enormous patience, "*are you or are you not still in my formation?*"

"*Five-Freight is in formation,*" Merek said. "*Minor systems event. The Architect was reading my mail.*"

On the right shoulder, Katie Gardner had both arms locked around the rail and her eyes squeezed shut. "I hate all of you," she said.

"Yeah," said Beckendorf, who had not let go either. "That's fair."

Annabeth looked down at the tablet.

It was full.

The cable had not crashed. It had connected, and in the four seconds it took Merek to wrestle his suit back into the sky, the suit had very helpfully tried to tell her everything about itself. Pages of it were still scrolling up the screen, in a dense Imperial Greek that her dyslexia should have scrambled into soup and somehow didn't. Diagnostic trees. Power curves. Thermal maps. A wireframe of the suit with every subsystem lit up in a different color.

She read it once quickly, and then a second time slowly, and by the end of the second time she had forgotten to be embarrassed.

The first thing she found was the veil.

It was not a cloak in any sense she understood. There was no camouflage layer, nothing that bent light. Instead the suit was doing something to its own emissions, every one of them, constantly, thousands of times a second. It was swallowing its own heat and pushing it out in places and directions where nothing would be listening. It was holding its radio silence so hard that the suit's internal links ran on something the readout called *tachyonic whisper*. It was shaping its own radar return to look like snow, like air, like nothing. The listing of what it was hiding from ran three screens long. Radar of every band she had heard of and several she hadn't. Infrared. Signals intercept. Electronic intelligence. Acoustic. Seismic. Satellite imaging in visible and multispectral light. And at the bottom, in a separate category, a line that Minerva translated for her in a small pop-up note: *Pre-Expansion detection systems (primitive). Status: fully masked.*

Primitive. Every radar in North America. Every spy satellite. Every listening plane the United States owned. *Primitive.*

And the mask did not stop at the armor. The suit had drawn a bubble around itself and everything touching it, out to about two meters, and inside that bubble the readout listed four bodies. One heavy, three light. Merek. Beckendorf. Katie. Her.

Annabeth looked down at herself, as if she would be able to see it.

The second thing she found was the power.

She had to read the number three times. Then she checked the units. Then she did the conversion in her head, twice, and then on the tablet's calculator, because she did not believe her head. The reactor in Merek's back, the little thing she had been leaning against for four hours, was putting out more power at a cruise than the nuclear plant at Indian Point. A lot more. Most of it was going into the grav-stamps and the veil, and the rest of it was going somewhere the readout labeled *reserve*, which she decided not to think about yet.

And the third thing she found was the reason she was warm.

She had wondered about it since Quebec. It was forty below up here, with the wind of their passage on top of that, and she should have been frozen solid through every layer she owned. Instead, the back plate under her had been pleasantly, mysteriously warm for hours, like a radiator on a winter morning. Now, on the tablet, she watched the suit's heat sinks on the thermal map. They were *moving*. Thin plates of some dark alloy were sliding beneath the outer armor, slowly and continuously, like the gills of a fish. They rearranged themselves in real time to dump the reactor's waste heat into exactly the places where its three passengers were touching it. Under her knees. Along her back. Under Katie's arms on the shoulder rail. Up through the pauldron under Beckendorf's glove.

The suit had been keeping them alive the whole way north, and it hadn't told anyone.

"Merek," she said slowly. "Your suit's been heating us."

"Mm."

"On purpose."

"It's got a passenger protocol," Merek said. "Neodamodes carry wounded sometimes. Salient's cold." A pause. "Also, I asked it to."

Annabeth didn't say anything for a while.

"Beckendorf," she said finally. "Come look at this."

"Not right now," said Beckendorf, who still hadn't let go of the rail. "Maybe never."

Annabeth unplugged the cable, very gently, and this time she said "Unplugging" first. Merek said "Thank you" with real feeling. Then she sat back against the warm plate, opened the maps Minerva had sent to the tablet before dawn, and tried to pay attention to them instead of to the number still sitting in her head. Topographic sheets of the Brooks Range. Old USGS glacial surveys. A thermal plot from a satellite pass the night before, which showed a cluster of very warm bodies sitting on a mountainside where it was forty-six below. And a single overlay, in a style she didn't recognize, that Minerva had marked with a note: *From my archive. Fourteen thousand years old, and eleven years in your future. Use it carefully.*

The overlay was a fortress.

It was laid over the ridge like tracing paper over a drawing. Cooling towers. Processor vault entrances. Geothermal shafts. Tunnels under the tower foundations, a lot of tunnels. A perimeter that ran along the ridgelines for kilometers, with gun positions marked at every saddle in a neat, careful hand.

Her hand. She knew it the second she saw it. The way the numerals sloped. The little hook at the bottom of the sevens that her third-grade teacher had tried and failed to train out of her.

"Merek," she said.

"Mm."

"Who designed the defenses at Alaska Hill?"

The big man in the suit did not answer right away. The grav-stamps pulsed under them. The ice went by.

"It's in the war-college syllabus," Merek said eventually. "First year. The whole site. The towers, the vaults, the tunnels. They make you draw it from memory." His helmet turned very slightly, the way it would if he wanted to look over his shoulder at her and couldn't. "The cadets call it *the Architect's Thesis.*"

Annabeth looked at the sevens for a long time.

"Okay," she said quietly. And she put the tablet down.

---

Out on the flank, over the open ice, Percy smiled.

It was a small smile, and nobody would have seen it if they had not all been watching. There was salt water under the ice. A lot of it. Hudson Bay was a big, cold, slow-breathing piece of the sea, and for the first time since Long Island it came up through the ice to him like a dog coming up the porch steps, and leaned against him.

He let himself drop a few meters lower without thinking about it, closer to the floes, and the fog under him got thicker and steadier. His hands came out of his pockets. He trailed his fingertips in the cold air above a lead of open water, and a ribbon of seawater rose up out of it to meet them, curled once around his wrist like a bracelet and fell back.

*That,* said Minerva, *is going to show up on someone's satellite as a localized weather anomaly.*

"It won't," Percy said. "The suits."

*The suits mask themselves and the people holding onto them. You are not holding onto anything, Varlet. You insisted.*

Percy looked at his hand.

"Huh," he said.

*I have been extending Merek's veil to cover you since we left the beach. It costs me approximately four percent of my available processing.* A pause. *You are welcome.*

"Thanks."

*You may also wish to know that the Watchpoint has updated both convergence estimates.* Her voice lost some of its dryness. *Neither update is good.*

Percy looked up, finally, away from the water.

"Tell me."

*The cultivators did not stop at the dropship, as the Watchpoint predicted. They went past it. They are on the ridge the archive calls Alaska Hill, nine kilometers further on, and they have been there for most of a day. There are many more of them than the first count showed. The Watchpoint's model is changing them from 'raid' to 'settlement.'*

"They're building something."

*They are laying something out. A ring, very large, around the whole hill. I do not know what it is. I know that the Watchpoint flagged their energy pattern as 'Jade Court', which is the Watchpoint's word for anything Sino-pantheonic it cannot sort any better. I do not think these people are the Court. I think they are someone the Court has not forbidden.*

"And the mortals?"

*The Army's company is still grounded at Coldfoot by ice fog. Three of their Chinooks are frozen to the apron.* A pause. *The White House has sent someone else instead. Two hours ago, a flight of tiltrotors left Eielson with a full company of United States Marines aboard. Weapons, drones, anti-armor teams. They will reach the dropship about when we do.*

Percy was quiet.

"Do they know what they're flying into?"

*No one on this planet knows what they are flying into, Varlet. Including us.*

The ice went on and on under them, white to the horizon.

Percy put his hands back in his pockets. After a while, without meaning to, he was thinking about the dock again.

**MV-22B "CRUSH ONE-ONE," 3,000 FEET AGL**

**NORTH OF THE YUKON RIVER, ALASKA**

---

"Listen up, Marines."

Gunnery Sergeant Ray Teague did not need a microphone to make himself heard over two Rolls-Royce turboshafts and a cabin full of cold, irritated infantry. He had a voice that had been developed over nineteen years for exactly this purpose. He stood in the aisle of the Osprey with one gloved hand locked on an overhead strap and his other hand holding a map board, and every helmet in the cabin turned toward him the way sunflowers turn toward the sun.

"BLUF. In ninety-five mikes we put this company down on a snowfield in the Brooks Range at an LZ we have never seen, against personnel we cannot identify, to secure an object nobody will tell us anything about. That's the bottom line. Everything I say after this is decoration."

Somebody near the ramp said, "Oorah," in a tone that had only partly been meant as sarcasm.

"Thank you, Corporal Iwu, for your enthusiasm. You will be first off the ramp." Teague turned a page on the map board. "The Skipper's going to give you the frag order, but I'm going to give you the parts that will keep you alive. Skipper?"

Captain Marcus Hale had been sitting on the troop seat nearest the cockpit with a laptop open on his knees, reading the same four pages over and over again. He closed it and stood up. Hale was not a big man. He was thin, with a farmer's face and deep lines at the corners of his eyes. He had commanded Lima Company for twenty-two months and two deployments, and he had a reputation in the battalion for never raising his voice. His Marines had a theory that he couldn't.

"Gents. Ladies." He looked down the cabin. Forty-one Marines in this bird, forty-three in Crush One-Two off the starboard wing. Rifle squads, a weapons platoon detachment with two Javelin teams, two Carl Gustaf gunners, a section of M240s, an 81 mortar team they had argued very hard to bring. A pair of Marine Raider attachments who had joined them at Eielson, said hello to nobody and were now asleep. A drone team with three small quadcopters in hard cases. A corpsman. Two corpsmen. "Here's what we know. It's short."

He held up one finger.

"Three days ago, something came down in the Brooks Range. Air Force tracked it in from orbit. It decelerated on the way down. Things that fall from space don't decelerate. This one did. It landed on a mountainside south of Atigun Pass, and then it disappeared off every sensor we have."

Two fingers.

"Two days ago, Eielson recorded a heat bloom in the same range. USGS logged a glacier near the site moving four hundred meters in eleven minutes. That's not a typo. I asked."

Three fingers.

"Yesterday, overhead imagery showed people on a ridge nine klicks from the object. Thirty at first. Then sixty. Then a lot more. They are standing in the snow at forty-six below in what the imagery analyst described, and I am quoting, as *pajamas.*" Somebody laughed and then stopped. "Some of them were barefoot. Some of them were standing in places where there's nothing to stand on."

Hale put his hand down.

"The Army was supposed to get there first. The Army is frozen to the ground at Coldfoot. So now it's us."

"Sir," said a lieutenant near the middle. Lieutenant Pruitt, Second Platoon. "What's the S-2 got on the personnel? Country of origin? Comms?"

Hale looked over at a staff sergeant sitting halfway down the cabin with a ruggedized tablet. "Staff Sergeant Bao. Tell them what you told me."

Staff Sergeant Linh Bao was the company's intelligence chief, a slight, precise Marine from Garden Grove who had once pulled a hostile radio net out of a Syrian hillside with nothing but a spectrum analyzer and a bad mood. She cleared her throat.

"Nothing," she said.

The cabin waited.

"No, I mean that's the finding." Bao turned the tablet around as if they could read it from there. "No comms. No RF emissions on the ridge. Not one. No cell, no sat phone, no VHF, no UHF, no burst traffic. No radar. No GPS jammers. No vehicles. No fuel signatures. No ADS-B, no AIS, no transponders. Rivet Joint flew the corridor twice last night and came back empty. Overhead thermal shows the bodies, but they're running *cold*. Colder than a person should be. No footprints leading to the ridge. No tracks. No supply line." She paused. "No OSINT either. Nobody's posted. Nobody's geotagged. There is no chatter in Chinese, Russian or anything else. Whoever they are, they did not come in on anything, and they are not talking to anybody."

"Spooks," somebody muttered.

"If they were spooks, I'd find their spook stuff." Bao shook her head. "Sir, I don't have an emitter. I don't have a single electron. It's like they aren't using electricity at all."

"Copy," said Hale. "So we go old school."

Teague took over again. He had been waiting for this part.

"Old school means eyes, Marines. Your eyes. The Raven goes up first, the quads go up second, and the snipers go to the high ground before anybody else moves. ROE is *weapons tight.* I want PID on every target. That means you don't shoot at something because it looks weird. Everything up there is going to look weird. You shoot when it's hostile and you can see it being hostile. If you are not sure, you call it up the chain, and you wait. If somebody on that ridge is standing on air, you call it up the chain and you wait. Clear?"

"*Clear, Gunny.*"

"If they fire on you." Teague let the cabin go quiet. "If they fire on you, you are United States Marines. You will return fire, you will gain fire superiority, you will maneuver, and you will close with and destroy them. I don't care if they're wearing pajamas. I don't care if they're wearing nothing. You know how to do this. This is the same job it always is, it's just colder."

"*Oorah.*"

That time, it was the whole cabin.

Hale waited for it to die down.

"One more thing," he said. "The tasking for this didn't come through the normal chain. It came straight from the Pentagon with the National Security Advisor's name on it, and then about an hour later a second set of instructions came in from NASA that nobody in the Pentagon seems to have written. I don't know what that means. I'm telling you because I want you to understand that nobody in the rear knows any more than we do." He looked down the length of the cabin. "We're the only people on the planet who'll actually see this thing. So whatever's up there, we're the ones who get to tell the truth about it."

He sat back down.

Teague watched him for a moment. Then he turned back to the cabin.

**ALASKA HILL, THE BROOKS RANGE**

**THE HOUR OF THE SNAKE**

---

The seventy-first formation flag went into the ice at the bottom of the eastern saddle, and Lu Chen, core disciple of the Heaven Sealing Pavilion, wiped sweat from his brow at forty-six degrees below zero and found that he had no strength left even to curse.

The flag was a little thing, as long as a forearm. Its pole was black iron-heart wood from the Pavilion's southern forests and its banner was a strip of yin-patterned silk embroidered with nine hundred and twenty characters in silver thread, folded twice. It had cost three hundred mid-grade spirit stones to refine. There were seventy-two of them. The one in his hand was worth more than the village Lu Chen had been born in.

He had spent the whole morning walking from one point to the next across this terrible mountain, and planting a flag at each one, and watching the light of the great array creep a little further around the hill.

"Senior Brother Lu!" A girl's voice came down from the ridge above him. "Senior Brother Lu, the elder asks how many remain!"

Lu Chen lifted his head.

Up on the slope, standing on her sword a few zhang above the snow, was Yun Qiaoqiao of the Cloud Chasing Sect. She was wrapped in pale blue cloud-silk with a fox-fur collar at her throat and, alone among all of them, had wisely chosen to wear boots. Her cultivation was at the second stage of the Separation and Reunion Realm, the same as his. Her hair was tied up with a jade pin that he happened to know had been a gift from her master, and her face was red with cold.

"One," said Lu Chen. "Tell Grand Elder Mo that this disciple will plant the last flag within the half hour."

"Grand Elder Mo says a quarter hour."

Lu Chen closed his eyes for a moment.

"A quarter hour," he said.

Yun Qiaoqiao laughed, a bright clear sound like a bell, and turned her sword and went back up the mountain.

---

The Seventy-Two Earthly Fiends Yin-Yang Earth Sealing Formation was not a thing the Heaven Sealing Pavilion did lightly.

The Pavilion had a long and storied history in this. For nine hundred years its people had been known across the righteous path for one thing above all others, which was that whatever the Pavilion found, it kept. Ancient caves. Spirit veins. The ruins of fallen sects. Secret realms that opened once a century. Where another sect would send disciples to grab what they could and run, the Pavilion sent formation masters, and the formation masters sealed the land itself. Then the Pavilion studied whatever it had sealed, for decades or centuries, at its leisure, and when it had wrung every secret out of the place it sealed the secrets too.

That was why the Pavilion was rich. It was also why nobody liked it very much.

The great array would close a ring around this mountain fifty li across. When it was complete, the yin and the yang of the earth inside would be turned against each other and locked, so that nothing could come in or go out without passing through the formation's eye. No divine sense would reach in. No escape talisman would work. The heavens themselves, as the formation manuals put it rather grandly, would be unable to peer inside.

Up here, Lu Chen privately suspected, that last part did not matter. The heavens weren't looking.

That was the strangest thing about this place. In all his twenty-six years he had never felt anything like it. On the day they arrived, he had sat down in meditation on the snow to draw in the spirit qi of the mountain, as any cultivator would on strange ground, and he had very nearly vomited. The spirit qi was thin here. It was thinner than in the dead deserts of the far west, thinner than in mortal cities. And under the thinness there was something worse. It was as if the heaven above this land had turned its face away, long ago, and never once looked back.

There was no Dao here. There was no heavenly will. There was only the cold, the earth and whatever lay inside the earth.

And what lay inside the earth was gold.

Lu Chen had felt it on the first night with his divine sense, deep under the hill, vein after vein of it, so pure and so dense that it drank the thin spirit qi of the mountain the way a sponge drinks water. It was not spirit gold. It was ordinary metal, mortal metal, of the kind used to make hairpins and coins, and it had no business behaving that way. But there were tens of thousands of tons of it, and it pulled at the qi with a strange, patient hunger, and when he had sent his divine sense down toward it a second time, the gold had *pulled back.*

He had not tried a third time.

---

Grand Elder Mo Tianhe of the Heaven Sealing Pavilion sat on a cushion of purple cloud at the summit of the hill with his eyes closed and his hands resting on his knees, and he did not appear to be doing anything at all.

He was old in the way that cultivators were old. His hair was white and long and his beard came to his belt, but his face was smooth as a young man's, and his eyes, on the rare occasions when he opened them, were as black as ink stones. He wore the deep purple robes of the Pavilion's inner court, embroidered with a seal script that moved when nobody was looking straight at it. Under him, held in his palm by threads of qi nobody else could see, the formation's central eye turned slowly, a disc of black and white jade as wide as a millstone.

He was at the second stage of the Immortal Ascension Realm. In the entire Pavilion, only four people stood higher.

Next to him, on her own cushion of white cloud, sat Elder Su Lianyue of the Cloud Chasing Sect.

She was a guest here, and both of them were very careful to say so. The Cloud Chasing Sect and the Heaven Sealing Pavilion had a relationship that the elders described as *harmonious* in public, and as other things in private. Su Lianyue was at the first stage of the Immortal Ascension Realm. She looked about thirty, with long black hair loose down her back and a face that was cool and lovely and made younger disciples forget whatever they had been about to say. She had come because the Cloud Chasing Sect had heard what the Pavilion found, and had sent her to make sure that whatever the Pavilion sealed up, a fair share of it would one day be unsealed again.

She was watching the lowest saddle of the eastern slope, where a small figure in Pavilion robes was trudging through waist-deep snow toward the last formation point.

"Your core disciple," she said, "is dawdling."

"My core disciple," said Mo Tianhe without opening his eyes, "has planted seventy-one formation flags since the Hour of the Rat, on a mountain where the heavens give him nothing. At his age I could not have planted forty." A pause. "Do not tell him I said this."

Su Lianyue smiled slightly.

Behind the two elders, on the long flat summit of the hill, the rest of their expedition was waiting.

The Heaven Sealing Pavilion had brought thirty of its own. Eleven core disciples, every one at the Separation and Reunion Realm. Nineteen inner disciples, high in the True Element Realm, at the seventh stage and above. The Cloud Chasing Sect had brought nine more, light and quick and all dressed in sky blue, and they stood a little apart, looking at everything and touching nothing.

And then there were the others.

It was well known in the cultivation world that the Heaven Sealing Pavilion was rich, and that it was miserly, and it was less well known that it would spend spirit stones like river water when it truly wanted something. For this mountain, it had spent. The spirit stones had flowed out of the Pavilion's treasury in sacks, and in return the Pavilion had bought the services of nearly forty cultivators from places no righteous sect would admit to visiting.

They stood in loose clusters across the summit, keeping their distance from the disciples and from each other.

Nearest the elders, as if he wanted them to see him, stood a tall young man in robes the color of drying blood. He wore a wide crimson sash and a pale jade mask that covered the upper half of his face. His name was Xue Wuya, and he came from the Blood Shadow Sect of the southern marshes, which the righteous path had declared a demonic sect and tried to burn out of existence three separate times. His cultivation was at the peak of the Separation and Reunion Realm, half a step from Immortal Ascension, and the snow around his bare feet had melted into a faint red slush.

Some way behind him sat a hunched old woman from Ghost Corpse Valley on a stool made of bones. She was knitting. Three iron corpses stood around her in the snow, eight feet tall and black with refining lacquer, with yellow talismans pasted to their foreheads that fluttered in the wind. Every so often she reached up without looking and patted one of them on the knee.

Not far from her, a rogue cultivator called Old Man Hu lay on his back in the snow with a red gourd of wine on his chest and his eyes closed. His robe was patched in four places. He was snoring. Nobody had seen him do anything but drink since the expedition arrived, and three different mercenaries had already told Lu Chen, quietly, not to stand downwind of him in a fight.

Two swordsmen from the Myriad Sword Sect stood a careful distance apart from all of them, a man and a woman in white with their swords on their backs, looking down their noses at everyone. They were from a righteous sect and wanted it known. They had also taken the Pavilion's spirit stones, which nobody brought up.

A woman from the Joyous Union Sect lounged on a rock wrapped in red gauze that should not have kept a cat warm, her legs crossed and her chin propped on one hand. Her name was Hua Ruyan. She had smiled at Lu Chen on the first day, and he had very nearly walked off the mountain.

A bald, huge man with arms like the trunks of trees stood next to a heap of black iron chains taller than he was. He was a body cultivator who called himself Iron Arm Kang, and he wore nothing above the waist. The snow that touched his skin hissed and turned to steam.

A beast tamer from the northern plains sat cross-legged on the coils of a frost-scaled python as thick as a well, which lay in a great white spiral at the edge of the summit with its head on the snow and its tongue flickering.

A puppet master sat on the ground with seven wooden puppets laid out on a cloth in front of him, oiling their joints one at a time.

And there were others. A monk with no eyebrows, who wore only a saffron robe and counted prayer beads made of finger bones. A pair of sisters from a poison sect, in green, who whispered to each other and laughed at nothing. A thin man in a scholar's grey robe, carrying a closed fan, who had not said a word to anyone and whose name nobody could remember even after he told them.

It was not, Mo Tianhe reflected, a company he would have chosen to sit down to tea with.

It was a company that could kill a small sect in an afternoon.

---

The male swordsman of the Myriad Sword Sect was named Bai Qingfeng, and he had grown bored of waiting.

He had borrowed one of the puppet master's spare figures for the purpose. It was a plain thing of pale wood, man-sized and featureless, and he had stood it up in the snow at the edge of the summit where the inner disciples could see. They had gathered without being told. A swordsman of the Myriad Sword Sect did not draw his blade for nothing, and the younger disciples of both sects had heard of the Sect's sword lectures, even if few of them had ever been permitted to attend one.

Bai Qingfeng stood before the puppet with his hands folded behind his back and his sword still sheathed.

"Humans possess a nature," he said. His voice was calm and carried well in the thin air. "It follows that weapons possess natures too. What is innate in human nature is good and evil. Be it evil, be it good, it is what the Heavens bestowed. From such simple beginnings, we have gradually become ten thousand completely different dispositions." He tilted his head toward the puppet. "And what is innate in a blade is…"

He swung.

Nobody saw him draw. There was only a whisper of white light, and the sword was in his hand.

"To kill. To kill for war. To kill for one's revenge. To kill for resources, for spirit stones, for treasures. And…"

The puppet fell. It fell in two pieces, sheared from the left shoulder to the right hip, so cleanly that the halves slid apart in the snow like two halves of a fruit.

"To kill the scum of the world. Traitors to their sects. Those who defy the Heavens." He did not look at Xue Wuya when he said the next words. He did not have to. "Demonic cultivators."

He sheathed his sword with a soft click.

"As such," said Bai Qingfeng, "the way of the blade has grown into countless styles of combat. Your weapon represents you as you are. And you are a messenger of your weapon."

The inner disciples bowed as one. "Thank you, Senior, for your guidance!"

At the other end of the summit, Xue Wuya of the Blood Shadow Sect had watched the whole lecture with his arms folded. When it was over, he laughed softly behind his jade mask.

"Well spoken, Fellow Daoist Bai," he said. "This one wonders what message the Myriad Sword Sect's blades carry, when they are paid in the Pavilion's spirit stones."

Bai Qingfeng's face did not change. But the puppet master, kneeling over his oiled figures, quietly gathered them up and moved them a little further away.

---

"Grand Elder."

Xue Wuya of the Blood Shadow Sect bowed. It was a perfectly correct bow, the bow of a junior to a senior, cupping his fist in his palm. It had just enough mockery in it that nobody could prove anything.

Mo Tianhe opened one eye.

"Speak."

"This junior has a small question." Xue Wuya straightened. Behind his mask his eyes were bright and amused. "The Pavilion is paying us to guard a mountain. But in three days on this mountain, this junior has seen nothing to guard it from. There are no spirit beasts here. There are no rival sects. There is no one at all, only snow." He spread his hands. "Is the Pavilion perhaps paying us to watch its disciples plant flags?"

There was a small, dangerous silence on the summit.

"Insolent!" One of the Pavilion's core disciples, a hot-blooded young man named Gongsun Jie, stepped forward with a hand on the hilt of his sword. "You dare speak to the Grand Elder in that tone? A demonic cultivator from a sect of marsh rats? Do you know the height of the heavens and the depth of the earth?"

Xue Wuya did not even look at him.

"Ah," he said to the air. "A little dog barks."

Gongsun Jie's face went white, then red. His sword came half out of its sheath with a long, singing hiss.

"Jie'er," said Mo Tianhe mildly.

The sword stopped.

"Sheathe it."

Gongsun Jie's knuckles were white around the hilt. Very slowly, he sheathed the sword. He stepped back into line with the rest of the disciples. His jaw was clenched so hard that Lu Chen, coming up the last slope with the final flag over his shoulder, could hear his teeth grind.

Mo Tianhe looked at Xue Wuya for a long moment with his one open eye.

"You were paid," he said, "because this mountain is not empty."

Xue Wuya tilted his head. "Grand Elder?"

"Three days ago, a vessel fell from the heavens nine li from where you stand. The heavens of this land are dead. Nothing falls from them. And yet the vessel slowed as it fell, as if it had a will. It sits under a concealment that this old man's divine sense cannot penetrate. Not a heavenly concealment. Not a formation. Something else." He closed the eye again. "Two days ago, a glacier on this mountain walked four hundred zhang in the time it takes to drink a cup of tea. You think the snow did that by itself?"

Xue Wuya said nothing.

On her cushion of white cloud, Su Lianyue made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

Xue Wuya was silent for a moment. Then he bowed again. This time, there was no mockery in it at all.

"This junior understands," he said.

"Hmph," he said to nobody. "This old man thought we came for the gold."

The last flag went into the snow on the eastern saddle a few breaths short of the quarter hour.

Around the whole hill, so far away that the farthest points were below the horizon, seventy-two little flags rippled at once in a wind that wasn't blowing. Lines of light leapt between them. The light was too thin to see when you looked straight at it. The edge of it shimmered in two colors against the blue.

The disc of black and white jade in his palm stopped turning. It locked, with a sound like a key in a lock.

"It is done," he said.

"Fellow Daoist Su is too kind."

Su Lianyue's smile did not move at all. "It is a pity the Heaven Sealing Pavilion teaches nothing else."

Somewhere among the disciples, a young Cloud Chasing girl coughed to hide a laugh.

Yun Qiaoqiao felt it first.

She had been standing at the southern edge of the summit with her divine sense spread thin and wide across the valleys, the way her master had taught her, keeping watch. "Elder," she said suddenly. "Elder Su. There is something to the south."

Every head on the summit turned.

Coming from the south, very fast. Three hundred li. Perhaps a little less." She opened her eyes. "There are mortals inside. Many mortals. Eighty, ninety. They are carrying iron. Much iron. Some of it is very strange."

There was a moment of silence. Then several of the inner disciples laughed.

"Mortals," said Gongsun Jie, who had not yet forgiven anyone for anything. "Mortal soldiers. Coming here, in their iron birds." He snorted. "Ants shaking a tree."

"Junior Brother Gongsun should not speak so carelessly," said Lu Chen, who had come up onto the summit with blood still on his chin. "The mortals of this land are not the mortals of our villages. Their iron birds cross the sea. Their weapons can level a hill. This disciple has read the reports."

"Then Senior Brother Lu should read fewer reports and cultivate more." Gongsun Jie turned to the Grand Elder and cupped his fists. "Grand Elder, this disciple asks permission to go and meet the iron birds. They need not come within the array at all."

Mo Tianhe considered him for a long moment.

"No," he said.

"Grand Elder, this disciple only—"

"The array is complete. They cannot see it. They cannot enter it. If they try to enter it, it will turn them aside, and they will think they have lost their way in the snow. If they persist," and here the Grand Elder's voice grew very slightly colder, "the Blood Shadow Sect will earn its spirit stones. Until then, you will stay on this hill. Do you understand?"

Gongsun Jie's face was a mask. "This disciple understands."

Xue Wuya, standing at the edge of the summit with his back to all of them, smiled behind his jade mask and said nothing.

---

It was Wen Shu, in the end, who would hear the rest of this story, years later and very far away, from a white-haired woman who had once been the young Cloud Chasing disciple at the southern edge of that summit. It was a story Yun Qiaoqiao told only when she was very drunk. And it always ended the same way.

She had been holding her divine sense out over the southern valleys, toward the iron birds.

And while she was doing it, something went past.

It did not go past the way the iron birds did. The iron birds were loud. They were hot. Her divine sense could feel the heat of them, the shaking of their wheels and the spark-sense of the iron inside them, from three hundred li away. This was different. This came from the southeast, from the direction of the frozen sea, very low and very fast. And when her divine sense touched the place where it was, her divine sense found nothing.

It was not that it found something hiding. Hiding was a thing she knew. A concealment technique felt like a closed door. A formation felt like a wall. This was neither. When her divine sense passed over it, there was simply less of the world there than there should have been. As if a hole in the shape of nothing were moving across the land. As if a stone were sinking through still water without making a single ripple.

Five such holes. And a sixth, smaller one, a little apart from the others.

And that sixth one, for the briefest instant, as her divine sense brushed it, *noticed her.*

It was like putting her hand into the sea. Not a pond, not a lake. The sea. Something enormous, cold and deep that went down further than she could reach, and that, for the space of a single breath, turned over in its sleep and looked up at the little hand that had touched it.

Then it turned away again, uninterested. As if it had been thinking about something else.

Yun Qiaoqiao opened her eyes. Her face had gone very pale.

"Elder," she said, and her voice came out much smaller than she meant it to. "Elder Su. Grand Elder Mo. There is something else."

Su Lianyue was already on her feet.

"Where?"

"Southeast. Coming toward the vessel. I cannot see it." Qiaoqiao swallowed. "I cannot see it at all. There is nothing there. Only, there are six nothings, and they are moving."

On his cushion of purple cloud, Grand Elder Mo Tianhe of the Heaven Sealing Pavilion was silent for a long moment.

"Nothing does not have a shape," he said at last. "Only something hides."

He lifted the black and white disc in his palm. Across the summit, without a single word of command, thirty disciples of the Heaven Sealing Pavilion and nine of the Cloud Chasing Sect stepped into their formation positions at once. The iron corpses of Ghost Corpse Valley lifted their heads. The frost python uncoiled. Iron Arm Kang picked up his chains. Old Man Hu sat up in the snow and drank a long swallow from his gourd, and his eyes when he lowered it were not the eyes of a drunk.

Xue Wuya of the Blood Shadow Sect turned around, and took off his mask, and smiled.

"Now," he said softly, "the Pavilion is getting its money's worth."

They did not fire volleys. They took turns, each marksman picking out a target through her own gap in the wall and working the bolt between shots with a smooth, unhurried rhythm. It was the rhythm Cerelli used on the range on Mars, the one she had taught every Psilos under her command. Every shot was a man or a woman in a robe going down in the snow. The sword qi came in faster, desperate now, and the heavies leaned into it. The rounds kept going out through the gaps, one at a time.

Lisandra laughed. It was a short, ugly sound around whatever she was chewing.

"*Duck hunting,*" she said.

The inner disciples broke first. They had never in their lives been on the wrong end of something they could not see coming, and they did not understand what was killing them. They turned on their swords and fled back up the saddle. The core disciples behind them lasted a few seconds longer, out of pride. The poison sister in green lasted until Cerelli shot her through the throat.

And then the saddle was empty except for the bodies.

Ashmead stood up out of the shield line, slowly, with smoke rising off his shoulder plates. The hole held around him. He looked back over his shoulder, toward the trench, toward the Ekdromoi and the Trophimoi waiting along the parapet with their batteries in their hands and their faces lit by the boiling light. His helmet tipped, very slightly.

"*Right, then,*" said the centurion. "*Time to play the piper.*"

The SP forces went in.

They did not go in to fight a battle. They went in, as Ashmead put it on the net, *to ruin the old man's afternoon.* Each of the Ekdromoi and Trophimoi had their own way of doing it, and they had been doing it, against things worse than cultivators, for longer than most of them had been alive.

Straban went first. He went through the gap between Ashmead and Quillon on the wind like a thrown knife, and was over the saddle and gone in a heartbeat. Thirty seconds later, a blizzard rose up out of nowhere over the northern slope inside the formation, a howling, horizontal, white-out storm that buried four of the Pavilion's nodes in two meters of snow and drove the inner disciples guarding them to their knees. The cultivators could have stopped it easily if they had known where the wind was coming from. They didn't. Straban was lying on his back on a drift a kilometer away inside the formation, with his arms folded, making weather.

The twins went through together, and by the time the hole closed behind them, Iane had already drawn three runes on the inside of the wall and Ione had drawn three more. Each rune was a tiny gate. Each gate opened into one of the others. And for the next ten minutes, every cultivator who went looking for them found a pair of Hecate legacies who stepped through one glowing doorway and came out of another one two hundred meters away, firing QVARTUS rounds into their backs.

Neumond did not go through at all. He did not need to. He sat on the parapet of the trench with his wings spread and his visor down and talked to the formation. Every eleven seconds, as the cycle came round, he whispered into it with the deception emitters. He sent false nodes. He sent echoes. He sent, at one point, a perfect copy of the Grand Elder's own qi signature, recorded off the eye on a previous cycle and played back on a node at the far side of the ring, so that for one confusing breath the formation thought it had two masters.

"*What was that?*" Ashmead asked him.

"*Spoof.*" Neumond's grin was audible again. "*Replay attack. The oldest trick there is. They taught us it in basic.*"

"*Who invented that?*"

"*They did,*" said Neumond, and pointed his chin at the black wall. "*Fourteen thousand years ago. On this mountain. Against the guy who taught it to us.*"

Hrafn went through on the third cycle with a raven on his shoulder and his runic axe alight. He found a node on the eastern saddle, and the six Pavilion disciples guarding it, and he sang to them. It was a low, slow, rolling song in a language older than Norse. The six disciples sat down in the snow one after another and fell into a deep, dreamless sleep. Then Hrafn tore their flag up out of the ice with his bare hands, stuck it under his arm and walked back out through the next hole with it, humming.

Xochi went through on the fourth. Nobody was ever quite sure what she did in there. But the whole western slope of the formation lit up blue for a while, and some of the screaming lasted a long time.

And all along the line, every eleven seconds, Cerelli and her Psiloi sent a round through the hole.

They didn't aim at anyone in particular. They had to aim at something four kilometers away through a four-second doorway that opened and closed on a clock. So they fired at the summit, at the eye, at the purple cushion and the old man sitting on it. Most of the rounds went wide. Some didn't. None of them killed him. But every single one of them made him *move*.

---

On the summit, Grand Elder Mo Tianhe of the Heaven Sealing Pavilion had been sitting very still for a quarter of an hour. His face was calm. His beard was blowing in the wind of the storm that should not have been there.

There was a thin line of blood running down his left cheek where a Psiloi round had come within a hand's width of his eye before he turned it aside with a flick of his sleeve. The sleeve was gone. So was a good part of his outer robe. Under it, his skin was smoking where the strange metal in the rounds had bitten through his protective qi.

The formation's eye trembled in his palm. It had trembled every eleven seconds for a quarter of an hour. Every time it trembled, something came through.

"Fellow Daoist Mo," said Elder Su Lianyue, very quietly, beside him. She had stopped smiling a long time ago. "How many nodes have we lost?"

"Four buried. Two seized. One taken." He did not open his eyes. "Someone is singing to my formation, Elder Su. Someone is singing to it in its own voice, and it is listening to them instead of to me."

"Who?"

"I do not know." The Grand Elder's jaw tightened, very slightly. "In nine hundred years, no one has ever done this to a formation of the Heaven Sealing Pavilion. No one has ever even *tried*. Our formations are a closed fist. They have opened my fist like a child's hand. Every eleven breaths." He opened his eyes, finally, and looked south, toward the low ridge, toward the black wall and whatever was waiting on the other side of it. "They are not cultivators. They are not gods. They are not mortals. And they know this mountain better than I do."

Lu Chen, kneeling a few paces away in the snow with blood drying on his chin, looked up.

"Grand Elder," he said quietly. "This disciple has a thought."

"Speak."

"This disciple thinks they have fought us before." Lu Chen swallowed. "Many times. And won."

Nobody on the summit answered him.

Far off, through the storm, on the far side of the black wall, the sky cracked again.

**THE WRECK OF CRUSH ONE-ONE**

---

Gunnery Sergeant Ray Teague had seen a lot of things in nineteen years. He had never, until today, watched an enemy line break and run up a mountain without a single Marine having hurt it.

He had watched it from behind the burning tail of his own aircraft, with a dead Marine at his feet and a live one in his arms, and he had watched what came after. The things that came down out of the sky to the south, five of them, huge and gold and grey and moving like tanks with wings. The people riding them. The ditch that dug itself in four seconds. The domes that came up out of the ground. The wall of blue ice. The black wall, beyond it, that went up into the sky forever.

And now one of the huge gold things was walking across the snowfield toward him, and there was a boy walking next to it.

The boy was maybe sixteen. He had black hair and a jacket nowhere near warm enough for the Brooks Range, and frozen blood under his nose, and his eyes were a strange sea-green color. He walked across the snow like it was holding him up. Every step, the snow on either side of him rose a little, in long low ridges, like bow waves. They went ahead of him toward the wreck, and when they reached it they rose up all at once into a curving wall of packed snow two meters high. It ran right around Crush One-One and all the Marines and wounded sheltering behind it, a wall between them and the mountain.

He stopped in front of Teague. He looked down at the dead Marine, and at the one Teague was holding, who was a nineteen-year-old lance corporal named Ruiz with a bad wound in his leg and his face grey with shock.

"Hi," the boy said quietly. "I'm sorry. We were too far out. We saw your helicopter go down and we came as fast as we could."

Teague looked at him for a long moment.

He looked at the thing in gold armor standing beside him. It was eight feet tall, with a dent in its chest plate the size of a dinner plate and red lidar light in its eyes. Clinging to its shoulders and back were three teenagers in winter jackets. One of them, a blond girl in a Yankees cap, was already climbing down and pulling a first-aid kit out of her coat.

He looked back at the boy.

"Who," said Gunnery Sergeant Ray Teague, "the fuck are you people."

The boy seemed to think about it.

"The cavalry," he said. "Probably." He knelt down in the snow beside Ruiz and put a hand on the wounded leg. Water came out of the snow around it, a lot of it, and wrapped around the wound, and the bleeding slowed. "Who's in charge?"

"*Skipper's over there,*" Teague said. He nodded toward the nose of the aircraft, where Captain Hale was sitting against the fuselage with a corpsman working on his scalp and the radio handset still in his hand. Hale was looking at them. He had been looking at them for some time. "Captain Hale. Lima Company."

"Okay." The boy stood up. "Can you take me to him?"

Teague looked at the boy. He looked at the gold giant behind him, who had gone down on one knee in the snow with a crunch of servos so that the girl in the cap could get to the next wounded Marine. He looked south, at the trench line, at the domes and the ice wall and the strange soldiers moving along it with their impossible rifles. He looked north, at the black wall, behind which an entire army in robes was hiding from those soldiers.

Then he looked at the four steaming cubes that had been the lead fire team of Second Platoon, which the old woman's iron corpses had dropped in the snow when they ran.

"Yeah," he said, and got to his feet. "Yeah, I can take you to him."

Behind him, on the low ridge, the Psiloi fired through the hole again, right on the eleven-second mark.
