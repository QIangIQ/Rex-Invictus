## Chapter 17: Gallows Rise

*"There is no after. There is the Flats, and then there is the next segment of the Flats."*

*Optio Hale Tarrant,* 6th *Cohort,* 44th *Trench Regiment*

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 160**

The plans of the inner belt were in the Third's archive, and Leda Corvis had read them on the hundred and sixtieth cycle of Rotation Eight, the day after the second belt fell, and had then gone to her quarters and sat on the edge of her bunk for a long time without taking her boots off.

The Third had dug the inner belt itself. That was the first thing she had not known, and the worst. Six hundred years ago, when the Arsenal was new and the Third's own anchor against the Phlegethon at Depth 40, the Third's engineers had dug a ring of works around the spur to protect it, and every Archflamen and every Steward since had added to it as the vaults went deeper and the Arsenal grew. It ran sixty-one kilometres around the spur. It was eight kilometres deep, thicker than the outer belt and the second belt together. After the Arsenal itself, it was the oldest structure on the Flats. Its plans filled eleven cases in the archive, and the Third's engineers, she had learned from the archivist, regarded them as the most discouraging documents the Third held.

She understood why. Trench lines. Bunkers. Pillboxes. Gun pits sunk into the basalt and roofed with it. Redoubts with their supporting works, every one of them sited so that every approach to it was a killing ground swept from three directions. Galleries and shelters beneath it all, too deep for the Phaethons to reach. Wire and minefields in front. And standing in their silos across the belt and the spur, sixty ceiling-lances, built to keep the deep's flying things out of the ceiling geometry, which the Arsenal's gunners had discovered in the first rotation of the siege could also be fired flat, across the Flats, crossing a regiment's front from end to end in a second.

The Phaethons could reach the lance silos now, from the old second belt. They couldn't break them. The silos had been built to withstand anything that came down out of the ceiling, and the Third had nothing on the ground as heavy.

She went back to her office in the morning and wrote the Lord-General a summary of it on a single slate, as she always did, and he read it and signed it and gave it back to her without a word.

The staff on Quarterdeck Nineteen still believed, on the hundred and sixtieth cycle, that the inner belt was the worst of it. That once the Third was through the belt, the Cinder Wall and the Acropolis would fall quickly. They were fixed targets that the Phaethons could grind down in time, and the inner belt was ten thousand targets that had to be taken one at a time. Corvis had believed it too. She would remember that afterward, and remember the date, and remember that she had believed it for a hundred and eighteen cycles, before the drowned came down.

---

The Command's original allowance had been four rotations. It had been extended twice. By Rotation Eight the Third had been given twelve rotations in all to retake the Arsenal, and it had four left. Nobody on the staff believed the timetable could be kept. Nobody knew what would happen when it ran out. Most of them assumed it would mean failure: that the Directorate would reduce the Third's priority, and the Command would take regiments away for other fronts, and the Third would be left in front of the Arsenal in a war it couldn't finish.

If the Archflamen held for four more rotations, he would have won, whether he understood it or not.

The Phalanx-Generals told Castorre that the Big Push had worn their regiments to nothing. The losses had been staggering. The vats at Watchpoint Hades would need weeks to clear them. An attack on the inner belt now would be futile and would cost transfers for no gain. The regiments needed time to refill and rebuild, and until then they would harass the inner belt with guns and raids and limited attacks, and the general offensive would wait.

Castorre agreed. Corvis watched him agree, and knew that he knew what everyone on the Flats knew: that the same pause gave the Archflamen time to rebuild his own strength.

He didn't replace any of the Phalanx-Generals. The Big Push had been won by a colonel the Eighth Line Phalanx had overruled, and everyone in the Command knew it, and there were officers on Quarterdeck Nineteen who said in Corvis's hearing that the Third's generals were Athenians of the Academy on Callistus, chosen for their method and their care with supplies, and that none of them could break a belt without somebody else's idea. Callix of the Second, whose probe had taken K-19, was the only one who had won a battle the Répertoire hadn't planned for him, and the staff disliked him for it.

"Do you want to replace them, sir?" Corvis asked him once, carefully, late in a dark watch.

"No," Castorre said. "They're competent, Leda. They know their business. And I've got four rotations left, and a new commander couldn't learn the Flats in less." He looked at his slate. "I'd rather have a general who knows where the shell dumps are than a genius who has to ask."

So the Third advanced by the slow method. It dug.

Its regiments came up to the inner belt and dug in facing it, and the ground between the old second belt and the inner belt filled with saps and dugouts and communication trenches running back to the second belt, so the Third's columns needn't use the old dock roads, which the Archflamen's gunners had ranged so precisely that a column on a road was destroyed before it heard the shells. Harassing fire fell on the working parties every watch. The work went on under it.

And on the inner belt, the Frost Masons of the Cocytus began to build.

Corvis saw it in the spotters' reports first, a few cycles after the *Rimebound* had lowered them onto the spur. A bunker on Leaf 54-46 that the Phaethons had broken open on the hundred and eightieth cycle was whole again on the hundred and eighty-third. Its roof was white. Then another, and another. The spotters logged them as they appeared, one after another, all along the inner belt, the broken works rebuilt and faced in something white that glittered faintly in the red light of the ash cloud. Ice, the spotters said. Cocytus ice. It set harder than the Arsenal's concrete and didn't melt in the heat of the Flats, and a bunker roofed with it shed a Phaethon shell as easily as a parapet shed rain.

By the end of Rotation Eight the spotters had logged more than four hundred works on the inner belt faced in white. The Tenth Ordnance Phalanx's fire on them produced no effect its observers could measure.

**LEAF 55-47, THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE *52ND*. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 270**

Centurion Ostap Lerche had been on the Flats since Rotation Three, in three regiments, and had been killed eleven times, and the thing he had learned in all that time that he considered most useful was never to look at the wire on a ridge before you had to go up it.

He looked at it anyway, on the two hundred and seventieth cycle, from the forward bay of the *52nd*'s *8th* Cohort on Leaf 55-47, through the colonel's own field-glass. She had handed it to him and told him to.

The ridge was two kilometres away across a shallow valley in the ash. It was low and bare and grey, the first of a series of ridges that ran south-east from Leaf 55-47 toward the spur, and along its crest the Archflamen's wire stood in a long black line against the red glow of the ash cloud. There were things hanging on the wire. He counted forty before he stopped. Some of them were soldiers of the Third, from the raids of the last hundred cycles, and some of them were older, and some of them he couldn't tell. They swung a little in the hot wind that came across the Flats from the river.

"Gallows Rise," the colonel said beside him. "That's what the regiment's calling it."

"I can see why, Colonel."

He lowered the glass and gave it back to her. Colonel Lowe took it without looking at him and went on looking at the ridge.

He liked her. He hadn't expected to. He had come to the *52nd* at the start of Rotation Eight from the *37th*, transferred by order to fill a gap, and he had expected a young colonel in her first battle to be one of two things: frightened or stupid. She was neither. She was quiet and careful and she listened, and she had broken the second belt with a night crawl that nobody in the Third had thought of in six hundred years, and she had come back down the Stair from the vats eight cycles after a Laistrygon killed her and walked the regiment's whole front on her first night back. The soldiers of the *52nd* would have followed her into the river. Lerche, who had been on the Flats long enough to stop following anyone anywhere if he could help it, found that he would have too.

"Those guns on the ridge can see the whole of our forward line," she said. "They've been dropping shells into our bays every watch since we got here. We've lost two hundred and six since the hundred and sixtieth cycle to plunging fire alone." She lowered the glass. "I can pull the forward cohorts back out of range and leave a wide no-man's land that somebody will have to cross again later. Or we can take the ridge."

"The Phalanx wants you to take it."

"The Phalanx wants me to take it," she agreed. "And it's right, for once. There are five more ridges after this one between here and the spur, and every one of them gives the Archflamen the best observation for kilometres. We'll have to take them all. We may as well start."

Lerche looked at the ridge again, without the glass. At the long black line of the wire on the crest, and the things hanging on it.

"We're at half strength, Colonel," he said.

"I know."

"The vats are sending us back a few hundred at a time. The guns are worn out. Half the Phaethons in the Tenth Ordnance have cracked barrels after the Push, and the rest are throwing wide."

"I know."

"Then how?"

She looked at him then. Her grey eyes were steady.

"Small," she said. "We don't try to go deep. We don't try to break into the inner belt. We take the ridge, and only the ridge, and throw them off the high ground above us. The regiments on either side make diversions to keep the Archflamen from moving reserves along the belt to meet us. Five cohorts in the first wave on a front of four kilometres, two in reserve, two more in a supporting attack on the left. None of them at full strength. Three hundred and sixty guns, most of them in a box barrage round the ridge to keep his reinforcements out. Then smoke. Then up the slope behind the smoke, with the heavy weapons in the forward bays firing high-angle over our heads onto his heavy weapons on the reverse slope." She paused. "Small, and fast, and in one cycle."

"And the *8th*?"

She turned back to the ridge and pointed, down at the left-hand end of it, where the crest turned and a knot of works stood up out of it, grey and squat, ringed with wire.

"Bastion G-70," she said. "Pillboxes, bunkers, gun pits. It's the hardest thing on the ridge. The Phalanx has released two Colossi from its reserve for it, and you'll have the regiment's four serviceable Thureos." She looked at him. "I'm giving it to you, Lerche. You've been on the Flats since Rotation Three, you've been killed eleven times and you're still here, and the *8th* will follow you up that slope."

Lerche looked at the bastion for a long time.

"Yes, Colonel," he said.

**THE GUN LINES OF THE TENTH ORDNANCE PHALANX, EIGHTY KILOMETRES BEHIND THE FRONT. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 277**

Nessa Carrow put her hand flat on the barrel of Gun Four and felt the crack.

It was there. She'd known it would be. It was a hairline, invisible unless you knew where to look, running a hand's breadth along the underside of the barrel a metre from the muzzle, and she had found it on the hundred and sixty-fifth cycle, three cycles after Gun Four fired the first shell of the war onto the Acropolis. She had reported it. The Ordnance Regiment's artificers had come and looked at it and said that the barrel was good for another two hundred rounds, perhaps three, and that there were no replacement barrels on the Flats and none coming down the cables, and that she was to keep firing until it failed.

Gun Four had fired a hundred and ninety rounds since.

"How is she?" said Aster Lenn behind her.

"She's fine."

"Nessa."

Carrow took her hand off the barrel and turned round. Lenn was standing at the edge of the pit with his keying kit under his arm, a tall thin young man of twenty-six with the gold hair and the gold eyes of his father, and he was looking at the barrel with the patient, worried expression he wore whenever he looked at anything she cared about.

"She'll last the night," Carrow said. "She'll last tomorrow. After that I don't know."

Lenn came down into the pit and put his own hand on the barrel, beside the crack, and closed his eyes. He did that sometimes. He was a son of Apollo, and a keyer, and he said that he could feel the guns, the heat in the metal and the strain, and Carrow had stopped telling him not to be ridiculous a long time ago. He was usually right.

"Two hundred," he said after a while. "Maybe. If we don't push her."

"We're going to push her tonight."

"I know." He took his hand away. "Gallows Rise."

"Gallows Rise." Carrow climbed up onto the loading platform and looked north, toward the front, eighty kilometres away. She couldn't see it from here. Nobody in the Tenth Ordnance ever saw the front. The gun lines lay eighty kilometres behind the inner belt on the old second belt, a long chain of deep pits sunk in the ash, each with its Phaethon in it and its ammunition bunkers and its crew dugouts, and from them you could see nothing but the ash and the ash cloud and the pits on either side. The front was a red glow on the horizon at night, and a rumble, and the coordinates on the fire-orders that came down the relay. "Box barrage round the ridge for the *52nd*. Then the far slope. All night. We're on the box."

"How many rounds?"

"Thirty."

Lenn was quiet for a moment.

"She won't like it," he said.

"She doesn't have to like it," Carrow said. "She has to do it."

---

They fired at the second bell of the dark watch.

Carrow had been firing Gun Four for eight rotations, and she had never got used to it, and she hoped she never would. The Phaethon was the heaviest gun the Third had on the Flats. Its shells weighed as much as a small tractor. When it fired, the whole pit jumped, and the ash leapt off the ground for fifty metres around in a great grey ring, and the sound of it went through your body and into your teeth and your bones and stayed there, ringing, long after the shell had gone.

Mosca and Dov and Borro and Abend loaded. They were the same four who had loaded Gun Four since the first barrage, the same four who had been in the ready pit when it went up in Rotation One and had woken eight days later in the vats with Carrow and Lenn and come back down the Stair together. They worked in near silence, past any need for words. The shell came up out of the bunker on the hoist and they swung it onto the loading tray and rammed it home, and the charges after it, and closed the breech, and Lenn stepped forward with his keying kit and laid his hand on the breech and spoke the keying, a few soft words in a language Carrow didn't know, and the breech glowed for a moment, gold, and went dark. Then Carrow fired.

Thirty rounds. Through the night. One every eleven minutes.

She felt the gun with every one. She stood on the platform with her hand on the barrel between rounds, on the crack, and felt the heat in the metal and the long shudder that went through it after each firing, and every time she felt it she waited for the barrel to fail. Round after round went up into the ash cloud toward Gallows Rise, eighty kilometres away, and fell somewhere she couldn't see, on the box around the ridge, to keep the Archflamen's reinforcements out.

At the twenty-ninth round Lenn put his hand on the barrel and kept it there a long time.

"One more," he said quietly. "And then stop, Nessa. Please. One more."

They fired the thirtieth round at the first bell of the two hundred and seventy-eighth cycle, and Carrow put her hand on the barrel afterward and felt it shudder and settle, and the crack was still a hairline, and Gun Four was still whole.

She sat down on the edge of the loading platform with her legs hanging over the pit and put her head in her hands.

"Well done, old girl," she said.

Lenn sat down beside her. Somewhere to the north, eighty kilometres away, the box barrage was lifting, and the mortars were laying smoke, and the *52nd* was going over the parapet.

**BASTION G-70, GALLOWS RISE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 278**

The ground in front of Bastion G-70 had taken the heaviest of the barrage and was nothing but craters.

Lerche went over the parapet at the head of the *8th* Cohort into the smoke and found that he couldn't walk. Nobody could. The craters overlapped, rim on rim, deep and steep-sided and full of loose grey ash that slid away under his boots and took him down into the bottom of each one before he could climb out of it up the far side. He went down into the first one on his back. He climbed out of it on his hands and knees and went straight down into the second.

Behind him, the first Thureos came over the parapet, ground forward ten metres to the lip of the first crater, tipped forward into it nose-down, and stuck.

Its commander tried to back it out. Lerche heard the engine scream and saw the tracks spinning in the loose ash, throwing up great grey plumes of it, and the tank going nowhere. After a minute the engine stopped screaming. The turret turned slowly, toward the bastion, and the gun fired, and went on firing, steadily, up the slope, from where it sat.

"That'll do," Lerche said to nobody. "That'll do. Stay there."

It stayed there for the rest of the cycle, a fixed pillbox in the first crater, firing up the slope at the bastion, and he never learned its commander's name.

They came out of the smoke at the foot of the slope, and the bastion opened fire.

He had been waiting for it. He had been on the Flats for five rotations and he knew exactly what it would be like, and it was worse. The pillboxes on the slope above him lit up one after another along the whole length of Bastion G-70, heavy guns and light guns and the flat white crack of lance-bolts from somewhere further up, and the fire came down the slope into the craters in long sweeping bursts, and the soldiers of the *8th* went down into the craters and stayed there. Lerche went down with them. He lay on his face in the ash at the bottom of a crater with his rifle under him and listened to the rounds going over.

Then the Colossi came up.

He had never been close to one before. They were super-heavies, the biggest tanks the Third had on the Flats, nearly as big as a Phaethon pit, slab-sided and low with twin main guns in a turret the size of a dugout, and the Eighth Line Phalanx had held them in reserve for the whole of the Big Push, finding nothing on the Flats worth spending them on. The first of them came up out of the smoke behind him on its great wide tracks, grinding over the craters with contempt, and its twin guns swung up the slope toward the bastion and fired together, and the pillbox in front of Lerche's crater disappeared.

The *8th* cheered. He heard it, all along the line of craters.

The Colossus went on up the slope, sweeping the trenches of the bastion with its guns. The bastion turned everything it had on it. Lerche lay in his crater and watched the rounds and the lance-bolts strike the great slab-sided hull, again and again, in showers of sparks and white fire, and the Colossus went on firing.

The second Colossus lost its right track to a heavy shell. He saw it happen. The shell struck the track a metre in front of the drive wheel and the track broke and came off the wheels in a long writhing iron snake, and the Colossus slewed round to the right and stopped, broadside to the bastion, in the middle of no-man's land. It went on firing, from where it sat. It couldn't go any further.

The second Thureos went over a mine. He didn't see that. He heard it, a flat hard crash somewhere behind him and to the left, and when he looked round the tank was burning, and its crew were coming out of its hatches into the smoke, and the bastion's guns found them.

"Signaller," he said.

His signaller was a boy of twenty named Corrado, in the crater beside him, with the relay set on his back. Lerche turned to him. Corrado was lying on his side in the bottom of the crater with his legs drawn up, and there was a great deal of blood in the ash under him, and the relay set on his back was smashed.

"Mine," Corrado said. His face was white. "Sorry, Centurion. There was a mine. In the crater. I didn't..."

"It's all right."

"I didn't see it."

"Nobody sees them. That's why they're mines." Lerche took the handset of the relay set and tried it. It was dead. He looked at Corrado, and at the blood, and at the smashed set, and then he looked up the slope at the bastion.

"Stay here," he said. "The medici are coming. I'll be back."

---

He went back himself.

He crawled back across the craters to the *52nd*'s forward trench, three hundred metres, with the bastion's guns sweeping the ground above him, and dropped over the parapet into the regiment's command dugout on the left of the line, where the colonel's second-in-command, a heavy grey man named Pallister, sat at the relay board with the colonel's map in front of him.

"Bastion G-70," Lerche said. He was out of breath. "Sir. It'll fall. The Colossus has broken one of the bunkers open. Direct hit. If I had a few more sections, I could take the position. It'll fall if you send the second wave now. Now, sir. Not in an hour."

Pallister looked at him, and at the map.

"The second wave's not ready, Centurion. It's still being supplied."

"Then send it as it is."

Pallister looked at the map for a moment longer. Then he picked up the handset.

---

The second wave went over the parapet across ground swept by every gun on the ridge, half-supplied, in the middle of the afternoon watch, and Lerche went back out across the craters ahead of it, and by the time it reached him the *8th* had a foothold in the bastion's forward trench.

He didn't remember most of what happened next. He remembered fragments. He remembered the forward trench of the bastion, a deep narrow cut in the basalt with the bodies of the Archflamen's soldiers lying in it, and the second wave coming down into it behind him, crowding, pushing forward. He remembered the approach trench to the broken bunker, a long zigzag cut climbing the slope, and clearing it with grenades, traverse by traverse, throwing the grenade round the corner and waiting for the crash and going round after it. He remembered the breach in the bunker, a ragged black hole in the basalt where the Colossus's shell had gone in, with smoke coming out of it, and somebody firing from inside.

"Flame," he said. "Flame section. Burn it out."

The flame section came up. Two breachers with flame lances, and they put the lances into the breach together, and the inside of the bunker went white and roaring, and the firing stopped.

He went in at the head of his soldiers.

There were still some of them alive in there, in the far corner, behind a wall of ammunition boxes, where the flame hadn't reached. He never knew how many. It was dark and full of smoke and the stink of burning, and they came at him out of the smoke, and he fought them hand to hand, with his bayonet and his pistol and, at the end, his hands. Something hit him across the left wrist and he felt the bone go. Something else, burning, struck him in the side, and he smelt his own field coat on fire, and beat it out with his good hand, and went on fighting.

Then it was over, and the bunker was his.

He organised the defence. He did it standing in the middle of the bunker with his left arm hanging and his side burned and smoking, giving orders in a voice he didn't recognise as his own, putting a heavy weapons section at the breach and a section at the inner door and sending runners back to the forward trench for ammunition. A medicus came up from the forward trench and looked at his wrist and his side and told him he was going back.

"No," Lerche said.

"Centurion..."

"No. Bind it. I'm staying."

The medicus bound it.

The remaining bunkers of Bastion G-70 fell one after another through the rest of the watch, each of them after hard fighting. The surviving Colossus, its hull blackened and scarred from end to end, ground up the slope into the bastion and stopped in a gap in its wall, where a bunker had been, and its crew killed the engine, and it became part of the wall. Lerche sent sections out along the ridge's trenches from the bastion, to the right, along the crest, taking the Archflamen's positions from the flank, and he heard the fire on the other cohorts slacken as they did. The attacks that had gone to ground got up and went forward again. Toward the end of the watch the relay operator he'd borrowed from the second wave told him that the *7th* and the *2nd* had reported their objectives taken.

The forward works on Gallows Rise belonged to the *52nd*.

Lerche sat down on an ammunition box in the middle of the bunker with his broken wrist in his lap and looked at the breach, at the grey light coming through it, and thought, for the first time in five rotations, that he might be allowed to sleep.

---

The colonel came up the slope in the last hour of the watch to see the bastion for herself, and found him there.

"Eleven thousand," she said. She sat down on another ammunition box opposite him. She was grey with ash and there was blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers. "Eleven thousand transfers, Lerche. For the whole regiment. The Répertoire's estimate was fourteen. It's the first time since the siege began that anyone's come in under the Répertoire's estimate."

"That's good, Colonel."

"It's good." She looked at his wrist, and his side. "They tell me you refused evacuation."

"Yes, Colonel."

"Why?"

He thought about it.

"I wanted to see it," he said at last. "I've been on the Flats since Rotation Three. Five rotations. Eleven transfers. I've been in a great many attacks and I've never once got to the top of anything. I've never once been in the place we were trying to take when we took it. I wanted to see what it looked like."

Lowe looked round the bunker. At the smoke-blackened walls, and the bodies, and the ammunition boxes, and the grey light through the breach.

"And what does it look like?"

"Like a bunker, Colonel," Lerche said. "That's all."

She laughed. He had never heard her laugh before. It was a short, surprised, tired sound, and it made the soldiers of the *8th* at the breach turn round and look.

"The Phalanx is sending it up to Quarterdeck Nineteen as a model action," she said. "A regiment at half strength takes a fortified ridge on a four-kilometre front in one cycle. They're going to say it proves the inner belt can be broken by limited attacks, ridge by ridge, all the way to the spur. They're already planning the next one."

"Will it work?"

"I think it might," she said. "I think it really might."

Outside, beyond the breach, the light was changing. Lerche saw it go, the grey of the afternoon watch turning darker, browner, and felt the wind change too, gusting suddenly through the breach into the bunker, hot and wet and stinking of sulphur.

The colonel stood up and went to the breach and looked out.

"Seep-storm," she said.

---

**GALLOWS RISE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 278, EVENING WATCH**

It came in across the Flats from the river in a long brown wall, rain and ash together, hot and acid, and the light went out of the sky under it, and Lerche lost sight of the valley below the ridge, and then of the forward trench, and then of the colonel standing at the breach three metres away.

Then the sky caught fire.

He didn't know what it was. Nobody on Gallows Rise did, at first. A light came down through the seep-storm from directly above, white and blinding and terribly hot, in a single long beam as thick as a tower, and where it touched the ash on the valley floor below the ridge the ash turned to glass. He saw it happen. He saw the ash go red and then white and then run, molten, in a long glowing trench a kilometre long, and set, and crack. The heat of it came up the slope and through the breach and hit him in the face with the breath of an open furnace.

"Solar lance," the colonel said. Her voice was quite steady. "That's the *Eye of Light*. That's the *Eye of Light*'s batteries."

The second beam came down on the *52nd*'s forward trench at the foot of the ridge. He saw it through the breach, through the storm, a long white line falling out of the dark, and where it fell the forward trench of the *52nd* was gone.

Then the shells.

They came down out of the low geometry through the storm, each one the weight of a Phaethon round, and where they landed they left craters the size of the Arsenal's heat-wells. Lerche felt the first one land somewhere behind the ridge, far behind, kilometres back, and felt the whole ridge jump under him. Then the second, closer. Then the third, on the slope below the bastion, and the bastion's wall cracked across, and the Colossus in the gap rocked on its tracks.

"Down," the colonel said. "All of you. Down."

They went down. Lerche lay on the floor of the bunker with his broken wrist under him and the colonel beside him and listened to the drowned fleet bombard the Flats.

There was no answer. That was what he would remember. The Third had a hundred thousand guns on the Flats, and none of them could reach a ship in the low geometry, and the drowned ships hung up there in the storm and fired down on the Third's trenches and the Third's guns and the Third's soldiers, and nothing fired back.

The bombardment lifted.

And out of the storm, burning, came the landing craft.

---

He saw the first of them through the breach. It came down out of the brown dark directly over the valley, a long low shape trailing fire from its old hull, the river burning in its seams, and hit the ash at the foot of the ridge, and burst open. Things came out of it.

They came out of it burning too. Tall, thin, black and grey, with skin as rough as slag and the river fire running along their bodies in long orange seams, in loincloths of some dark woven stuff, with blades in their hands that glowed a dull red. Dozens of them. Hundreds. They came out of the landing craft onto the ash and turned toward the ridge and came up it, running, through the storm, without a sound, and the river fire in their bodies lit them up so that he could see every one of them.

"Cinderborn," the colonel said.

The *8th* fired on them from the bastion. Lerche saw the rounds strike them and do nothing. He saw a breacher at the breach put a flame lance into the face of the first of them from five metres, and the Cinderborn walked through the flame. He saw a soldier of the *8th* throw a grenade of slag-gel, captured from the Archflamen's own stores on the ridge that morning, and the gel burst over three of them and burned, and they kept coming, with the gel burning on their bodies, and didn't seem to notice it.

They were made in the river. The river's fire ran in them. Nothing that burned could hurt them.

The *8th* broke.

He saw it begin and couldn't stop it. The soldiers at the breach turned and ran for the inner door and the trench behind the bastion and the slope beyond it, down toward the valley and the *52nd*'s lines, and the soldiers behind them saw them run and ran too. He shouted at them. The colonel shouted at them. They went anyway, a long stream of them going back down the slope into the storm, and the Cinderborn came up the slope behind them and into the bastion.

They climbed the Colossus. Lerche saw them through the breach, swarming up its scarred sides in the storm, black and orange, and tearing open its hatches with their hands, and going inside. He heard the crew. Then he didn't.

"Colonel," he said. "Go."

She looked at him.

"Go. Now. The regiment needs you. You can't stop this. Nobody can." He got up off the floor with his good hand on the ammunition box and his broken wrist hanging. "I'll hold the bunker as long as I can. Go."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she went.

He never knew whether she got out. He found out later that she did, down the slope through the storm with a dozen soldiers of the *8th* she had stopped and turned and made to walk back with her instead of run, and that she reached the regiment's lines in the valley an hour later and began at once to rally them. He never knew it then.

He held the bunker with eleven soldiers of the *8th* who hadn't run. He held it for nine minutes.

The Cinderborn came through the breach at the end of the ninth minute, and at the head of them came a tall one, taller than the rest, with the river fire running so thick in its body that it was more orange than black, and a long curved blade in its hand that glowed red from hilt to point. It came straight at him.

He fired his pistol into its chest until the pistol was empty, and it kept coming.

He thought, at the end, as the blade came down: *well, at least I got to the top of something.*

---

His implant fired. The relay operators of the *52nd*, in the valley below Gallows Rise, logged it firing and logged his upload leaving the pylon.

The Répertoire logged it arriving at Watchpoint Hades empty.

Draugos the Kindled had blessed his warbands' blades in the Phlegethon at the bend before they came up. A blade of the river severed what the Third sent upward as cleanly as Styx-iron.

The landing threw the *52nd* off Gallows Rise and gave the ridge back to the Archflamen. The model action was overtaken within the hour.

**THE GUN LINES OF THE TENTH ORDNANCE PHALANX. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 278, THIRD HOUR OF THE LANDINGS**

The gun lines of the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx were eighty kilometres behind the front, and in eight rotations nothing had ever attacked them, and they were guarded by their own crews.

Carrow was asleep in the crew dugout under Gun Four when the Cinderborn came down.

She woke to Lenn's hand on her shoulder, shaking her, and the sound of the alarm, the long falling wail of the gun lines' siren that she had heard in drills and never once in earnest. It was dark in the dugout. Lenn's face above her was white.

"Nessa," he said. "Nessa. Get up. They're here."

"Who?"

"The river," Lenn said. "The river's here."

She went up the dugout stair into the pit with her pistol in her hand, and the seep-storm hit her in the face, hot and brown and stinking, and through it, along the gun line, in both directions, she saw the fires.

There were landing craft down among the pits. Three that she could see, burning, their hulls split open on the ash, two hundred metres north and four hundred south and one somewhere further, a glow in the storm. And between the landing craft and among the pits, black and orange, moving fast, were the Cinderborn.

She saw Gun Six go. It was four pits north. She saw the Cinderborn swarm down into it, over the parapet and down the ladders and onto the loading platform, and saw the crew of Gun Six come up out of their dugout to meet them, and saw the crew go down. Then something in the pit caught, the charges or the shells in the ready bunker, and Gun Six went up in a great white column of fire that lit the whole gun line and threw Carrow flat against the wall of her own pit.

When she got up, Mosca and Dov and Borro and Abend were around her, with their rifles, and Lenn, with his keying kit under one arm and a pistol in his other hand that she had never seen him hold before.

"What do we do?" Dov said.

Carrow looked at Gun Four. At the long barrel rising out of the pit into the storm, and the hairline crack a metre from the muzzle that only she knew was there.

"We hold the pit," she said.

---

They held it.

She would never be able to explain afterward how. The gun crews of the Tenth Ordnance were gunners, not soldiers. They carried rifles because the Regulations said so, and fired them once a rotation on the range for the same reason, and none of them had ever expected to use them on anything. The Cinderborn came to Gun Four's pit in the fourth hour of the landings, a dozen of them, out of the storm from the north where Gun Six was still burning, and the crew of Gun Four met them at the parapet with rifles that couldn't hurt them.

Lenn saved them.

She saw him do it. He stood up on the loading platform in the storm with his keying kit open at his feet, and he didn't pick up his pistol. He put his hand on the breech of Gun Four, just as he did when he keyed a shell, and spoke, louder than she had ever heard him speak, in the language she didn't know. The breech glowed gold. And the glow went out of the breech, along his arm, into him, and he turned toward the Cinderborn coming over the parapet and opened his hand at them.

The light that came out of his hand was the colour of noon on a ceiling world, and it struck the first of the Cinderborn full in the chest. The river fire in the Cinderborn's body went out. It stood on the parapet for a moment, black and grey, its orange seams suddenly dark, looking down at itself. Then Mosca shot it, and it fell.

"It's the river fire," Lenn said. His voice was shaking. "Nessa. It's the river fire that makes them. Put it out and they're just..."

He opened his hand again.

They held the pit for forty minutes, Lenn putting out the river fire in the Cinderborn one by one with his father's light and the loaders and Carrow shooting them when it was out. Lenn's hand was burned black by the end of it, and he was grey-faced and swaying and could barely stand. At the end of the forty minutes a regiment of the Fifth Line Phalanx, hurried back from the line in tractors through the storm, came along the gun line from the south and drove the Cinderborn off.

Carrow sat down on the loading platform with Lenn's head in her lap and his burned hand in hers, and looked along the gun line at the fires.

The Cinderborn had destroyed eleven Phaethons in the gun lines of the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx before they were driven off. Gun Six. Gun Nine. Guns Two and Three of Battery *27*. Seven more she didn't know. Eleven of the great guns, the heaviest the Third had on the Flats, and there were no more coming down the cables to replace them.

Gun Four stood in its pit in the storm with its long barrel rising into the dark, and the hairline crack a metre from the muzzle, whole.

"You didn't tell me you could do that," Carrow said.

"I didn't know," Lenn said. His eyes were closed. "I didn't know I could. I've never tried." He was quiet for a while. "Nessa. That ship. The *Eye of Light*. The solar lances, on the ridge. That's my father's light. That's what he gave to the House. He gave it to that ship to fight the Princes of the deep." He opened his eyes and looked up at her. "And the river's got it now."

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 279 TO 300**

The Répertoire's reconstruction of the landings of the two hundred and seventy-eighth cycle counted forty-one separate descents along the Third's arc in the first six hours.

Corvis read it at her desk on the two hundred and eighty-first cycle, with the casualty returns stacked on one side of her and the quartermasters' returns on the other, and the coffee gone cold in its cup.

Forty-one landings. Single landing craft carrying a warband of a hundred Cinderborn. The *Eye of Light*'s own Lanterns, which had put down two thousand Forsworn and drowned soldiers behind the Fifth Line Phalanx at Ore Head. Nine of the Third's regimental command dugouts overrun in the first six hours, and two Phalanx headquarters attacked. The Tenth Ordnance Phalanx's gun lines, eighty kilometres behind the front, struck in the third hour, and eleven Phaethons destroyed.

And the Forsworn from the *Eye of Light* had gone through the Third's rear areas in the Empire's armour, using the Empire's codes, and walked into its command dugouts as friends. Nobody on the Flats could tell friend from enemy any more until the shooting started.

Two hundred and four thousand transfers in the first cycle of the landings. More than on any single cycle of the Big Push. Nineteen thousand of them null.

Corvis looked at that figure for a long time.

One in eleven. The highest proportion the Third had ever recorded. Most of them had fallen to the blades of the Red Furnace and the Styx-iron of the Forsworn. The rest, the Répertoire said, without saying it, to whatever had been catching the Third's uploads on the Flats since the first barrage. Which was now catching more.

She thought about the Wall at Watchpoint Hades, and Ricci, and Tribune Wren's timing graph. She thought about what Ricci's Wall must look like now.

---

The Third improvised. Corvis would say afterward, when she was asked, that given its training it had improvised creditably, and she would mean it.

Its regiments cut their trench systems into separate keeps that could be held on their own, and turned their support lines round to face outward. The Phalanx-Generals pulled cohorts back from the forward bays to form local reserves that could counter-attack a landing within the hour. The outrider squadrons, which had had little to do since the outer belt fell, became the Third's fastest means of reaching a landing before it could dig in, and their armoured hounds went racing across the Flats every watch toward the next column of smoke. Within ten cycles most landings were being destroyed within a watch of coming down.

The drowned adapted. They landed at night, and in the seep-storms, and in greater numbers.

The siege was over, and something else had begun. Corvis watched it change on the map table, cycle by cycle. For eight rotations the Third had chosen where the battles would be fought. Now it couldn't. The drowned came down wherever they chose, out of the low geometry the Fleet had lost, and where they struck hardest they put the Archflamen's line back where it had stood before the Big Push. Over the following weeks, the drowned ships brought down heavier equipment, and the Archflamen's cohorts came out across no-man's land to join the attacks on the Third's weakened forward positions. Where there had been a clear ring of trenches round the spur, there was now a ragged line that bulged forward in some places and fell back in others.

And the supply was going.

The Third's supplies came down the relay cables at Landing Shelf Kappa from convoys in the low geometry, and the Fleet had guarded those convoys for nine rotations, and the Fleet had lost the low geometry. The convoys couldn't come down. The shell stocks on the Flats were still large, thanks to the quartermasters' decision in Rotation Zero to stock for six rotations, but they couldn't be replaced now. Neither could the soldiers. The vats at Watchpoint Hades could still write the Third's transfers into new bodies, but the bodies couldn't be brought back down the cables. By the end of Rotation Eight, three hundred and ten thousand soldiers of the Third were waiting at the ceiling depots for a way down.

Corvis and the quartermasters sat for four cycles with every return they had and worked out how long the Third could fight on what it had left.

"Nine months of front time," she told the Lord-General, "at the rate of fire we've kept up since the Big Push. Fifteen at half that rate."

Castorre looked at the slate.

"Half," he said.

From the two hundred and ninetieth cycle the divine batteries were rationed to forty shells a gun a cycle and the Phaethons to six. Harassing fire on the inner belt was suspended except on leaves under attack. Every keyed shell on the Flats was placed under the Phalanx-Generals' own control. For eight rotations the Third had fought knowing its guns would always have more shells than the Archflamen's. From the two hundred and ninetieth cycle, it didn't. The Arsenal's vaults still held eight thousand years of stock, and the Archflamen's gunners, who had never rationed anything in their lives, fired more in the last months of Rotation Eight than at any time since the first barrage.

The Third had to learn to fight under an enemy sky, too. It had no anti-air guns on the Flats beyond the heavy weapons of its regiments. The ceiling-lances had kept the geometry clear for six hundred years, and nobody had ever needed any. The quartermasters converted quad-launchers to fire upward and sent them forward to the gun lines. The engineers roofed the communication trenches near the command dugouts with steel and basalt. The regiments began to post sentries facing the ceiling, which no sentry of the Third had done since the first rotation.

None of it was enough.

On the two hundred and ninety-fifth cycle a signal came down from Watchpoint Hades under the Command's seal, and Corvis read it before she took it in.

The Command had ordered an inquiry into the conduct of the siege. It would be led by the Dionysian Logistics Directorate, under the senior Logothete Irene Vasska, and it would have the power to remove the Third's command if it found that the command had failed in its duty.

Castorre read the signal without expression. Then he laid it on the desk beside the two letters he had taken out of his coat, the Archflamen's and Callix's, and looked at all three for a long time.

"Vasska," he said. "I know the name."

"She audited the Lethe holds in Rotation Three, sir. She found eleven thousand tonnes of shells on the books that weren't in the vaults. Two quartermasters were sent to the Cocytus front."

"Yes," Castorre said. "I remember." He put the letters back in his coat, and left the signal where it was. "Tell her she'll have every return we've got, Leda. Every figure. Everything."

"Yes, sir."

"She'll find what she finds."

**THE SHALE PLATEAU, BEYOND THE HEAD OF THE DEMUS GORGE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 300**

The outrider patrol was eleven hounds strong, and its decurion, a lean dark woman named Hedda Brakk who had ridden the Flats since Rotation One, brought it up onto the lip of the Shale Plateau at the first bell of the three hundredth cycle as she had every tenth cycle since the battle of the low geometry, to look at the wreck.

It lay where it had come down, a hundred and forty kilometres behind the Third's line in the south-west, at the end of a furrow three kilometres long that it had torn through the shale when the fire barge broke its back. It was a drowned transport hulk, long and black and low, split in the middle where the *Slow Mercy* had gone into it, crusted with the river bed and patched with white ice. Its holds were shut. They had been shut for ninety cycles. The staff on Quarterdeck Nineteen had marked it on their maps as the *Lament of Ixion*, from the name on its hull in the Fleet's old script, and treated it as a wreck, and sent Brakk's patrol to look at it every tenth cycle in case it wasn't.

Brakk lay on her belly on the lip of the plateau with her field-glass and looked at it.

It looked the same as it had for ninety cycles. Black hull. White ice. Shut holds.

Then, as she watched, the holds began to open.

They opened slowly, all along the length of the hulk, great doors in the black hull swinging outward one after another with a long low groan of iron that she could hear even from four kilometres away. Light came out of them. The orange light of the river. And then, out of the light, onto the shale, things began to come.

She watched for eleven minutes before she sent the first report. She wanted to be sure.

They came out of the holds in their thousands. Drowned soldiers in rotted armour of a dozen different centuries, some of it Imperial, some of it nothing she had ever seen. Drowned sailors. Things that weren't soldiers or sailors at all, that went on four legs or six or none, that she had no names for. They came out onto the shale in the orange light and formed up, in ranks, almost in order, with tall figures in black moving among them that she knew from the briefings were Forsworn.

Then the lower holds opened, at the bottom of the hulk, where it lay in the furrow. They were bigger than the others. Much bigger. And out of the lower holds of the *Lament of Ixion*, one after another, slowly, on legs as tall as a regiment's barracks, came the Behemoths.

Brakk counted them. Nine.

The tallest was sixty metres high. Its plating was black, a deep dull black that drank the orange light, black as nothing she had ever seen. The river had kept it a thousand years.

She lowered her glass and looked at the trooper beside her, a boy of twenty named Faust, whose hound was lying in the shale beside him with its ears flat against its skull and a low whine in its throat.

"Send it," she said. "Everything. Priority. To the Ninth Line Phalanx and to Quarterdeck Nineteen. Nine Behemoths. Drowned. Forge pattern. A host behind them. Moving."

"Moving where, Decurion?"

Brakk looked back through the glass. The nine Behemoths were turning, slowly, all together, on the shale in front of the hulk. Turning north. Toward the head of the Demus Gorge, and beyond it, a hundred and sixty kilometres away across the empty Flats, the rear of the Ninth Line Phalanx, which had nothing behind it at all.

"Us," she said.
