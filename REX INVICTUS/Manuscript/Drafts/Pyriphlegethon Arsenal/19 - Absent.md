## Chapter 19: Absent

*"Every family is to be informed. The families of the absent are to be told that word, and no other."*

*Commander Thaleia of Lethe Hold, order of Rotation Nine, Cycle 15*

---

**THE VAULTS BENEATH THE ACROPOLIS. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 4**

Sworn-Optio Damon Garr had stood guard on the door of Vault Eleven for three rotations, and in all that time nobody had ever tried to go through it.

There was no reason anyone should. Vault Eleven was the deepest of the old Hephaestean stores, a long cold cavern cut into the rock beneath the Acropolis at the bottom of the vault stair, and the door to it was a slab of black iron four metres high that took two Sworn to open and had a seal on it older than the Empire. Behind the door, in racks that went back into the dark further than Garr's lamp could reach, were the distillates. He had seen them once, when the Archflamen's quartermasters had taken the inventory at the start of the Hold: row on row of sealed iron flasks, each the size of a man, each stencilled with a grade and a river and a date so old that the numbers meant nothing. The Hephaestean chemists had made them six thousand years ago, before the Senate stopped them. The Senate had forbidden them, and ordered them destroyed, and they had never been destroyed. They had sat in Vault Eleven ever since, under the seal, and the Archflamen kept them there as his last weapon, for the day when all seemed lost.

Garr's orders were simple. Nobody went through the door without the Archflamen's word.

They came down the vault stair at the second bell of the dark watch.

He heard them first, a long way up the stair, and knew at once that they were not Sworn and not garrison and not anything of the Arsenal's. They walked too evenly. Their boots came down on the iron steps all together, a slow heavy tread, and there was no talk and no clink of equipment and no breath, only the boots, and under the boots, coming down the stair ahead of them, a smell he didn't know. Wet. Old. The smell of a cellar where the water had stood a long time.

Then the cold came, and the grey light.

He raised his rifle. Beside him, at the other side of the door, Sworn Kester raised his.

They came round the last turn of the stair into the light of Garr's lamp, and he saw them, and his rifle sank.

There were thirty of them. They wore armour, or what had been armour once, plate of a dozen different patterns, Imperial and older, all of it rotted through with rust and black with water, and in the gaps where it had rotted away there was something growing, grey and soft and faintly luminous, filling the gaps in the plate with soft grey growth. Their helmets were open-faced. Under the helmets they had no faces. Garr would never be able to say it any more clearly than that, afterward, to the Sworn-Centurion who questioned him. They had faces, in the sense that there were heads under the helmets and the heads had a front. But there was nothing on the front. The features had gone soft and smooth and grey, worn down past any carving left a thousand years in the rain, until there was only the shape of a face left, with nothing in it.

At their head walked a tall figure in grey, and she had a face.

It was a woman's face, long and pale and quite beautiful, and quite empty, and she looked at Garr with grey eyes that saw him and did not, he understood at once, care in the least whether he was there.

"Open it," she said. Her voice was soft and dry.

"I can't," Garr said. He was surprised to hear his own voice. "The Archflamen's orders. Nobody passes without his word."

The woman looked at him for a moment. Then she looked past him, at the black iron door and the seal on it.

"What is your name, soldier?" she said.

He opened his mouth to tell her, and found that he couldn't remember it.

It was there. He knew it was there. He could feel the shape of it, the place where it should be, a word he had said ten thousand times. But when he reached for it there was nothing in his hand, only grey, soft and cold, and the more he reached the further away it went.

"I don't..." he said.

"No," the woman said gently. "You don't. Open it."

He opened it.

He stood aside afterward and watched them carry the flasks out, two of the faceless ones to each, up the long vault stair into the dark, and he didn't know how many they took, and he didn't try to stop them. Kester didn't either. When the last of them had gone up the stair, and the grey light had gone with them, he stood at the open door of Vault Eleven with his rifle in his hands and tried for a long time to remember why he was standing there.

His name came back a little after the first bell. Most of it.

The Archflamen was told within the hour. Garr heard afterward, from Kester, who had it from a Sworn who had been in the Katabasion when the news came, that the Archflamen had listened to it with his eyes closed and his hands folded on the table, and when it was finished had said nothing at all for a long time. Then he had asked the Kindler Dace, who now commanded the Sworn, whether the Sworn could have stopped them. Dace had said no.

The Archflamen had nodded, and gone back to his prayers, and given no order.

**THE POCKET ON THE LETHE CUT, LEAF 54-49. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 6**

Trooper Jessamy Pike had been a ration clerk in the Lethe Hold for four years before the draft, and she still did sums in her head when she was frightened.

She did them now, on the fire-step of Bay 9 of the *54th*'s *3rd* Cohort, at the third bell of the dark watch, with the mist up to her knees and her rifle on the parapet in front of her, looking out at the low rise of ash a kilometre away where the picket stood. Fourteen thousand, one hundred soldiers in the pocket. Rations for nine cycles at full issue, seventeen at half, twenty-six at a third. The colonel had put them on half on the three hundred and seventy-ninth cycle. That left eleven. Water for... she did the water. Twenty cycles, if the condensers held. They wouldn't hold. Condensers never held.

The picket was still there. She could see it in the faint red glow from the ash cloud, twenty dark shapes on the top of the rise, and the tall one in the middle of them, quite still, facing the pocket. It had been there for eighteen cycles. It never moved.

"Pike," said the trooper beside her, a big soft-spoken dockhand named Hollen Cray. "What are you counting?"

"Rations."

"How many?"

"Eleven cycles. At half."

Cray was quiet for a while.

"And then?"

"And then we go on a third," Pike said. "And then a quarter. And then..." She stopped. She didn't like the end of that sum.

The mist was rising. She could feel it climbing her legs, cold, a little higher than last night. It came up out of the Lethe Cut every dark watch now, at the third bell, white and thick, and filled the trenches at the south end of the pocket and spread north along the communication trenches, and lay there until the first bell, and then went back down into the Cut. The colonel reported it to the Eighth Line Phalanx every cycle. Pike knew. She had been a ration clerk and could write a fair hand, and the colonel had borrowed her twice to copy his reports when his adjutant was busy. She had copied out the same words both times. *Mist from the Lethe Cut in the dark watch, thicker than the previous night. Cold. Disperses by the first bell. No enemy activity. Picket on the rise unchanged. Request instructions.*

The Eighth never answered. She knew that too.

She had known Colonel Vadas for four years before the draft. Everybody in the Lethe Hold's ration office had. He had run the ration board, and he had been the kindest man she had ever worked for, round and pink and cheerful, who remembered everybody's name and everybody's children's names and brought in biscuits his wife had baked on the last day of every month. When the Hold raised the *54th* off the ration rolls in Rotation Two, she had been on the list, and so had he, and she had been glad. She had thought: at least it's him.

She still thought it. Even now, with the mist at her knees and the picket on the rise and eleven cycles of rations at half. She would see him come along the trench on his rounds in the dark with his lantern and his notebook, stopping at every bay, and he would say "Jessamy" in his warm round voice, and ask how her mother was, and tell her not to freeze when the flares went up, and she would feel better.

He came along the trench at the fourth bell. He stopped at Bay 9.

"Jessamy," he said. "Hollen. How are we?"

"Cold, Colonel," Cray said.

"Yes. Yes, it is." He looked down at the mist, at their knees. "Thicker tonight."

"Yes, Colonel."

He stood there for a moment with his lantern, looking out over the parapet at the picket on the rise. His round face in the lantern light was tired and grey. She had never seen him look tired in four years in the ration office.

"Colonel," she said. "Is anyone coming?"

He didn't answer at once. He looked at the picket for a long time.

"I've asked," he said at last. "Every cycle. I'll go on asking." He turned and smiled at her, the round warm smile she remembered from the ration office. "Keep counting, Jessamy. Somebody has to know how much we've got left. It might as well be you."

**THE COMMAND DUGOUT OF THE *54TH*. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 9**

Vadas had begun to write two reports every cycle.

The first was the one he sent to the Eighth Line Phalanx at the first bell, in his small neat ration-office hand, about the mist and the picket. The second he kept in his notebook. It was a list.

He had started it on the second cycle of Rotation Nine, when he understood that nobody was going to answer the first. It was a list of his soldiers. All of them. Fourteen thousand, one hundred names, by cohort and maniple and section, in the order he had them from the regiment's roll, with a line beside each name for what had happened to them. He had copied the names out of the roll himself, every night, a few hundred at a time, sitting at his table in the dugout with his reading glasses on the end of his nose, while the mist came up the dugout stair and lay on the floor round his boots.

His adjutant, Ottilie Crane, had found him doing it on the fifth night.

"Colonel," she'd said. "What's it for?"

He'd thought about it.

"In the ration office," he'd said, "we kept a record of everything that came in and everything that went out. Every tin, every sack, every loaf. Nobody ever asked to see it. But if nobody kept it, nobody would know." He'd turned a page. "Somebody should know, Ottilie. Afterward. What we had, and what happened to it. Somebody should have the list."

She'd looked at him for a long time. Then she'd sat down at the other end of the table and taken half the roll from him and begun to copy too.

They finished it on the ninth cycle. Fourteen thousand, one hundred names, in two notebooks, in two hands. Vadas closed them both and laid them side by side on the table and put his hands flat on them.

"Thank you," he said.

"Colonel." Crane was looking at the dugout stair, where the mist was coming down. It was early. It was only the second bell. "It's early tonight."

He looked. It was. It came down the stair in a long white slow fall, step by step, and spread across the floor of the dugout, and climbed his boots, and his shins, and it was colder than it had ever been.

And there was something in it. He saw it as it reached the lamp on the table. A faint grey light glowed deep in the white.

"Ottilie," he said. "Get the centurions."

**THE LIP OF THE LETHE CUT. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 11, FIRST WATCH**

The first attack came at the change of the first watch on the eleventh cycle, without a barrage.

Pike saw it begin. She was on the fire-step of Bay 9 at the south end of the pocket, where the trench ran along the lip of the Cut, and she was looking down into the Cut, as she did every night, though the colonel had told them not to. The mist was in the Cut as it always was, a white sea filling the long black gash in the ash from wall to wall, so deep that she had never once seen the bottom. She always looked at it, and she was doing a sum.

The mist began to glow.

It was faint at first. A pale grey light, deep down in the white, a long way below her, spreading slowly, staining the mist from inside. Then it brightened. Then it began to climb.

It came up the face of the Cut toward her. She watched it come. It climbed the black rock wall of the Cut thick and heavy and slow, grey-white, glowing, filling every crack and ledge as it came, and she understood quite suddenly that it wasn't the mist. It was something in the mist, something heavier, something grey, and it was coming up out of the Cut toward the trench, and toward her.

"Gas," Cray said beside her. His voice was high. "Gas. Pike, that's gas. Hoods. Hoods."

They had hoods. Every soldier of the Third had a respirator and a sealed hood against gas. They had drilled with them every week since the *54th* was raised. Pike pulled hers on over her helmet and sealed it at the neck, as she had been taught, and checked the seal, and checked it again, and looked out through the glass eyepieces at the grey coming up the face of the Cut.

It came over the lip.

It came over the lip of the Cut along the whole southern edge of the pocket at once, a long grey wave, slow and heavy, and lay down on the ash and rolled forward into the craters and filled them, and reached the parapet of Bay 9, and came over it, and down into the trench, onto the fire-step, round her boots and her knees and her waist.

She felt it go through the hood.

She had been told that nothing went through the hood. She felt it go through anyway. It went through the seal at her neck and through the glass of the eyepieces and through the cloth, as easily as water goes through a sieve, and she breathed it, and it was cold, and it tasted of nothing at all.

She thought: what was I counting?

She had been doing a sum. She knew that. She had been doing a sum about something, rations or water, a few seconds ago, and she had the shape of it still, the place in her mind where it had been. But the numbers had gone. She reached for them and they weren't there. There was only grey.

She thought: never mind. I'll do it again.

She started the sum again. Fourteen thousand, one hundred soldiers in the... in the... She couldn't remember where. Fourteen thousand, one hundred. Rations for... She couldn't remember the rations.

She sat down on the fire-step.

---

She didn't remember sitting down. She found herself sitting, a little while later, with her back against the revetment and her rifle across her knees and the grey all round her, and she couldn't remember how she had come to be there, or how long she had been there, or where there was.

It was a trench. She knew it was a trench. She knew what a trench was. There was a soldier sitting next to her, a big soft man with his hood off and his hands in his lap, looking at nothing. She knew him. She was sure she knew him. She looked at his face for a long time and tried to remember his name and couldn't.

She looked down at her own hands. There was a rifle in them. She knew what it was. She couldn't remember why she was holding it.

There were sounds somewhere. Shouting. Guns. They seemed far off and of no importance.

She thought: there was something I was supposed to do. Something somebody told me. Something about flares.

She couldn't remember.

She sat in the grey, and the grey was cold and soft and quiet, and after a while she stopped trying to remember anything at all.

---

When she came back, the grey had thinned, and Bay 9 was full of dead soldiers, and she was lying on the trench floor with somebody's boot in her face.

She sat up. Her head ached terribly. She had no idea how long she had been gone. The hood was off; she must have taken it off, though she didn't remember doing it. The light was different. It was later. The grey lay in the bottom of the trench now, ankle-deep, glowing faintly, and above it the air was clear enough to see, and what she saw was the bay full of dead.

Some of them were the *54th*. Some of them weren't.

The ones who weren't wore armour, or what had been armour once, rotted black, and in the gaps of it something grey and soft was growing. They had no faces. She looked at one of them, lying on the fire-step where she had been sitting, with a bayonet through it, and saw where its face should have been, and saw only a smooth grey shape with nothing in it, and turned away and was sick on the trench floor.

Cray was gone. She looked for him. He wasn't in the bay. He wasn't among the dead. His rifle was lying on the fire-step where he had been sitting, and his hood beside it, and he was gone.

She found out later. She found out from a centurion of the *3rd* Cohort, a grey-faced woman named Selby who found her in Bay 9 an hour afterward and dragged her back along the communication trench to the support line. While the grey was at its thickest, Selby told her, whole maniples had put down their rifles and taken off their helmets and climbed out over the parapet into no-man's land, and walked away. Cray was one of them. Selby had seen him go. He had climbed out over the parapet of Bay 9 quite slowly, and stood up on the ash, and looked round, and walked away into the craters, south, toward the Cut, with his hands at his sides. He hadn't looked back. He hadn't seemed to know where he was going.

"Why didn't you stop him?" Pike said.

Selby looked at her.

"I couldn't remember who he was," she said.

**THE COMMAND DUGOUT OF THE *54TH*. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 11**

The first assault came out of the grey while the *54th* stood in it.

Vadas pieced it together afterward from the runners and the relay and from what Ottilie Crane had seen from the top of the dugout stair, where she had gone to look and come back down white and shaking. It was led by engines. They came out of the grey across no-man's land from the south, from the direction of the Cut: drowned Hoplons and Thureos and assault carriers, of a pattern the Forge hadn't built in a thousand years, their hulls rotted black with Lethe-water. They crossed no-man's land against almost no resistance, since the soldiers who should have fired on them were sitting in the fire bays with their rifles across their knees, trying to remember their names. They came up to the *54th*'s wire and over it and lowered their ramps, and the faceless soldiers came out.

They hadn't forgotten how to fight. Whatever else they had forgotten, they hadn't forgotten that. They went into the *54th*'s trenches with the discipline of the old legions, Crane said, in good order, by sections, sweeping the fire bays clear and throwing grenades of the grey down the dugout stairs. In close combat their long grey blades went through the *54th*'s armour and the *54th*'s bayonets turned on theirs.

The *54th* stood and fought.

Vadas would have that in his report. He would make sure of it. The *54th* stood and fought. The bakers and the clerks and the ore-lift hands and the dockhands, who had never been in a battle until that night, who had come up out of the grey with gaps in their minds and found the faceless soldiers in their trenches, stood and fought them with bayonets and rifle butts and entrenching tools and their bare hands, and died in their bays.

At the third bell, his centurions on the south end of the pocket called down their own guns on their own trenches.

He gave them leave. He sat at his table in the dugout with the relay handset in his hand and the two notebooks under his other hand and listened to Centurion Selby of the *3rd* Cohort tell him, in a perfectly level voice, that the faceless ones were in Bays 7 through 14 and the *3rd* couldn't get them out, and that she wanted the battery of divine mortars on Bays 7 through 14 at once, and that the *3rd* would take its chances.

"Your soldiers are in those bays, Centurion."

"Some of them, Colonel. The ones who remember who they are are in the support line. The ones in the bays..." She stopped. "The ones in the bays don't know what's happening to them, Colonel. Please."

He gave her the mortars.

The mortars had no keyers. Their shells weren't blessed and couldn't be. They fell on Bays 7 through 14 anyway, on the faceless ones and the *54th* alike, and on the soldiers sitting in the fire-steps with their rifles across their knees who didn't know their own names.

As the grey thinned, a second wave came across no-man's land through the counter-barrage behind the Unremembered: thralls of the Arsenal and Sworn, in the old brown-grey, at a run. They went into the trenches the faceless ones had cleared and drove the *54th*'s survivors back along the communication trenches, and by the first bell of the twelfth cycle they held the support line on the south side of the pocket.

Vadas picked up the relay handset and called the Eighth Line Phalanx.

"*54th*," he said. "Colonel Vadas. I can hold for one more cycle. After that my ammunition will be gone and my last guns overrun and my regiment scattered." He paused. "Request instructions."

There was a long silence on the line.

"Hold until relieved, Colonel," a voice said at last. He didn't know whose.

"Yes," Vadas said. "Thank you."

He put the handset down.

**THE POCKET ON THE LETHE CUT. ROTATION NINE, CYCLES 12 TO 15**

They held for three more cycles.

Pike would never be able to say how. She remembered pieces of it, as she remembered pieces of everything after the first night, with gaps between them where the grey had been. The grey came up out of the Cut again and again, every watch, glowing, and every time it came the *54th* lost more of itself. Some of them sat down in it and came back an hour later with the hour gone. Some of them sat down in it and didn't come back, and when the grey went down again they were standing up in the fire bays with their helmets off, looking round with mild puzzled faces, and then climbing out over the parapet and walking away.

The faceless ones didn't kill those. Pike saw it. She saw a faceless soldier come along a trench past a trooper of the *54th* who was standing in a fire bay with his helmet in his hands, looking at it, and the faceless soldier stopped and looked at him, if a thing with no face could look, and then went past him and on along the trench to the next bay, where a trooper of the *54th* was still holding a rifle, and killed that one.

They gathered the dead into heaps. She saw that too. In the craters in front of the pocket, in the grey, the faceless ones carried the *54th*'s dead out of the trenches and laid them in the craters in heaps, carefully, almost tenderly, one on another, and left them there in the mist.

The pocket shrank. Pike remembered it shrinking. Bay by bay, trench by trench, the faceless ones and the thralls and the Sworn came in from the south and the east and the west, and the *54th* went back before them, toward the lip of the Cut, where there was nowhere to go. The field guns went silent one by one as their ammunition ran out, and their crews left them and came back into the trenches with rifles. The regiment's last tanks, four Thureos the Eighth had left behind for want of a way to move them, fought hull-down from their pits until the drowned carriers came up and drove straight over the trenches beside them and collapsed the walls onto whoever was under them, and then knocked the tanks out one after another.

On the fourteenth cycle she saw the colonel.

She was in the last line of trenches, near the command dugout, with what was left of the *3rd* Cohort, and he came up the dugout stair into the grey light with his lantern in one hand and his two notebooks under his arm. He was quite calm. His round face was grey and filthy and there was blood on his collar, and his reading glasses were gone, and he looked along the trench at the soldiers of the *54th* crouching in it, and smiled at them.

"Jessamy," he said, when he came to her.

"Colonel."

"Do you know how much we've got left?"

She had done the sum. She had done it that morning, while the grey was down, before she could forget it again.

"Nothing, Colonel," she said. "We've got nothing left."

He nodded slowly.

"Yes," he said. "I thought so." He looked down at the two notebooks under his arm. Then he held one of them out to her. "Take this, Jessamy. Will you? Ottilie had the other one, and I don't know where Ottilie is. Take this, and keep it. Whatever happens. Keep it."

She took it. It was heavy, a thick black notebook with a cracked cover, and she knew what it was before she opened it. She had copied half the names in it herself.

"Colonel..."

"Keep it," he said. He put his hand on her shoulder, briefly, as he had in the ration office when she'd done a long sum right. Then he turned and went back along the trench, toward the south, toward the lip of the Cut, with his lantern, and she watched him go until the grey came up again and she couldn't see him any more.

---

She never knew what happened to Colonel Vadas. Nobody did. The Répertoire logged his implant firing at the second bell of the fifteenth cycle, at the lip of the Cut, and logged his upload arriving at Watchpoint Hades null. That was all.

All contact with the *54th* Trench Regiment ended at the fourth bell of the fifteenth cycle, when its last pocket was broken.

**THE GREY. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 15, FOURTH BELL**

Pike was sitting in a crater.

She didn't know how she had come to be there. She had a notebook in her lap, a thick black notebook with a cracked cover, and her hands were folded on top of it. She didn't know where the notebook had come from. She knew it was important. She knew that someone had given it to her, and told her to keep it, and that she was keeping it.

It was quiet. The grey lay all round her, soft and cold and glowing faintly, and through it she could see shapes moving, slowly, without hurry: soldiers, or things that had been soldiers, walking among the craters. Some of them had faces. Some of them didn't. They didn't seem to notice her.

There were heaps of the dead in the craters round her. She looked at them without much interest.

She tried to remember her name.

It was there. She could feel the shape of it. It had two parts, she thought, and the first part was longer than the second, and somebody had said it to her recently in a warm round voice. She reached for it, and it wasn't there.

She opened the notebook.

It was full of names. Page after page of them, in two different hands, by cohort and maniple and section, with a line beside each one. She turned the pages slowly. She didn't know any of the names. She didn't know why there were so many of them, or why someone had written them down.

But there was a number at the bottom of the last page, in the first of the two hands, small and neat, underlined twice. *14,100.*

She looked at it for a long time.

She thought: I know how to do this.

She didn't know what the number was. She didn't know what it was for. She didn't know her own name, or where she was, or why she was sitting in a crater in the grey with the dead round her. But she knew how to do a sum. She had always known how to do a sum. It was the last thing in her that the grey hadn't reached, deep down, at the bottom of everything, where she couldn't remember putting it.

She put her finger on the first name in the notebook, and began to count.

**LETHE HOLD. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 15, SEVENTH BELL**

Commander Thaleia of Lethe Hold received the Répertoire's list three hours after it was issued.

It came by relay to her office on the upper tier of the Hold, where she had sat for eleven years, and her clerk brought it in on a slate and put it on her desk and went out again without a word, which was how she knew before she read it.

She read it.

It was the list of the *54th* Trench Regiment, Eighth Line Phalanx, *3rd* Siege Army, raised from the ration rolls of the Lethe Hold in Rotation Two. Fourteen thousand, one hundred names. Against ten thousand, nine hundred of them, the Répertoire had entered a single word: NULL. They had been transferred during the four cycles of the stand on the Lethe Cut, every one of them, and every one of them had arrived at Watchpoint Hades with nothing in it.

Against the remaining three thousand, two hundred names, the Répertoire had entered a different word.

ABSENT.

She read the note at the bottom of the list. It was brief and quite clear. The three thousand, two hundred had never been transferred. Their implants still reported a heartbeat. Since they were not dead, the Répertoire could not list them as dead. Since they were not with their regiment, and their regiment no longer existed, it could not list them as present. It had entered them as absent.

Thaleia read the note three times. Then she put the slate down on her desk and sat looking at it.

She had signed the *54th*'s draft orders herself, in Rotation Two, when the shells stopped coming and the Third needed soldiers and the Lethe Hold had nothing else to give. She had signed them at this desk. She remembered the day. She had known it was a ration-roll regiment, bakers and clerks and dockhands and ore-lift hands, and she had known they had never been trained, and she had signed anyway. The Third had asked, the Hold owed the Third, and the Third had promised that every one of them would come back down the Stair.

And she had known Corin Vadas for twenty years.

He had run the Hold's ration board. He had been the kindest man on the board and the most exact, and he had brought biscuits his wife baked to every meeting, and when the Hold raised the *54th* and nobody would take the regiment, he had come to her office and sat in the chair on the other side of this desk and said, "Well, Commander, I suppose somebody has to, and I know where everything is." She had given it to him. She had been grateful.

She looked down the list until she found his name.

VADAS, CORIN. COLONEL. NULL.

She sat still for a long time.

Then she called her clerk back in.

"Every family," she said. "Every family of every soldier on this list. They are to be informed. All of them. By the end of the cycle."

"Yes, Commander."

"The families of the null are to be told that their soldier was killed in the defence of the Lethe Cut, and did not return." She paused. "The families of the absent are to be told that their soldier is absent. That word, and no other, whatever they ask."

The clerk looked at her.

"Commander, they'll ask what it means."

"Then tell them the truth," Thaleia said. "Tell them nobody knows."

The clerk went out. Thaleia sat at her desk alone. After a while, she reached for the Hold's register, the great bound book that every commander of Lethe Hold had kept since the Hold was founded, in which every regiment the Hold had raised was entered, and every return, and every loss. She opened it at the page for the *54th*.

There was a code for this. She knew there was. She had never used it. No commander of Lethe Hold had used it in her lifetime, or in her predecessor's. She had to look it up in the back of the register, in the table of codes, and when she found it she sat looking at it for a long moment before she picked up her pen.

CODE 44. A regiment lost entire, with no return.

She entered it on the page for the *54th*, in her own hand, with the date. Then she closed the register.

---

She went herself to the house of Corin Vadas's wife that evening.

It was on the fourth tier of the Hold, a small house in a row of small houses, with a window box of grey Lethe-moss and a brass knocker in the shape of a hand. Thaleia knocked. After a while the door opened, and a small round grey-haired woman in an apron stood in the doorway, with flour on her hands.

She looked at Thaleia, at the Commander's insignia on her collar, and at her face. Then she looked down at her own floury hands for a long time.

"Come in, Commander," she said at last. "I've just put the biscuits in. He liked me to bake on the last day of the month. I never stopped." She stood aside. "I suppose I'll have to, now."

Thaleia went in.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 16**

The Third's soldiers called the southern Flats the Grey from then on.

Corvis heard the word for the first time on the sixteenth cycle, from a runner of the Seventh Line Phalanx who came up the cables to Quarterdeck Nineteen with a dispatch and stood in her doorway afterward, waiting to be dismissed, looking at the map on her wall.

"That's the Grey, ma'am," he said, pointing. "Down there. Where the *54th* was."

She looked at the map. At the place on the southern Flats, against the Lethe Cut, where the *54th*'s pocket had been. The augur overlay had already begun to mark it: a pale wash, faintly grey, spreading out from the Cut over Leaves 54-49 and 54-50 and the craters round them.

"Why do they call it that?"

The runner thought about it.

"Because that's what it is, ma'am," he said. "It's grey. It stays grey. The outriders went down there yesterday to look. They say the grey's still lying in the bottoms of the trenches and the craters. They say there are things moving in the dugouts. Out of the river." He paused. "And they say the *54th*'s still there. Some of them. Walking round. In the grey. Among the dead. Nothing touches them."

"The absent."

"Yes, ma'am." He looked at the floor. "The outriders say one of them was sitting in a crater, ma'am. A girl. With a book in her lap. Counting."

Corvis looked at him for a long moment.

"Counting what?"

"They didn't know, ma'am. They didn't get close. The grey was too thick." He looked back up at the map. "They say she was still counting when they left."

---

For the Unremembered, the *54th* had been a test, and the test had succeeded. From then on they used Grey Seventh wherever they went, and the Third's soldiers learned that a grey light in the craters meant an attack within the hour.

Wherever the Unremembered passed, they left the ground grey behind them. They did it deliberately. Corvis came to understand that slowly, over the cycles that followed, from the outriders' reports and the Répertoire's maps and the long careful questioning of the few soldiers of the Third who had seen the faceless ones and come back with their memories. The Unremembered had no interest in the Archflamen's war. They were indifferent to whether the Arsenal stood or fell. Their purpose was the Lethe's, which was for the Flats to forget that anyone had ever held them, and they were preparing the southern Flats, carefully, rite by rite, as ground fit for the things in the river that had not yet come up.

The Third had no defence against Grey Seventh. The quartermasters issued sealed hoods of the pattern worn against Lethe seep in the holds, which delayed the vapour by about a minute. The Apollonian physicians at Watchpoint Hades tried draughts of nectar taken before an attack, which let some soldiers who had breathed the grey recover more of what they had lost, though never all of it. The only thing that worked reliably was distance. Regiments on the edge of the Grey were ordered to keep their forward trenches thinly manned and their soldiers in the support line until an attack had shown itself, so that the Unremembered's attacks fell on emptier trenches. A thinly held trench was easily taken. The Third gave up four kilometres of the southern Flats in the rotation after the *54th* was lost.

On the Répertoire's maps, the pale wash spread. Corvis watched it, cycle by cycle. From the Lethe Cut, north and west, toward the Demus Rim.

She kept a copy of the *54th*'s list in her desk drawer, with the two words on it. She didn't know why. She took it out sometimes, late in the dark watch, and read the names.

There was a name halfway down the list of the *3rd* Cohort that she found herself coming back to, though she had never met the woman and never would. PIKE, JESSAMY. TROOPER. Formerly a ration clerk, the roll said, of the Lethe Hold.
