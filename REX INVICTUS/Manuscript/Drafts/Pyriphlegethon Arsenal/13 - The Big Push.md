## Chapter 13: The Big Push

*"Give it everything. It keeps what you give it."*

*Colonel Damaris Lowe,* 52nd *Trench Regiment, Eighth Line Phalanx*

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 60**

The Répertoire named the offensive Operation Antilochus, after a son of Nestor who had died saving his father, and Leda Corvis read the name at the top of the first page of the plan and decided that somebody in the Répertoire had either a sense of humour or none at all, and that she would never find out which.

The soldiers of the Third were calling it the Big Push before the orders had reached the regiments. Corvis heard it from her own clerks. Nobody on the Flats ever used the official name, and after the first week neither did she.

It was the largest offensive the Third had mounted since the start of the siege, and the Répertoire had planned it on the scale of the Saturation, with a fire plan that the Saturation had lacked. Every Line Phalanx was to attack on its own front. The Second and the Fifth in the north would fix the Archflamen's cohorts along the old northern spur and the Mora Cut. The Seventh and the Ninth would attack on the western Flats. The Eighth, fresh and at full strength, would make the main effort in the centre, on the leaves the Répertoire judged weakest. Behind the Eighth, the Fourth Assault Phalanx, rebuilt after the loss of its reserve to the Wedge in Rotation Four, waited with three armoured regiments, two hundred and sixty tanks and a hundred and ten Hoplons to drive through wherever the belt broke. The Tenth Ordnance Phalanx and the divine batteries of the Line Phalanxes had been given eleven cycles of preparatory fire, the longest bombardment of the siege. The quartermasters had spent two rotations stockpiling for it. There were nine million shells on the Flats, a third of them keyed, which was more than the Third had fired in its whole first three rotations.

The Répertoire's estimate for the offensive was one million, eight hundred thousand transfers over thirty cycles.

Corvis had read that figure many times now, and it still surprised her each time she looked at it. She had sat with the quartermasters and the vat-masters for three cycles working out what it meant. Watchpoint Hades could return about forty thousand soldiers a cycle at full capacity, nine days after their deaths. The Command had agreed to clear the vats for the Third and to suspend the posting of returned soldiers to other theatres for the duration, so that every soldier who went up on the Flats would come back down the Stair to the same regiment. But the vats were the limit, and nothing the Command agreed to could change it. An offensive that transferred more than forty thousand soldiers a cycle would use up the Third faster than the vats could put it back together. By her own count, the Third could keep up the losses of a full general offensive for ten cycles at most before its forward cohorts began to run out of soldiers.

If the second belt hadn't broken by then, it wouldn't break.

She put that on the last line of her summary, by itself, and took it in to the Lord-General.

Castorre read the summary through. He read the last line twice. Then he signed it.

"I'll tell the Command we expect to be before the inner belt by the end of the rotation," he said. "And move the College's liturgy of thanksgiving to Rotation Ten."

"It's already in Rotation Nine, sir. You moved it from Five."

"Then move it again." He put the stylus down. "Is Colonel Lowe here?"

"Outside, sir."

"Send her in."

Lowe came in with her helmet under her arm and the grey ash of the western Flats still on her boots, and stood in front of the Lord-General's desk at attention. She was thirty-four and looked older, a tall, spare, fair-haired woman with a long, serious face and the grey eyes of her ancestor, and Corvis, who had read her file, thought that she had the stillest hands she had ever seen on an officer of the Third.

"Colonel," Castorre said. "Sit, please. Thank you for coming up."

"Sir."

"Your regiment's the point of the Phalanx's main effort. You've seen the plan."

"I have, sir."

"What do you think of it?"

Lowe considered him for a moment. Corvis saw her decide to answer honestly, and liked her for it.

"It's sound arithmetic, sir," she said. "Eleven cycles of fire, nine million shells, a wave on the whole front. It'll work if the belt is what the Répertoire thinks it is."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it'll cost what the Répertoire thinks it'll cost, sir, and a good deal more." She put her helmet on her knee. "I've been in the forward bays of Leaf 57-40 for three months. I've watched that belt every night. It doesn't behave the same as a line of trenches. Every time we put something into it, a raid, a sap, a barrage, it takes it and it gives nothing back. I've never seen ground like it. You give it soldiers and it keeps them. You give it shells and it keeps those too, and the next morning it's the same as it was." She paused. "If we're going to break it, sir, we'll have to give it everything. It keeps what you give it."

Castorre looked at her for a long time.

"Thank you, Colonel," he said at last. "That will be all."

When she had gone, he sat for a while without speaking. Then he took the two letters out of his coat, the Archflamen's and Callix's, and laid them side by side on the desk, and looked at them, and put them back.

**LEAF 50-27, THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE *29TH*. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 89**

Trooper Jory Cade had looked at the thirty-one every morning for two rotations, and on the morning the bombardment began he looked at them again.

You couldn't help it on Leaf 50-27. They were there in front of you as soon as you stood up on the fire-step at stand-to, a hundred and forty metres out on the Arsenal's wire, in front of the dark hump of Ceiling-Lance Seven, tied up in a long row along the second strand where the Arsenal's soldiers had put them after the raid, and every morning the light came up grey through the ash and there they were. They didn't look like people any more. They hadn't for a long time. They were a row of old grey bundles hung up on the wire to dry, with the black stains on the bandages long since gone brown and the ash drifted up against them in little banks, and now and then the wind took one of them and it moved a little on the wire, and everyone in the forward bays looked away.

Cade had known four of them. He had known Iska Tamm best. She had been a breacher in the heavy maniples, and he had been her loader for a year in the *2nd* Cohort before they moved her up, and she'd taught him how to clean a flame lance without taking the skin off his fingers. She was the fourteenth from the left. He had counted, the first morning, when the light came up and the medicus came back in across the ash with her face set like stone and wouldn't tell anyone what Tamm had said to her out there. He had counted every morning since.

The bombardment started at the first bell.

He had never heard anything like it. Nobody had. Every gun in the Third fired at once, from the Mora Cut to the Lethe Cut, a hundred and nine kilometres of guns, and the sound of it fell on the Flats with the weight of the roof of the world coming in, and went on falling through the minute, the hour and the watch. The second belt disappeared behind a wall of smoke and fire that went up into the ash cloud and lit it from beneath, orange and red and the sickly white of the keyed shells, and the ground shook under Cade's boots on the fire-step and went on shaking. The dirt sifted down from the revetments in a steady trickle. The water in the sump at the bottom of the bay danced in rings.

"Eleven cycles of that," said Corporal Fenna Rusk beside him, shouting into his ear. She was no relation to the other Rusk, and she was sick of people asking. "Eleven cycles. There'll be nothing left."

Cade said nothing. He was looking at the thirty-one.

The Arsenal's gunners had been ranging on Leaf 50-27 since the raid. Everyone in the *29th* knew it. They had worked out every metre of no-man's land in front of Lance Seven in the two rotations since, every crater, every fold in the ground, every gap in the wire, and every few cycles they had dropped a single shell somewhere on it to check their figures. Cade had stood on the fire-step and watched them do it. A single shell, at a single spot, and then nothing for three cycles, and then a single shell at another spot. They were patient. They had all the shells they wanted and nowhere else to put them.

Now the bombardment fell on the wire in front of Lance Seven, on the second belt's forward trench and the shelters beneath it and the long dark hump of the lance itself, and the thirty-one went up into the smoke with everything else, and Cade lost sight of them.

He found he was holding his breath, and let it out.

"Good," Corporal Rusk said beside him, more quietly now. She had seen where he was looking. "Good. Let them burn. It's the best thing for them."

---

The bombardment went on for eleven cycles. The Third's soldiers slept through it, after the first two, since soldiers could sleep through anything. They ate through it and wrote letters through it and played cards in the dugouts through it, and the dust came down from the roofs in a steady trickle into their tea. The Arsenal's gunners shelled the Third's lines every watch to disrupt the preparations, and the Third's gunners shelled the Arsenal's gunners back, and the trench walls on Leaf 50-27 shook day and night and the sump in the bottom of the bay never stopped dancing.

On the sixth cycle Cade went to see Medicus Valcourt.

She had her aid post in a dugout on the support line behind Leaf 50-27, three metres down under a roof of ash-sacks and steel joists, and she was sitting at her table with her register in front of her when he came down the steps. She had kept it since Rotation Four. Every soldier of the *29th* who had transferred, every one who had come back wrong, every one who had gone null, in her small neat hand in columns down the page. The *29th*'s colonel had told her to stop keeping it, two rotations ago. Everyone in the regiment knew that she hadn't.

"Trooper," she said. "Cade, isn't it. *2nd* Cohort. Are you hurt?"

"No, Medicus."

She waited. She was a small dark woman with grey in her hair and a face that had been lined when she came to the *29th*, and had become a great deal more lined since, and she had a way of waiting that made you say things.

"I want to know what she said," Cade said. "Tamm. The night you went out to her. You came back and you wouldn't tell anyone. I was her loader for a year, Medicus. I want to know."

Valcourt looked at him for a long time. Then she closed the register.

"She asked me for a grenade," she said.

Cade said nothing.

"I didn't have one. I had nothing but dressings and a needle. I sat with her for an hour. She told me about a man in a grey coat who had come to the wire and sat with them, and a keyer from the Forge who cried. She said that somewhere else wanted them more." Valcourt's voice was quite steady. "I don't know what she meant by it. I've thought about it every night since and I don't know. Then she asked me for a grenade, and I didn't have one, and I came back." She opened the register again and turned it round, and showed him the page. TAMM, ISKA. *29th*. HEAVY MANIPLE. NULL. In her small neat hand. "That's all, Trooper. That's everything."

Cade looked at the page for a while.

"Thank you, Medicus," he said.

"Cade." She closed the register again. "Do you carry a grenade? For yourself? The last one?"

"Yes, Medicus."

"So do I," Valcourt said. "Now."

**LEAF 50-27. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 100**

Provost-Optio Severa Quill came down to the *29th*'s *5th* Cohort on the hundredth cycle, in the dark watch, an hour before the first wave was due to move into the fire bays, and the soldiers of the *5th* watched her come down the communication trench with the expression soldiers always kept for a provost-optio, which was no expression at all.

She was used to it. She had been a provost-optio for nine years, in six regiments, on four fronts, and she had come down into a good many communication trenches in the dark watch before an attack and been looked at in exactly that way. She wore the plain black field coat of the Command with the silver flash of the Répertoire on the collar, and a pistol on her belt, and a slate on a cord round her neck with her warrant on it, and nothing else that marked her as different from the troopers she walked among. It was enough. Every soldier on the Flats knew what the flash on the collar meant. It meant that the officer wearing it carried the Répertoire's authority, and could use it.

Each cohort of the Third went into an attack with a provost-optio. The Regulations were quite clear on why. Her task was to keep the cohort going forward. If soldiers fell back without orders, she was to stop them, by any means. The Regulations left the means unspecified, and every provost-optio knew what that meant.

The *5th*'s centurion met her at the head of the trench. He was a broad, slow, grey-haired man named Antoun Dree, who had been in the *29th* for twenty years and had the patient, heavy look of a man who had been killed a great many times and was no longer much interested in the question.

"Provost," he said.

"Centurion."

"You'll want to know where we're going."

"I've read the plan, Centurion. Thank you. I'd like to walk the trench before they move up."

"Help yourself." Dree looked at her for a moment. "They're good soldiers, Provost. The *5th*. They've been on this leaf since the beginning. They dug the first rifle pits on the Flats. They took the outer belt. They held the Drum." He paused. "They'll go."

"I'm sure they will, Centurion."

She walked the trench. It was full of soldiers, the first wave of the *5th* packed into the fire bays and the second wave behind them in the support line, loaded with grenades and ammunition and three cycles' rations, sitting on the fire-steps and the trench floor with their rifles between their knees and the dull orange light of the barrage on their faces. Most of them didn't look at her. Some of them did, and looked away again at once. She walked down the whole length of the cohort's front and back again and looked at every face.

She had learned to read soldiers before an attack. Nine years of it. You could tell a cohort that would go from a cohort that wouldn't, most of the time, and you could tell which of the soldiers in it would be the first to come back, if any of them came back. It was in their faces, and in their hands, and in where they were looking. It was a thing you learned, and she had learned it well.

The *5th* were looking at the wire.

A good many of them were, more than she had ever seen in a cohort before an attack, and they were all looking at the same thing, out over the parapet into the smoke, at a place a hundred and forty metres away where the second strand of the Arsenal's wire ran across the front of the lance. She couldn't see anything there. The barrage was falling on it, and the smoke was too thick. They kept looking at it all the same.

She stopped beside a young trooper on the fire-step of the third bay, a thin dark boy of perhaps twenty with a smear of ash on his cheek, who was looking at it harder than any of them.

"Trooper," she said. "What's out there?"

He looked round at her. He saw the flash on her collar and his face went still.

"Nothing, Provost," he said.

"What were you looking at?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"The thirty-one, Provost," he said. "From the raid. On Lance Seven. They're out there on the wire." He looked back at the smoke. "We look at them every morning. Everyone does. You can't help it."

Quill had read the report of the raid on Lance Seven. She had read the Répertoire's assessment of the rescue, and the dissent, and its refusal. She hadn't known that the thirty-one were still out there.

"What's your name, Trooper?"

"Ostrander, Provost. Tam Ostrander."

"Thank you, Ostrander."

She went on down the trench. At the end of it she stopped and looked back along the bays, at the soldiers of the *5th* sitting in the orange light with their rifles between their knees, looking at the wire, and something stirred at the back of her mind, a small cold uneasiness that she did not like and could not name.

She wrote in her slate: *5th Cohort. Morale questionable. Attention fixed on Lance Seven. The thirty-one.* Then she put the slate away.

At the first bell of the hundred and first cycle the barrage stopped.

**LEAF 50-27. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 101**

The silence was the worst thing.

Cade had heard the guns for eleven cycles, every hour of every watch, and when they stopped the silence came down over the Flats so suddenly that it was almost a sound in itself, a great ringing emptiness in his ears. Somewhere along the trench a whistle blew. Then another, and another, all along the front, the whistles of every cohort of the Third from the Mora Cut to the Lethe Cut, a hundred and nine kilometres of them, thin and shrill in the silence.

"Over," said Centurion Halloran of the *2nd*, beside him. "Over. Over. Go."

Cade went up the ladder and over the parapet with the rest of the first wave of the *2nd* Cohort, four hundred thousand soldiers of the Third going over the parapet along a hundred and nine kilometres of front at the same moment, in the grey half-light of the first bell, into the smoke.

The ground was soft. That was the first thing he noticed. Eleven cycles of shells had churned the ash of no-man's land into a deep grey powder that sucked at his boots and slid away under him, and it was hard to walk on and harder to walk on in a line. He tried to keep the line. Everyone tried. The *29th* had been taught to advance at a walk, in parade order, as the Third had advanced for six hundred years, and they walked forward into the smoke in a long ragged line with their rifles held across their chests, and the smoke closed in around them, grey and stinking.

The Arsenal's thralls were already coming up out of their shelters.

They had sat out the barrage underground, as they always did, thirty metres down in the deep shelters beneath the second belt, and they had come up the moment it stopped. Cade had known they would. Everyone had known. The bombardment had been meant to kill them, and it never did. It churned up the ground and broke the wire and smashed the trenches and killed the ones caught in the open, and the rest sat it out underground and came up their shafts to their guns when it lifted. They were at their guns now. He heard them before he saw anything, a long rattle from somewhere ahead in the smoke, low along the ground, then another, and another, and the rounds came out of the smoke a metre above the ash, and the line around him began to fall.

He went down. Everyone went down. He hit the ash on his face with his rifle under him and lay there, and the rounds went over him in long sweeping bursts, and somewhere to his left a man was screaming.

Then the shells began to land.

The Arsenal's gunners had been ranging on Leaf 50-27 for two rotations. They had every metre of it. Cade lay in the ash and felt the first salvo come down exactly on the line of the *2nd* Cohort, where it had gone to ground, and the second come down exactly on the line of the *3rd* behind it, and the third exactly on the gap between them, and he understood that they had known exactly where the *29th* would go to ground, and when, and that they had been waiting two rotations to do this.

The smoke cleared for a moment in front of him. He saw the wire, a hundred metres away, the Arsenal's second strand running across the front of the lance. The thirty-one were still on it. The barrage hadn't touched them. They hung there in their long grey row, unchanged, with the ash banked against them, in the middle of the shellfire, and nothing in the world could move them. He looked for Tamm, the fourteenth from the left, and found her.

Then the smoke closed in again.

He lay in the ash for a long time. The shells came down around him in salvoes, and the guns went on sweeping the ground above him, and the screaming to his left stopped and started again and stopped. Somebody crawled past him, going back, dragging a leg. Then somebody else, not dragging anything, going back fast on his hands and knees.

Cade lay and looked at the place in the smoke where the wire was.

He thought about the thirty-one. He thought about Tamm on the wire, asking Valcourt for a grenade, and Valcourt not having one. He thought about the eleven cycles of barrage that hadn't moved them, and the two rotations the Arsenal's gunners had spent waiting for this, and the black stains on the bandages, and the null.

He thought: I don't want to die out here, in front of them.

He turned round in the ash and began to crawl back.

---

Quill saw the first of them coming back at the end of the first hour.

She had gone over with the *5th*'s first wave, as the Regulations required, at the rear of the line where she could see the whole cohort, and she had gone to ground with the rest of them when the guns opened. She lay now in a shallow crater in the ash fifty metres in front of the *29th*'s parapet with her pistol in her hand, watching the smoke ahead of her, where the *5th* had gone to ground among the *2nd* and the *3rd*. She had watched the barrage walk up and down the line of them, salvo after salvo, with a precision she had never seen from an enemy's guns, and she had watched the line break up into separate soldiers lying in the ash, and some of those soldiers stop moving.

Then she saw soldiers coming back out of the smoke.

They came singly at first, crawling. A man dragging a leg. Another man behind him, unhurt, crawling fast. Then two together, then three. They came out of the smoke on their hands and knees and their bellies, heading for the parapet, and none of them looked at her, and none of them were wounded except the first.

She knew what this was. She had seen it before, twice, on other fronts, in nine years. It always began like this. A few soldiers, then a few more, and if nobody stopped them, the cohort.

She stood up out of the crater.

"Halt," she said. She didn't shout. She had learned long ago that a provost-optio who shouted was a provost-optio who was frightened, and soldiers could tell. "Halt. Go back to your line."

The nearest of them stopped, on his hands and knees in the ash three metres away, and looked up at her. He was a thin dark boy of about twenty with a smear of ash on his cheek. She knew him. It was Ostrander, from the third bay, who had been looking at the wire.

"Provost," he said. His voice was shaking. "Provost. They're on the wire. They're still on the wire. The shells didn't move them. They're just... we can't..."

"Go back to your line, Trooper."

"I can't." He was crying. She saw it, the tears cutting tracks through the ash on his cheeks. "I can't, Provost. They'll put us on the wire. They'll put us on the wire like them. I can't."

"Trooper Ostrander. Go back to your line. That is an order."

He looked at her. Then he looked back at the smoke behind him, toward the wire. Then he began to crawl forward, past her, toward the parapet.

She shot him.

She did it as she had been trained to do it, once, in the back of the head, at three metres, so that his implant would fire cleanly and his upload would go up whole, and he fell forward on his face in the ash and was still. She heard the faint tick of his implant firing. The Regulations were quite clear on that, too. A soldier executed on the field for cowardice was transferred, not killed. He would wake at Watchpoint Hades in nine days with a note on his slate and a court-martial waiting for him, and if the court-martial was lenient he would be back on the Flats in a month. It was a delay. That was all.

She stood over him with the pistol in her hand and looked at the place in the smoke where the wire was, and thought, quite clearly, *if the Mast is listening*.

Then she put the thought away. There were two more of them coming out of the smoke behind him, and she turned to meet them.

---

Cade came back over the parapet of the *29th*'s forward trench at the end of the second hour, and dropped into the fire bay, and found it full.

The second wave was in it. The second wave of the *2nd* Cohort and the *3rd* and the *5th*, packed into the fire bays and the support trench, waiting to go over as the first went forward, loaded down with grenades and ammunition and three cycles' rations, and they had been watching the first wave come back. There were a good many of the first wave in the bay already, lying on the trench floor and sitting against the revetments with their heads in their hands, and more were dropping over the parapet every minute, and the second wave were looking at them.

"Get up," a centurion of the second wave was shouting. Cade didn't know him. "Get up. Get back out there. Get up."

Nobody got up.

"*Get up.*"

The Arsenal's barrage came down on the forward trench.

It came down on the fire bays and the support trench and the communication trenches all at once, in one tremendous salvo, the gunners on the second belt dropping their range from no-man's land to the *29th*'s own lines in a single movement, timed to the minute the first wave came back, and the trench around Cade went up in fire and smoke and flying earth. He was knocked flat. When he got up, there was a hole in the revetment beside him a metre across, and the centurion who had been shouting was gone, and the bay was full of smoke and screaming.

Then the fighting started.

He never knew afterward who began it. He saw some of it and not the rest. He saw a trooper of the second wave seize a trooper of the first by the collar and try to drag him to the ladder, and the trooper of the first hit him in the face with the butt of his rifle. He saw an optio of the *3rd* trying to push a crowd of the first wave back up the ladder, and the crowd pushing back, and the optio going down under them. He saw two soldiers of the *5th* rolling on the floor of the bay among the smoke and the bodies, fighting with their bare hands, and he never knew which wave either of them was from. And all the time the barrage came down on them, salvo after salvo, the gunners on the second belt walking it up and down the forward trench of the *29th* with the same patient precision they had used on no-man's land.

He saw the provost-optio of the *2nd* Cohort.

His name was Anders Vey, and he had come down to the *2nd* in the dark watch just as Quill had come down to the *5th*, in his black field coat with the silver flash on his collar. Cade had seen him in the bay before the attack, a tall thin pale young man who had looked at every face in the cohort and written something on his slate. He was standing on the fire-step of the next bay now, with his pistol in his hand, and he had shot two soldiers of the first wave who were trying to get past him into the communication trench, and he was turning to shoot a third.

Somebody shot him.

It was a single shot, from the floor of the bay, close. Vey's head jerked and he went sideways off the fire-step into the sump and lay still, face down in the grey water, and his implant ticked. Cade heard it. He saw who had fired. It was Optio Merrin Holt of the *2nd*'s *1st* Maniple, a small fierce grey-haired woman who had been in the *29th* for fourteen years and had taught Cade to march, and she lowered her rifle and looked at Vey lying in the sump for a moment without any expression at all.

Then she looked up, and saw Cade watching her.

They looked at each other for a long moment, through the smoke, across the wrecked bay.

Then Holt turned away and went down the communication trench toward the rear, walking, with her rifle in her hand, and Cade watched her go, and said nothing, then or ever.

---

Within two hours of the whistles, the attack of the *29th* Trench Regiment on Leaf 50-27 had become a rout.

Quill came back over the parapet near the end of it. She had shot three soldiers of the *5th* in no-man's land, and she would have shot more, but the barrage had come down on the forward trench and the second wave had begun to come apart, and she had understood, standing in the ash with her pistol in her hand, that there were no longer enough of them going forward to make any difference. She came back over the parapet with the last of the first wave and dropped into a bay full of smoke and dead soldiers, and found the *5th* gone.

The bay was empty except for the dead. The communication trench behind it was full of soldiers going back, the first wave and the second together, in a long jostling stream, not running, but not stopping either, and the barrage falling on them.

She stood in the bay and watched them go.

Centurion Dree was sitting on the fire-step at the end of the bay with his back against the revetment and his helmet in his lap. He was unhurt. He looked up at her as she came along the bay.

"Provost," he said.

"Centurion. Where's your cohort?"

"Going back." He looked down the communication trench at the long stream of soldiers. "I tried. I want you to put that in your report. I tried. I stood on the ladder and I shouted at them and I hit two of them, and they went round me." He was quiet for a moment. "Twenty years, Provost. Twenty years I've had soldiers of the *29th* in my hands, and they've never once failed to go when I sent them. And they wouldn't go. Not past the wire. Not past the thirty-one."

Quill said nothing. She was looking at the slate on the cord round her neck, at what she had written the night before. *Morale questionable. Attention fixed on Lance Seven.*

"There's a provost-optio dead in the *2nd*," Dree said. "Vey. Somebody shot him. And another in the *4th*. I heard it on the net."

"Who?"

"Nobody knows, Provost." He looked up at her with his heavy patient face. "And nobody ever will."

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 101, EVENING WATCH**

The reports came into Corvis's office all through the first cycle of the Big Push, faster than her clerks could log them, and by the evening watch she had a picture of the day that she did not like at all.

In places the Third had reached the second belt and carried its forward trench with the bayonet. Some of the regimental commanders reported good progress. Others reported failure and losses their staffs hadn't finished counting. The second wave had been ordered forward along the whole front regardless, and by the evening watch it was clear that most of the Archflamen's cohorts had held, and that the ones driven out had simply withdrawn to positions further inside the second belt and manned them. The thralls had come up out of their shelters as they always did. The Arsenal's guns had not been silenced. The belt had taken everything the Third had given it and kept it.

On Leaf 50-27, before Ceiling-Lance Seven, the *29th* Trench Regiment had broken.

Corvis read the reports from the *29th* three times. Then she went in to the Lord-General.

"The *29th*," she said. "Leaf 50-27. Sir, the attack collapsed in the first hour. The first wave went to ground under the guns and then began to come back. The provost-optios fired on the soldiers falling back. Three of them did. And in two cohorts, the soldiers shot the provost-optios." She put the slate on his desk. "Then the first wave met the second in the fire bays and they fought each other under the barrage. Within two hours, sir, the regiment was in rout."

Castorre looked at the slate for a long time without picking it up.

"The *29th*," he said. "They dug the first rifle pits on the Flats."

"Yes, sir."

"They took the outer belt. They held the Drum. They..." He stopped. "Lance Seven."

"Yes, sir. The thirty-one are still on the wire there. The *29th* has been looking at them every morning for two rotations."

He put both his hands flat on the desk. He didn't say anything for a long time. Then he picked up his stylus and wrote something on the slate and pushed it back across the desk to her.

"Send it up to the Command," he said. "Factual. All of it. Including Lance Seven." He looked up at her. "And Leda. There'll be a verdict. You know there will. When it comes, I want to read it before it goes to the regiments."

"Yes, sir."

"What else?"

"The south, sir." She laid another slate beside the first. "The *39th*, Ninth Line Phalanx, on Leaves 51 and 52. Their gunners won the counter-battery duel outright. They destroyed nearly every Arsenal gun that could reach their front, and when their infantry went over, almost nothing fired on them from behind the wire. The *39th* went straight through the forward trench of the second belt. They're still advancing. They were still advancing at the dark bell."

Castorre picked that one up. He read it twice.

"Make it the main effort," he said. "Their front. For the rest of the offensive. Put the Fourth Assault Phalanx through them."

"It'll take a cycle to bring the Fourth into position, sir. The *39th* will have to hold what they've got through tomorrow."

"Then they'll hold it."

---

The second cycle was a cycle of holding.

Every cohort that had reached the second belt tried to secure what it held, or tried again for the objective it had missed on the first cycle, and under the artillery duel the infantry fought a hundred separate battles in the craters and in fragments of captured trench. The vats at Watchpoint Hades were full. The first transfers of the offensive wouldn't return to the line for a week. By the end of the second cycle, several of the Third's colonels were asking for the offensive to be called off or reduced. At the rate they were losing soldiers, they said, their regiments would cease to exist within the week, and the vats couldn't refill them in time to matter.

Corvis took their requests in to the Lord-General one at a time. There were seven of them by the dark bell.

Castorre read every one. Then he put them aside.

"No," he said. "The offensive goes on. Every cycle we wait gives the Archflamen another cycle to dig in again. We're bleeding, Leda. But so is he. He has no vats. Every soldier he loses is gone. If we stop now, we've paid for nothing."

"Yes, sir."

"Tell the colonels there'll be more attacks tomorrow. Tell them I'm sorry."

---

On the third cycle the Fourth Assault Phalanx went in through the *39th*.

Its tanks went forward slowly across the craters on Leaves 51-41 and 52-41 and deployed into line, with the Hoplons behind them, fifty soldiers in each, and the Archflamen's positions in front of them were by then a loose line of smashed bunkers and walls and shell-holes that couldn't stand for long against the weight of an Assault Phalanx. The Hoplons dropped their ramps on them and put their breachers in. By the middle of the cycle, the Fourth's tanks and infantry were moving forward again, and on Quarterdeck Nineteen Corvis watched the little grey markers on the map table crawl across the line of the second belt and begin to move into the space beyond it, and allowed herself, for one watch, to think that it might be done.

The Archflamen's commanders had seen the danger on the first cycle and spent the second reinforcing the sector. On the third they counter-attacked.

They came with Thureos tanks and the Arsenal's own Hoplons, and new divine mortars on crawlers from the vaults, and Kyklops siege carriages, and a squadron of twelve Lance-Hunters, the long-barrelled tank destroyers the Arsenal built for the deep holds, which nobody in the Third's intelligence had known were on the Flats. As the Fourth's leading tanks went forward, the resistance in front of them grew and grew. By the middle of the afternoon watch, the Fourth had been stopped, and the Arsenal's armour was pushing it back. Its tank losses mounted through the watch until its commanders called for a defensive barrage to cover their withdrawal.

Corvis watched the grey markers crawl back across the line of the second belt, one by one, and the brown markers of the Lance-Hunters follow them.

The breakthrough had been stopped.

There was better news only at the far eastern end of the attack, where the *26th* Trench Regiment of the Fifth Line Phalanx took its first-cycle objectives on Leaf 62-38, two cycles late, and prepared to push again on the fourth with the tank companies of the *14th* Armoured. Corvis logged it. She logged everything. Then she went in to tell the Lord-General, and found him already standing at the map table, looking at the grey markers and the brown, and she saw from his face that he already knew.

---

**WATCHPOINT HADES, THE COURT OF THE RÉPERTOIRE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 104**

The verdict came three cycles after the rout, as Corvis had known it would, and Provost-Optio Severa Quill was in the room when it was read.

She had been called up the Stair as a witness on the hundred and second cycle, with the other two provost-optios of the *29th* who were still alive, and she had sat in an anteroom at Watchpoint Hades for a cycle and a half waiting to be heard. When she was heard, it took eleven minutes. She told the three officers of the Répertoire who sat at the long table what she had seen on Leaf 50-27 and what she had done, and showed them her slate, with the note she had written the night before the attack. They read it without comment. They asked her how many soldiers she had executed in the field, and she told them three, and they asked her the names, and she gave them, and one of them wrote the names down. They didn't ask her about the thirty-one, and she didn't tell them.

Then she was asked to wait again, and she waited, and on the hundred and fourth cycle she was brought back into the court to hear the verdict. The Regulations required that a provost-optio of the regiment be present when sentence was passed.

The court was a long low room in the upper tier of Watchpoint Hades, without windows, lit by pale lamps set in the ceiling. At the far end of it was the long table, and behind the table the three officers of the Répertoire in their black coats with the silver flash on their collars, and in front of it, standing in a row between two files of provosts, were the officers of the *29th*.

There were seven of them. The colonel, Oswin Marle, a lean, worn, grey-haired man of sixty who had commanded the *29th* for eleven years and had been killed on the Flats forty-four times. His second-in-command. His chief of staff. His adjutant. The three senior centurions of the regiment's forward cohorts, Dree among them. They stood in a row in their ash-stained field uniforms with their caps under their arms and their faces quite still, and they didn't look at Quill when she came in.

The senior officer of the Répertoire read the verdict. It was short.

"*The officers of the 29th Trench Regiment, being charged with the failure of their regiment to advance on Leaf 50-27 on the hundred and first cycle of Rotation Eight, and with the rout of their regiment from the field in the face of the enemy, and with their failure to prevent or punish the murder of two officers of the Command by soldiers under their command, are found guilty on all counts. The court finds that their conduct falls under CODE 31. The sentence of the court is that they be transferred by order, and that their patterns be not restored.*"

There was a silence in the court. Quill had heard verdicts read before, a great many of them. She had never heard one like that.

*That their patterns be not restored.*

She looked at Colonel Marle. He was looking at the wall above the heads of the three officers of the Répertoire, and his face hadn't changed at all. Only his hands moved. She saw them tighten on the cap under his arm, once, and then go still.

"*The 29th Trench Regiment is disbanded,*" the officer of the Répertoire read on. "*Its colours are to be burned at the depot at Kappa. Its surviving soldiers are to be transferred to the Forlorn Cohorts on the Cocytus front. The soldiers who fired upon the officers of the Command are to be identified and tried under CODE 31. The court so orders.*"

He put down the slate.

"Provost-Optio Quill," he said. "You will accompany the condemned to the vats and witness the sentence."

---

The vat hall was on the lowest tier of Watchpoint Hades, and it was silent.

Quill had never been in this part of the watchpoint. There was a single vat in the hall, set apart from the long ranks of the others, a great glass cylinder in its cradle under a single pale lamp, empty, with the fluid in it shining faintly. A vat-master stood beside it in a grey coat with her hands folded in front of her. A technician of the Répertoire sat at a console beside the vat with his hands on the keys.

The provosts brought the seven officers of the *29th* into the hall one at a time.

Quill stood against the wall and watched. That was her duty. She watched the provosts bring Colonel Marle into the hall and stand him in front of the console, and she watched the technician read his warrant aloud and key in a number, and she watched the colonel close his eyes. A provost-centurion stepped forward and shot him, once, in the back of the head, exactly as she had shot Ostrander in no-man's land three cycles before, so that his implant would fire cleanly.

She heard it fire. The faint tick.

She watched the technician's hands on the console. She watched the little bright light on his screen that was the colonel's upload go up from the pylon on the lowest tier of Watchpoint Hades toward the relay chain, and she watched the technician touch a key, and the little bright light went out.

The vat stayed empty. The fluid in it went on shining faintly under the pale lamp. Nothing came to fill it.

"*Transferred by order,*" the technician said, in a flat voice, to nobody, and wrote something on his slate. "*Pattern not restored. Null by order.*"

They brought in the next one.

Quill stood against the wall and watched all seven. It took forty minutes. When it was done, the provosts carried the bodies out, and the vat-master put her hands back in the pockets of her grey coat and walked away down the long hall between the ranks of the other vats without a word, and the technician switched off his console and followed her. Quill was left alone in the hall with the empty vat.

She stood there for a long time.

She had always believed, as every soldier of the Third believed, that the Archflamen's people were the only ones who could make a soldier null. That was what made them the enemy. That was what made the black stains on the bandages of the thirty-one on the wire so terrible. The Empire gave you back. That was its promise. Whatever happened to you on the Flats, however many times you died, you came back down the Stair. The Empire might take your memories, a little at a time. It might take your name, or your face, or the name of the gun you served on. But it gave you back.

She had just watched it decide not to.

She thought about Dree, standing in the row with his cap under his arm. *Twenty years, Provost. They've never once failed to go when I sent them.* She thought about Ostrander, crying in the ash. *They'll put us on the wire like them.* She thought about the technician's hand on the key, and the little bright light going out.

She went back up to the court and signed her deposition, and then she went to the reception halls and found the list of the soldiers who had transferred from the *29th* on Leaf 50-27, and found Ostrander's name on it. He was due to wake in four cycles. There was a note against his name in red, in the Répertoire's hand. *Executed in the field for cowardice. Court-martial pending.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she took out her stylus and wrote her own name beside it, and her rank, and the words *witness for the defence*, and went to find somebody who would tell her how to do that properly.

---

**THE DEPOT AT KAPPA. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 107**

They burned the colours of the *29th* Trench Regiment on the parade ground at Kappa at the first bell of the hundred and seventh cycle, and Jory Cade stood in the ranks of the survivors and watched it done.

There were nineteen hundred of them on the parade ground, all that was left of the *29th* that hadn't transferred or gone null on Leaf 50-27, drawn up in a hollow square in their ash-stained field uniforms without their weapons, with the provosts of the depot around them. In the middle of the square, on a brazier, the colours lay where the depot commander had put them: the regimental standard of the *29th*, crimson and gold, with its battle honours embroidered down the hoist in silver thread. Cade knew some of them. *The Drum.* *The Outer Belt.* *The Cinder Stair*, the first of all, from the first rotation, for the rifle pits. There were a great many older ones he didn't know, from fronts he'd never seen, from before he was born. Six hundred years of them.

The depot commander read the sentence. Then a provost put a torch to the brazier, and the colours caught.

They burned slowly. Cade watched the crimson go black at the edges and curl, and the silver thread of the honours glow red and then go dark, one at a time, down the hoist. *The Cinder Stair* went last. He watched it go. Nobody in the square made a sound.

Then the provosts marched them to the transports.

The sentence had been read in every regiment on the Flats the cycle before, and Cade had heard afterward how it had been taken. He heard that in the *44th*, on Leaf 52-34, Centurion Marrow had read it out to the *6th* Cohort at stand-to and then stood for a long time on the fire-step without saying anything, and that one of her troopers had asked whether it was true that the Command could do it, make a soldier null by order, and she had said yes. He heard that the provost reports from every Line Phalanx on the Flats for the rest of the rotation showed a sharp increase in soldiers found in the support lines without orders. He heard that the soldiers who had shot the provost-optios had never been identified, though the provosts had questioned every survivor of the *2nd* and the *4th* for two days, and that the matter had been closed.

The provosts had questioned him too. For four hours, in a dugout at Kappa. They had asked him what he had seen in the bay on Leaf 50-27, and who had fired, and he had told them that he had seen nothing but smoke.

He sat in the transport now, on the long bench, with the other survivors of the *2nd* round him, waiting for it to lift off for the Cocytus front, where the Empire sent its condemned. Across the hold from him, three places down, Optio Merrin Holt sat with her hands in her lap and her eyes closed.

She opened them once, when the engines started. She looked across the hold at him, for a moment, without any expression at all.

Then she closed them again, and the transport lifted, and Cade looked out of the port at the Flats falling away beneath them, grey and smoking under the ash cloud, until the ash cloud closed over them and he couldn't see anything at all.
