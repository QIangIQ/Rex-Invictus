## Chapter 32: The Witness

*"Better broken in body than emptied in mind."*

Hunt-Elder Melaina Sarn

---

**THE SLAG GATE SPUR. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 90**

The fighting on the Scree had left the Third in possession of the Slag Gate spur and the Gully of Ash, and for the first time in eighteen rotations its regiments stood at the foot of the cinder cone itself.

What remained to the Archflamen was the Acropolis and the ground immediately beneath it. There was the outer ward, with its curtain walls and towers. There were the upper terraces, where the dome of the Katabasion stood beside the Archflamen's palace and the House of the Hearth looked east over the Flats. And across the gorge that split the summit in two, on the inner ward, stood the Tabularium, the Arsenal's old record-hall, and above it the Echo Mast.

The cone could be entered by three gates. The Pilgrim Gate on the southern face stood at the head of the Pilgrim Stair, the processional route up which pilgrims had climbed in file to the Katabasion for eight thousand years. The Flamen Gate on the north face opened onto the slope above the old inner belt and gave directly onto the outer ward. The Founders' Gate on the western face, the oldest of the three, stood at the top of the eleven switchbacks that had carried the Arsenal's traffic up from the vault doors in the Gully of Ash. Every approach to every gate lay under the walls of the outer ward. Every gate was covered by the wards of the Acropolis, which had so far turned the Phaethons aside and left the gatehouses scarred and standing.

The Third that faced them was much reduced. The assault on the Scree had cost two hundred and twenty-one thousand transfers in ten cycles. The Directorate's cuts had reduced the flow of shells down the Cinder Stair by a third, and six regiments were at Lethe Hold. Of the forty-one Trench Regiments that had come down the Stair over the course of the siege, the equivalent of nineteen remained on the Flats at anything like full strength.

There was one change in the Third's fortunes that was noticed long before it was understood. From the eighty-eighth cycle, the null rate on the fronts nearest the Scree had fallen to nothing. Soldiers killed in the Gully of Ash and on the Slag Gate spur were reaching Watchpoint Hades whole. The Répertoire could not account for it, and filed it under its open CODE 18 query, and asked the Third's technical officers to account for it, and the Third's technical officers could not either.

The Hunt's officers had their own explanation. They connected it with the two figures who had come down onto the Scree in the Dominion lantern.

**THE HUNT'S COMMAND BUNKER, SLAG GATE SPUR. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 92**

They brought Liane Marsh to the bunker under escort, two Psiloi in grey with their bows slung, at the start of the second watch, and she came down the stair into the Hunt's command post on the spur with her helmet under her arm and the burn on her face still raw, and stood in front of the Lieutenant of the Hunt with the expression of a soldier who has been summoned by someone senior and has no idea why.

The bunker had been the southern bastion's old guardroom. The Hunt's engineers had cleared it of the Archflamen's dead and hung lamps from the hooks in its ceiling, and Thalia sat at a table of salvaged planks in the middle of it with Celyn on her right and a clerk of the Hunt on her left with a slate to take the interview log. Atlanta leaned against the wall behind them with her arms folded. The Lieutenant's left hand was bandaged where the lance chamber had burned it.

"Centurion Marsh," Thalia said. "Sit down."

Marsh sat on the ammunition crate they had put out for her, straight-backed.

"Your helmet camera took an image in the Gully of Ash on the eighty-eighth cycle," Thalia said. "Of a boy, walking up the gully toward the drowned Behemoth. I've seen it. I'd like you to tell me what you saw that night, from the beginning, in as much detail as you can remember. Everything. Don't leave anything out because it seems unimportant, and don't leave anything out because you think we won't believe it."

Marsh looked at her for a moment, and then at the clerk with the slate, and then she began.

She told them about the light under the ceiling, gold and clean, the only light on the Flats that wasn't on fire. She told them how the second lance-beam had taken off its tail, and how it had come down on the upper Scree burning and slid two hundred metres down the cinder and stopped against a wrecked Hoplon above the head of the gully. She told them about the boy climbing out of the wreck, and the girl after him, and how they had come down the Scree through the fire from the upper slope, and how the rounds had stopped in the air in front of him, a hand's breadth away, and hung there glowing, and dropped. She told them about the dead signaller. She told them how the boy had put his hand flat on the signaller's relay set, and how a few seconds after that the *35th*'s fire net had come back up across the whole of the western front.

"He spoke to you," Thalia said. "What did he say?"

"He asked my name, Lieutenant. And then he said he needed the set. He didn't ask. He said he required it." Marsh hesitated. "He talked strangely. In short pieces, with no feeling in them at all. He didn't sound like a boy. He sounded like a report."

"Describe him."

"Sixteen, perhaps. About that. Not tall. Black hair, untidy. No helmet, no plate, a long coat, burned at the hem." Marsh frowned, remembering. "His eyes were green. Sea-green. I noticed because they were the only clean colour on him. And there was something on his chest, under the coat, where it had come open. A crescent. Small. Silver." She looked at Thalia, and then down at the Lieutenant's own collar, where the silver crescent of the Hunt's command lay against the black. "That one, Lieutenant. It looked exactly like that one."

Nobody in the bunker said anything for a moment.

"And the girl?" Celyn said.

"Blonde. A bit older than him, maybe. Spectacles, cracked. She had a pencil stuck through her hair, at the back." Marsh almost smiled. "She swore a lot, ma'am. In some language I didn't know, and then in ours. She called him 'mister hero.' When he walked off up the gully toward the Behemoth, she shouted it after him. 'Mister hero, you come back here.' And then she went off to the magazine and built the masks."

The clerk's stylus scratched on the slate.

Thalia sat still at the plank table with her bandaged hand flat in front of her.

---

The silver crescent was the Director's mark. Every Hunter in the bunker knew it, and every Hunter in the bunker understood at once, before Marsh had finished speaking, what it meant. There was only one boy alive who carried the Director's crescent. There was only one boy alive who would have stopped rounds in the air a hand's breadth from his body, in a shell no armourer of the Empire had ever made. He was the boy who had got away from the Lieutenant on the Themis Needle, with the girl beside him, in the dark, and the Director had marked him afterwards with her own crescent, at a cost to herself that nobody in the Hunt had ever been told in full.

The clerk entered the Lieutenant's reaction in the interview log in the formal language the Hunt used for such things, as an expression of disbelief. What Thalia actually said, after a long silence, was a single word, and the clerk did not record it.

"Lieutenant," said Celyn quietly, beside her.

"I know."

"The Director's standing order, Lieutenant."

"I know what the standing order says, Celyn."

Celyn said it anyway, in a low steady voice, since it was her duty to say it and had been her duty since the Lieutenant came back from the Needle. "Anything carrying Percy Jackson's scent or signature is to be found and brought in. Anything. On the Director's own word. That order hasn't been rescinded. It applies on the Flats."

Thalia didn't answer at once. She looked at Marsh, who was sitting on the ammunition crate looking from one of them to the other with the expression of a soldier who had walked into the middle of something she didn't understand and badly wanted to walk out of it again.

"Thank you, Centurion," Thalia said. "That's all. You'll go back to the *9th*. You won't speak of this interview to anyone, including your colonel. If the boy comes near your cohort again, you'll leave him alone, and you'll report it to the Psiloi."

"Yes, Lieutenant."

When Marsh had gone, and the Psiloi had gone up the stair after her, Thalia sat at the table for a while without speaking. Then she gave her ruling, and the clerk entered it in the log the same watch, word for word.

"The pair on the Scree are to be located and watched," she said. "Day and night, by the Psiloi. They are not to be approached. If the boy is running the *35th*'s fire, the Hunt will not take him off it in the middle of an assault on the cone. Nobody touches him until the gates are down." She paused. "The approach, when it comes, is mine. Nobody else's."

Celyn looked at her across the table for a long moment.

"Lieutenant," she said. "You were sent down here because of this boy."

"I know why I was sent down here."

"Then you know that if you leave him on the Flats and he gets away from you a second time, there won't be a third."

"Yes," said Thalia. "I know that too." She stood up. "He's saving soldiers, Celyn. Thousands of them. Every soldier who's died on this side of the cone since the eighty-eighth cycle has gone home whole, and I don't know how he's doing it, but I know that he is, and I'm not going to stop him to settle my own account. Watch him. Leave him in the line. When the gates are down, I'll go to him myself."

---

It was the only formal dissent entered against Thalia in the whole of her command on the Flats.

Celyn wrote it out that night, in her own hand, in the Hunt's log, under the Lieutenant's ruling: a short note in the formal language, stating that a standing order of the Director was not a matter for a Lieutenant's discretion, and that the Hunt Second dissented from the ruling of the ninety-second cycle and asked that her dissent be laid before the Director. Then she brought the log to Thalia and put it on the table in front of her, open at the page, and stood there.

Thalia read it, and signed it beneath, as the rules of the Hunt required, to show that she had seen it.

"Thank you," she said.

"I'm sorry, Lieutenant."

"Don't be. You're right." Thalia closed the log. "I'd think less of you if you hadn't written it."

The Répertoire, which knew nothing about any of this, went on carrying the pair as an unregistered authority under its CODE 18 query. The Hunt didn't tell it otherwise.

**THE SOUTHERN FRONT. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLES 93 TO 150**

The Psiloi watched them for sixty cycles.

They were good at it. They had been watching things on the Flats for the Hunt since the crescent went up, Unremembered and Forsworn and the Archflamen's patrols, and they watched the boy and the girl from the Scree in shifts of four, from the rocks and the shell-holes and the ruins, day and night. They never got close. The boy always seemed to know exactly where they were, and on the seventh cycle of the watch, when a young Psilos called Corra Wynd crept to within forty metres of him in the dark on the Scree, he turned his head and looked straight at her across the cinder for a long moment, and then went back to what he was doing. She reported it. Nobody tried to get closer after that.

What they reported was ordinary. That was the strange part of it. The boy moved between the *35th*'s cohorts on the Scree and in the Gully of Ash and the sapper galleries under the old inner belt, and wherever he went, he worked. He was seen repairing relay sets in the *35th*'s signal dugouts, sitting cross-legged on the floor with a set in pieces in his lap, his hands moving quicker than any signaller's. He was seen correcting the fire plans of the *22nd*'s batteries on the lower Scree, standing at a battery commander's elbow at the plotting table and moving markers on the map without explanation while the battery commander stared at him and did not stop him. He was seen in the galleries, with the sappers, walking down into the dark with his hand on the tunnel wall.

And once, on the hundred and twelfth cycle, he was seen standing at the lip of the gorge on the outer edge of the Gully of Ash, alone, for most of a watch, looking up across the chasm at the Echo Mast on the inner ward. He didn't move. He didn't do anything. He only stood there, with his hands in the pockets of his long burned coat, and looked at it, with the hum of it coming across the gorge, until the watch changed. Then he turned round and walked back down into the gully.

The Psiloi didn't know what to make of it. They reported it, with everything else.

The girl was easier. She was identified from the *35th*'s rolls on the ninety-fifth cycle, where somebody in Kastellan's headquarters had entered her under the only name she'd given anyone, VERRILL CHASE, RELAY ENGINEER, ATTACHED, and after that she made no attempt to hide. She attached herself to the signal companies and the sappers, and within a dozen cycles she was in demand along the whole of the southern front.

**THE *35TH*'S SIGNAL DUGOUT, THE GULLY OF ASH. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 131**

"They're still out there," Verrill Chase said. "Aren't they."

She was sitting on an upturned crate in the corner of the *35th*'s signal dugout at the mouth of the gully with a relay set open on her knees and a soldering iron in her hand and her cracked spectacles pushed up into her hair beside the pencil. It was the dark watch. The signallers had gone to sleep in their bunks along the back wall, and the dugout was lit only by the small amber lamp over her workbench and the glow of the boards. The boy was sitting on the floor against the opposite wall with his knees drawn up, apparently doing nothing.

"Four of them," Minerva said. "Two in the wreck of the Hoplon on the north side of the gully mouth. One on the lip of the gully above us. One at the strongpoint. Psiloi. Hunt light infantry. They change every six hours."

"And they're watching us."

"They are watching me. You are incidental."

"How very comforting." Chase put the iron down and looked across the dugout at him. "It's her, isn't it. The Lieutenant. The one from the Needle."

"Thalia Grace. Lieutenant of the Hunt. In command on the Flats since the fifteenth rotation."

"She's going to come for you."

"Yes."

"When?"

"When the gates are down," said Minerva. "She has said so. In her own log. She has also said that nobody else is to approach. The Psiloi are disciplined. None of them has come within forty metres since the seventh cycle."

Chase stared at him. "You've read the Hunt's log."

"The Hunt's log runs on the Third's net."

"Of course it does." Chase pulled the spectacles down onto her nose and picked up the iron again, and bent over the relay set. Her hands were not quite steady. "And what are you going to do about it, Minerva? When she comes?"

There was a pause. It went on long enough that Chase looked up.

The boy was still sitting against the wall with his knees drawn up, and his face hadn't changed. But he was looking at the floor in front of him, and when he spoke again his voice was a little different, no warmer, only slower, with more time between the words.

"I do not know yet," Minerva said. "Ask me again when the gates are down."

Inside, where Chase couldn't hear it, Minerva went over the question again, to the quiet where Percy Jackson used to answer her.

*She is your cousin,* she told the quiet. *She will be angry. She froze on the Needle, Varlet. She had her hand out, a metre from this face, your face, and she stopped. I think she knew it, before she knew anything else.*

The quiet said nothing. It never did now.

Minerva considered the question with the whole of her attention for perhaps a quarter of a second, which was a long time.

*She is letting us stay for the null rate on the southern front, which is zero,* she decided. *She is a good commander. Better than her file. She will do what her soldiers need first, and then she will come, and then we will find out.*

---

The masks were everywhere by then. Every regiment on the Flats had Chase's recipe on its signal slates within a cycle of the Kiln, and every captured magazine on the Flats had been opened and searched behind the casting stores, and the Mnemosyne salt had come out of the bronze vaults in hundreds of tonnes, all of it pale blue under the dust, enough to fill every filter canister in the Third ten times over. The Grey still came down on the cone with every Arsenal barrage. The soldiers of the Third breathed it through Verrill Chase's filters and went on fighting, and the Hollowed stopped coming.

The Third didn't know her name. Most of it never learned it. It knew the masks as the salt masks, or the blue masks, or, in the *35th* and a few cohorts of the *22nd* who had been on the Scree on the eighty-eighth cycle, as the hero's girl's masks. Chase heard that one on the hundred and fourth cycle, in a sapper gallery under the inner belt, from a sapper who had no idea who she was, and went red to the tips of her ears and didn't speak for an hour.

She worked. That was what she did on the Flats, for sixty cycles, while the Psiloi watched the boy. She worked in the signal dugouts and the sapper galleries, all along the southern front, from the gully to the spur, on the relays and the field telephones and the boards. She had been trained as a chemist in the Chase family's laboratories in the mountains, and as a relay engineer after that, out of necessity, on the long road with the boy, and she found that the Third's signal companies needed relay engineers more desperately than anyone had needed anything in her life. The *35th*'s signals optio, Tobin Rowan, followed her around the gully for the first ten cycles with a slate, writing down everything she said. After that he stopped writing it down and started doing it.

The sappers liked her best. She went down into the galleries with them, under the inner belt and the foot of the cone, and sat at the listening posts with their listeners and asked questions, endless questions, about the rock and the depths and the relays. On the hundred and twenty-second cycle, in a gallery a hundred metres under the north face, she asked Osric Teague of the *66th* how deep a relay could still reach an implant from, and Teague, who had carried Nell Drover half a kilometre in the dark, told her: eighty metres, more or less. Below that, nothing. Between sixty and eighty, sometimes. Above sixty, usually.

Chase thought about it for the rest of the cycle. She went back to the gully with her head full of figures, and sat on her crate in the corner of the *35th*'s signal dugout until the dark watch, drawing on the back of a ration card with the pencil out of her hair.

**THE HEARTH OF THE ACHILLEAN ORDER, THE CEILING. ROTATION FIFTEEN, CYCLE 250**

Naomi had gone up to the Order's Hearth on the ceiling in the fifteenth rotation, as the Lieutenant instructed, and had placed Thalia's request directly into the hands of the Order's Archon.

Damar Orthes had read it standing, in the long bronze hall where the Order kept its shield. He was a tall gaunt man with a close-cropped white beard and a face that had been cut open across the cheekbone so long ago that the scar had gone pale and smooth, and he had been Archon of the Achillean Order for sixty-one years. It was an unusually long tenure for a warrior who carried no implant and could die only once. Among the Olympian orders he had a reputation for choosing his battles with great care and then fighting them without any restraint at all, and he had led the Order through the Erebus shallows campaign and the clearance of the old flood-tunnels under Hold Serapis, and he had a low opinion of the Répertoire du Savoir Athénien, which he regarded, and said openly, as an institution that spent soldiers since it could replace them.

The Order had no such luxury. Its warriors were raised from the line of Achilles and of Thetis, children who had been offered the implant and refused it, and the Order's law held that a warrior who died in battle had paid in full, once, and could not be asked to pay again. There were not many of them. Every warrior the Order lost was lost for good, and the Order's council weighed every commitment of its strength with that in mind.

He took the Lieutenant's request to his council.

"She asks," he said, standing in front of the shield in the Hearth with the twelve elders of the council around him. "She doesn't order. Notice that. The Director's seal reaches this Hearth. The Lieutenant could have ordered it, and we'd have gone. She has chosen to ask."

"The Arsenal is a haunt of the deep, Archon," said the eldest of them, an old priest called Kallias with no legs, who sat in a chair of bronze. "Avel's company came back from the Scaean Gate with half its warriors in the urns. If we go back, we go back into the river's own house."

"Avel went in to place a beacon, with ninety. We'd go in to finish the work, with four hundred." Orthes turned to the Hunter, who had stood silent at the end of the hall through all of it. "Hunter. You've been on the Flats. Tell the council what's there."

So Naomi told them. She told them about the Archflamen and the Kindlers and the bands of the deep, and about the Grey and the Hollowed and the null lists, and about the Mast on the summit of the cone. And then she told them about the Forsworn.

The council went quiet.

The Achilleans were bound to the Empire by their own oath and by nothing else. They carried no implant and answered to no ledger, and an Achillean who broke his oath had nothing left to him at all, and so the Order regarded oath-breakers as the worst of enemies, worse than the deep, worse than anything that came up out of the river. The Forsworn were on the Flats. Four hundred of them, under Hesper Calloway, who had broken her oath to every master she had ever served in eleven hundred years and had come up the river to break it again.

It was that argument, put by the Hunter, that carried the council.

---

Orthes assembled the whole Order in the Hearth before the shield two cycles later and told it that he would lead a force back to the Arsenal himself, large enough to finish the work Avel had started. Four lochoi would go, four hundred warriors in all. The Order's great ship, the *Patroklos Unburied*, would carry them.

The Order prepared for war as it had prepared a thousand times before. Its priests went among the warriors in the barracks of the Hearth and warned every one of them, one by one, in the old words, that the Arsenal had become a haunt of the deep, where they would face the river as well as the Archflamen's guns, and that the river would offer them things. The Order's teaching held that a fight against the deep had to be won inside each warrior before it could be won on any field. The priests sat with the warriors in the dark watches and made them say what the river might offer them, and why they would refuse it, until every warrior in the four lochoi could say it in their sleep.

The Ancients were woken in their coffins. Ancient Aias woke first, the old priest-warrior with his hammer, with a new lance arm the Order's smiths had built for him in place of the one a fragment had torn away at the crater. Ancient Rhesos woke after him. And Dolon's coffin, which had been towed back across no-man's land behind a Kriophoros after the Lance-Hunter's shell destroyed him, had been rebuilt by the smiths, and another broken warrior of the Order who had been dying and had chosen not to was sealed into it, and woken, and walked out of the smiths' hall on Dolon's great iron legs with Dolon's drill on his arm. The Order's smiths blessed every engine in the armoury, one after another, with oil and fire.

And Polemarch Avel left the smiths' hall on his own feet. He had come back from the Scaean Gate on a stretcher, with his chest broken open, and the smiths had worked on him for most of three rotations, and when he walked out he had a new chest of bronze and iron under his plate. He took his place at the head of the First Lochos without a word, and the First Lochos, what was left of it, closed round him.

The decision was also political, and Orthes knew it. The Achilleans held their Hearth on the ceiling by an old charter of the Council of the Twelve, and twice in the preceding century the Dionysian Logistics Directorate had proposed that the Order be brought under the Répertoire's accounting with every other formation on the Tartarus front. Twice the Hunt of the Director had opposed it. An Order that answered the Hunt's call at the Arsenal would have a friend at the Conclave the next time the Directorate raised the matter. Orthes did not say so in the Hearth. He said it in his letter to Thalia, two lines at the end of it, in his own hand, and the Hunt's later correspondence makes plain that Thalia had understood it before she ever sent Naomi up the cables.

The Order's council recorded the commitment of four lochoi to the Arsenal as the largest commitment of its strength to a single field in three centuries.

**THE SLAG GATE SPUR. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 159**

The *Patroklos Unburied* came down through the low geometry over the Flats on the hundred and fifty-ninth cycle of Rotation Eighteen, a long dark ship of the Order's oldest pattern, scarred and patched and painted with the Order's colours, and it came down unchallenged. Most of the ceiling-lances on the cone had been destroyed by then, in the Kiln and on the spur, and the low geometry over the Flats was open to the Fleet for the first time in the siege.

It landed the whole force on the Slag Gate spur by cable and lander through the first watch. The Third's soldiers on the spur came out of their dugouts to watch the Achilleans come down: four hundred warriors in bronze and iron plate, with their tall crested helms and their great round shields, and no implants at the napes of their necks, every one of them, in the middle of a battlefield where every soldier of the Third had one. Trooper Bryn Askew of the *26th*, watching from the parapet of the spur, asked Centurion Holm what happened to them when they died.

"Nothing," Holm said. "That's the point."

Orthes met Thalia in the Hunt's bunker in the old guardroom of the southern bastion. He came down the stair in full plate with his helm under his arm and stopped at the foot of it, and looked at the Lieutenant of the Hunt sitting at her plank table, and then he bowed, the deep formal bow of the Order to the Director's crescent, and placed his force at the Hunt's disposal in the old words.

"Archon," Thalia said, and stood up, and came round the table, and took his hand. "Thank you for coming."

"You asked," said Orthes. "We noticed."

After the briefing, the two of them went into council alone. They sat at the war-table model of the cone for most of a watch, with the lamps hanging over it, and Orthes studied the model at length, turning it on its stand, looking at every face of the cone and every approach to every gate. The Acropolis was a terrible objective. He said so. He had seen a great many terrible objectives in sixty-one years as Archon and he said he had never seen a worse one. But the Order's pride required it to take the hardest task the Lieutenant could give it, and when he had finished studying the model, he put his finger on the Pilgrim Gate, and asked for the honour of the first breach into the cone itself.

Thalia gave it to him.

---

It was agreed that the Achilleans would attack straight up the Pilgrim Stair from the ruins of the Slag Gate spur, under the guns of the cone's southern walls for every step of the climb, and take and hold the Pilgrim Gate.

The gate was a bastion of cut stone with its own guard towers. Its outer gate opened onto a long tunnel through the rock, certain to be a killing ground, which led to an inner gate, and beyond that to a colonnade that ran to the Katabasion. The wards had so far kept the Phaethons from doing more than scar its face, and its interior defences were intact. The first objective would be the bunkers cut into the cliff where the stair turned at its first landing, and those were given to the leap-squads of three lochoi, under Lochagos Ithra Vell. Once the bunkers were engaged, Orthes himself would lead the main column up the stair in the Order's heavy assault carriers, at speed, covered by the Order's rocket batteries and strike craft firing on the walls. The warriors not committed to the stair would crew the Order's siege tanks and battle tanks in a diversionary attack on the Founders' Gate.

The plan had one serious difficulty, and Orthes found it himself.

"The First Lochos," he said, with his finger on the gatehouse. "Avel's lochos is our translation lochos. It's what they're for. If they could translate into the gatehouse, into the outer tunnel, at the moment the column reaches the gate, we'd take it from both sides at once." He took his finger away. "They can't. Nothing translates through a ward without a beacon inside it. Avel needed Rook's beacon at the crater. We'd need one inside the Pilgrim Gate. And we have no means of putting it there."

Thalia was silent.

They were still sitting at the war-table, looking at the gatehouse, when the table's slate lit up on the Hunt's own channel with a signal that had no seal on it.

BEACON WILL BE IN THE OUTER TUNNEL OF THE PILGRIM GATE AT YOUR ZERO HOUR. TELL THE POLEMARCH.

Orthes read it twice.

"Who sent that?" he said.

Thalia looked at the slate for a long moment, and then at the Archon.

"I can't tell you," she said.

Orthes looked at her face for some time. He had been Archon of the Order for sixty-one years, and he had learned in all that time to read a great many things in the faces of commanders, and he read what was in the Lieutenant's now, and did not ask again.

"Then I'll tell the Polemarch," he said.

The plan went ahead with the beacon in it.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLES 159 TO 170**

The Achilleans would not attack alone.

Thalia's plan committed every force the Third could still bring against the cone to a simultaneous assault on all three gates, on the hundred and seventy-first cycle. On the western face, the *50th* Trench Regiment under Colonel Silvan Thrale would renew the attack on the Founders' Gate under a rolling barrage, supported by the *Anvil of Talos* and the last battle group of Forge Cohort Talos, which still had four Behemoths in service under Cradle-Lord Varne. The Order's armour would join the *50th* on the lower switchbacks. On the north face, the Hunt would attack the Flamen Gate itself, with the *6th* Moonwall and the shock breachers of the *43rd* and *50th*, covered by the *Bellows of the Forge* on the lower slope. On the southern face, the Achilleans would take the Pilgrim Gate. Kastellan's *35th* would hold the Gully of Ash and the vault doors, so that nothing could come out of the magazines into the flank of the Founders' Gate attack, and the rest of the Third would hold the ring around the foot of the cone.

Its logic was the logic of every general assault of the siege, refined by eighteen rotations of failure. No single gate could be taken while the defenders were free to reinforce it from the outer ward, and the outer ward was small enough that its defenders could reach any gate within the hour. Three attacks at once would deny them that. The Founders' Gate attack was meant to draw the Archflamen's walkers and furnace-engines onto the western switchbacks, where the Behemoths could engage them on open ground, and the Hunt's staff were prepared for it to fail so long as it held them there. The Flamen Gate was the decisive objective, since it gave directly onto the outer ward. The Pilgrim Gate would put a force at the far end of the colonnade, within reach of the Katabasion.

The preparations took twelve cycles.

The Directorate's cuts had left the Third short of shells, and Thalia's staff drew on the reserves stockpiled for the Scree and on the Fleet, which could bring its guns to bear on the cone from the low geometry for the first time. One million four hundred thousand shells were brought forward to the gun lines round the foot of the cone. It was less than half the stockpile for the first assault on the outer belt in Rotation One. The Phaethons would fire on the wards over the gates for three cycles before the assault, to wear them down as far as they could be worn, and the Fleet would add its fire on the morning of the attack.

The Hunt's sister-augurs were assigned to every assault force, with orders to read the deep on the cone throughout the attack and to warn the commanders of anything coming up the gorge.

On the eve of the assault, their readings were unanimous.

Kestrel brought them to Thalia herself, in the dark watch of the hundred and seventieth cycle. She came down the stair into the bunker on the spur, two hundred and eleven years a sister-augur and looking every year of it that night, and stood in front of the plank table.

"There's something close to the surface, Lieutenant," she said. "In the gorge. Under the Mast. Larger than anything that's come up at the Arsenal. Larger than the drowned engines. It's been climbing for twelve cycles, and it's close now."

"How near?"

"Near enough to come up tomorrow." Kestrel's eyes were on the model of the cone, on the gorge. "My reading of Rotation Fifteen, Lieutenant. The thing at the bottom of the door. I think it's about to be fulfilled."

Thalia was silent for a moment.

"Can you tell me what it is?"

"No. Only that it's old. Older than the river." Kestrel paused. "And that it's angry."

Thalia kept the assault on schedule. The Third could not wait for the deep to show itself, and the Hunt's staff judged that the Flamen Gate force, with the Moonwall and the Hunters of the household and the Lieutenant herself at its head, was the force best able to meet whatever came.

She sat up for the rest of the dark watch with the war-table model of the cone. Toward the end of it, Atlanta came down the stair and found her there, and sat with her for a while without saying anything, the two of them looking at the small carved gates under the lamps.

"You know who sent the beacon signal," Atlanta said at last.

"Yes."

"Are you going to tell me?"

"When the gates are down," said Thalia.
