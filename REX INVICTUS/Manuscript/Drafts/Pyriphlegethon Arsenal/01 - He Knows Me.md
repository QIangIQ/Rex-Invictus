# The Pyriphlegethon Arsenal

## Chapter 1: He Knows Me

*"He went down into the dark alone. He knows your name. When he comes back up the road you are walking, the deep will be emptied and the vats will close."*

*Archflamen Sabinus Orrhae, sermon at Lethe Hold, Rotation Minus Four*

---

**LETHE HOLD, SECTOR 74B. HERMESEAN HOLD 17. ROTATION MINUS FOUR**

The fog had been sitting on the Lethe for six cycles straight, and Commander Thaleia had started to take it personally.

"Crawlers on the upper road," Ilon said from the cognition chair. He had his gear pushed up on his forehead and a ration bar hanging out of his mouth like a cigar. "Eleven of them. Big ones. Painted white, if you can believe it."

"Painted white." Thaleia didn't look up from the overlay. "In Tartarus."

"Painted white with gold trim. There's a banner on the lead one. Hold on, I'm zooming." He chewed. "It's a lamp. A big gold lamp on a white field. And under it, in very large letters, it says 'HE KNOWS YOU.'"

That got her to look up.

The upper road came down the strata from the Acheron stairs in a long scar of cut basalt, and for most of the four thousand cycles she had held Sector 74B, the only things that used it were ration haulers, relief cohorts and the occasional Enforcement transport with its windows blacked out. The visor feed showed eleven tall crawlers grinding down it in single file under the green glow of the hold lamps, each one as white as a tooth, and behind them a column of people on foot that went back up the switchbacks further than the feed could resolve.

"Who's walking?" she asked.

"Everybody." Ilon flicked the feed to infrared. "Troopers, mostly. Some ore-lift thralls. Couple of hundred pilgrims in robes, which, honestly, good for them, because I wouldn't walk that road in plate. And there are armed escorts on the flanks. Women. Short spears, lamps on poles." He squinted. "Vestals? Those look like Vestals."

"The Archflamen," Thaleia said.

"The what now?"

"The new Archflamen of the theatre. College sent a notice six rotations back. Sabinus Orrhae. Apollo on one side, Hestia on the other. He's touring." She brought the notice up from the Hold's archive and slid it across to his pane. "Confirmation of Flamens, blessing of consecrated ordnance, an account of the faith of twenty-seven million soldiers for the College. That kind of thing."

Ilon read it, then read the column of people again, then read the notice a second time with his ration bar held very still.

"That's not a tour," he said. "That's a parade."

The overlay chimed. Thaleia glanced down at the bottom pane, where AtRep's feed scrolled in its usual unhurried amber.

*CODE 5 – Signum Hostilis Visus. Sector 74B, upper road. Unregistered mass movement, est. 1,400.*

*Verdict: Pending.*

"It thinks they're hostile," Ilon said, delighted. "Athena thinks the priest is an invasion."

"Athena thinks everything is an invasion until somebody tells her otherwise." Thaleia keyed the field log, typed for a second, and sent it.

*CODE 18 – Speculum Falsum. Sector 74B, upper road. Movement identified: Archflamen's progress, College notice ref. attached. False positive confirmed. T., Commander.*

AtRep took the entry without comment, which was the closest it came to gratitude. The amber line on the bottom pane cleared itself and was replaced by a ration-allocation conflict from somewhere up near Depth 4.

"You're going to go meet him, aren't you," Ilon said.

"I'm the commander of the sector, Ilon. The Archflamen of the theatre is coming down my road. Yes, I'm going to go meet him."

"Can I come?"

"No."

"Can I watch on the feed?"

"You're going to anyway."

He grinned at her and settled back into the chair with the ration bar. "Wear the good cloak," he said. "The one without the Lethe stains."

---

**LETHE HOLD, THE VAT-RITE HALL**

She wore the good cloak.

The vat-rite hall of Hermesean Hold 17 was a long low chamber cut into the strata above the recovery vats, with benches for four hundred and an altar of grey stone at the far end where the Hold's Flamen said the rite over every soldier who came back down the cables from Watchpoint Hades. On an ordinary cycle the Flamen said it to perhaps thirty people. The returned soldiers came because they had to, and stood in the back with their new skin still pink and their eyes still adjusting to the hold lamps, and left before he had finished the second verse.

The hall was full.

Thaleia had to stand in the doorway. Troopers she knew by name were packed onto the benches shoulder to shoulder in their trench grey, and more of them stood along the walls, and more still sat in the aisles with their rifles across their knees. The pilgrims from the road had filled the space under the altar. Two of the white-robed Vestals stood at the altar's corners with their spears grounded and their lamps held high, and the light from the lamps was a steady gold that did nothing at all in the draught from the vents.

The Archflamen was not what she had expected.

He was forty-one, according to the College's notice, but he looked younger. He was a slight man, narrow in the shoulders, with a broad pleasant face and close-cut brown hair already going grey above the ears. He wore the white vestments of his office without the gold collar, and he had taken off his sandals, which meant he was standing barefoot on the cold stone of a Tartarean hold, which Thaleia had never once seen a priest do. He was not preaching yet. He was walking down the centre aisle, very slowly, stopping at every row.

"What's your name?" he asked a trooper on the end of a bench.

The trooper was a kid from the *42nd* Draft, Dorrin something, eighteen or nineteen, with a burn scar across the bridge of his nose from a Phlegethon vent he had stood too close to in his first rotation. He went red to the ears.

"Dorrin Pask, Your Eminence."

"Dorrin Pask." The Archflamen put a hand on his shoulder. "How many times, Dorrin?"

The kid blinked. "Sir?"

"How many times have you gone up?"

"Oh." Dorrin swallowed. "Six. Six times, Your Eminence."

"Six." The Archflamen nodded as if this were important information that he intended to keep. "Do you remember them?"

"Yes, sir."

"All six?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then you know more about what the Imperator did than anyone in the College," the Archflamen said, and moved on to the next bench.

Thaleia watched him do it for the whole length of the hall. He asked every name. He asked every count. Some of the troopers answered with a number and some of them couldn't, and when they couldn't he just nodded and squeezed their shoulders and said, "That's all right. He can," and moved on. By the time he reached the altar the hall had gone quiet in a way she had never heard a room full of soldiers go quiet, and the Vestals had lowered their lamps a little so that the light fell on him and not on the stone.

He didn't use the liturgy.

"Before any of you were born," the Archflamen said, "before your mothers were born, before the Empire had a name for this place, a man went down into it alone."

His voice wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. The vents had picked that moment to go quiet too.

"You know his name. You swear by it every time you go up. He went down past the Lethe and past the Styx and past the fire. He went down to the very bottom of the dark, where nothing had ever gone and come back, and he came back. Every one of you who has died in this place and woken in the vats has walked his road. Some of you have walked it six times." He found Dorrin Pask in the third row and smiled at him. "Some of you have walked it more times than you can count. That isn't a curse. That is the Imperator's road. He made it. He knows every step of it."

Somebody near the wall said, "Ave," very quietly, as if they weren't sure they were allowed.

"He knows every one of you," the Archflamen said. "He knows your name. He knows your count. And when he comes back up the road you are walking, the deep will be emptied and the vats will close, and you will not have to walk it ever again."

He let that sit.

Then he lifted both hands, palms out, and began the actual rite, the old one, the one the Hold's Flamen said thirty times a cycle to thirty people who didn't listen. Thaleia knew it by heart. Everyone in the hall knew it by heart. It was the short dry text the College had been using for twelve hundred years, and it ended with the Flamen asking whether the returned soldier knew where they were, and the soldier answering with their sector and their unit and the Imperator's name.

The Archflamen reached the question.

"Do you know where you are?" he asked them.

Four hundred soldiers of Sector 74B answered him together.

"He knows me."

It went through Thaleia like cold water. It wasn't the response. It wasn't any response the College had ever approved. She knew perfectly well that he hadn't taught it to them, because he hadn't said those words once in the whole sermon, and she had been listening. They had made it up. They had made it up just now, out of what he had said, all four hundred of them at once, and they had said it as though it were the oldest thing they knew.

The Archflamen bowed his head.

"Yes," he said. "He does."

---

Afterward there was a reception in the Hold's officers' mess, which was a generous name for a room with a table in it, and Thaleia found herself standing beside the Archflamen with a cup of something hot that the Vestals had brought with them and that tasted of honey and smoke.

"It's a Hestian infusion," he said, when he saw her looking at it. "The Virgo Maxima sends it down with them. I'm told it keeps the cold out."

"Does it?"

"No," he said cheerfully. "But it's warm while you're drinking it, and that's something."

She liked him. That was the problem, she would decide later. She liked him immediately and completely, the same way the four hundred in the hall had liked him, and she was a commander of the Lethe Hold with four thousand cycles on the river, and she didn't like anybody.

"Your Eminence," she said. "The response. In the hall."

"Yes." He was quiet for a moment. "That's been happening."

"Since when?"

"Since Hold Kestrel. Three holds back." He turned the cup in his hands. "The first time, I corrected them. I told them the proper response. They said it, and they meant it, and then the next hold said 'He knows me' again without anyone telling them to, and the hold after that. I stopped correcting them." He looked at her, and his eyes were brown and steady and not at all the eyes of a man who thought he was doing anything wrong. "Should I have kept correcting them, Commander?"

"The College won't like it."

"The College hasn't been below Depth 3 in two centuries," he said, without heat. "The College counts the rites and sends me letters about attendance. Your soldiers know where they are. They've known for longer than the College has had a building. I don't think I'm going to take that away from them for the sake of a liturgy."

She didn't have an answer to that.

"Thirty-eight of my people asked me tonight if they could transfer to your escort," she said instead. "Did you know that?"

"I didn't." He looked genuinely troubled. "I don't take soldiers from the holds. I don't have the authority. My escort is the Vestals and the household. The rest of them..." He made a small, helpless gesture toward the window, where the white crawlers stood under the hold lamps and the people who had walked behind them were already bedding down along the road in the fog. "The rest of them just come."

"They're deserting to come."

"Then send them back," he said. "Please. I mean it. Send them back, Commander. I'll tell them myself to go back if you want me to."

He meant it. She was almost sure he meant it.

A man came in from the corridor while they were talking. He was tall, older than the Archflamen by a decade, in a plain grey coat with the household's lamp stitched small on the collar, and he went straight to the Archflamen's elbow and murmured something Thaleia didn't catch. The Archflamen nodded.

"Forgive me," he said to her. "The household. There's always something." He put his empty cup down on the table and took her hand, and his grip was dry and firm. "Commander Thaleia. Thank you for your hall. Thank you for your soldiers. I'll pray for the Lethe Hold every night that I'm in the deep."

"Your Eminence."

He went. The man in grey held the door for him and looked back at her once, a long flat look, before he let it close.

Thaleia went back up to the observation bunker. Ilon was still in the cognition chair, with the feed of the vat-rite hall frozen on the moment the four hundred had answered.

"So," he said. "Do I still log it as a false positive?"

She looked at the frozen feed for a long time.

"Yes," she said. "Leave it."

---

**HOLD SERAPIS, DEPTH 31. ROTATION MINUS THREE**

The Haruspex of the Phlegethon holds came to the household at Hold Serapis on the ninetieth cycle of the second rotation of the tour, and the Archflamen would remember the meeting for the rest of his life, which was not as long as he expected.

Hold Serapis was where his predecessor had died. Helvius Strabo had lived for six hundred and four Gaian years, carried most of that distance by the physicians of the Apollonian wing, and had spent the last century of it in a chair by the window of the Archflamen's rooms at Serapis looking down at the Acheron and refusing to go any deeper. The rooms still smelled of him. Orrhae had them aired and found that it didn't help.

"He never went below thirty-one," he said, standing at the window. "Did you know that? Fifty-eight years as Archflamen of this theatre, and he never once went below this window."

"Strabo was an old man who liked his chair," said the Haruspex.

His name was Ilan Dace. He was thirty, a legacy of Hecate through his mother and Hermes through his father, and he had been Haruspex of the Phlegethon holds for six years, which meant he read the omens for the commanders of the holds between Depth 38 and Depth 44 and told them, as far as anyone could tell, more or less what they wanted to hear. He had come up to Serapis on his own account. Nobody had sent for him. He had a long, plain, clever face and a soft voice and he sat in Strabo's old chair by the window as if he had been sitting in it for years.

"You don't like chairs?" Orrhae asked him.

"I don't like being in one place, Your Eminence. The deep holds are dying for want of someone who'll go and stand in them. The College sends Flamens down to the Phlegethon who can't name the river's four bends. They don't know which vents breathe on which cycle. They don't know that the Fire Steps garrison at Depth 42 has been running on half rations since the last reformation peak, because nobody above Depth 30 has bothered to ask." He smiled a little. "I know. I've been asking for six years. I'd like someone to listen."

"And you think I'll listen?"

"I think you've already started," Dace said. "I was at Hold Kestrel. I heard what the soldiers said to you in the hall."

Orrhae turned from the window.

"You came all the way up from the Phlegethon for that?"

"I came up from the Phlegethon for you." Dace held his gaze. "Your Eminence, I've read omens for twelve hold commanders. Most of them are good soldiers. All of them are tired. None of them believe in anything anymore except the next ration hauler and the next AtRep verdict. You walked into a hall on the Lethe and four hundred soldiers told you the Imperator knew their names. You didn't tell them that. They told you. Do you know how long it's been since a soldier of this theatre said anything to a priest that wasn't in the liturgy?"

"I don't."

"Neither does the College," said Dace. "That's my point."

---

He was useful.

That was what Orrhae would say, afterward, to anyone who asked why the Haruspex had joined the household. Dace was the most practical man he had ever met. He knew which holds had food and which didn't, which commanders would receive an Archflamen and which would lock their gates, which roads between the holds were safe and which had been eaten by the Phlegethon in the last peak. He knew the names of hold commanders' children. He knew where the old shrines were in the deep holds, the forgotten ones from the first centuries of the war, with soldiers' names scratched into the walls in alphabets the College didn't teach anymore. He took the Archflamen to them one by one. They prayed in them together.

He also drank.

Orrhae found out about that on the hundred and twelfth cycle, by accident, when he came into the household's map room early and found Dace sitting alone at the table with a small grey glass vial in front of him and a dropper in his hand.

"Medicine," Dace said, without looking up. He put three drops of the vial into a cup of water, very carefully, and drank it. "For the omens. Reading the deep is hard on the head."

"What is it?"

"River water." Dace stoppered the vial and put it back in his coat. "Very little of it. It helps me forget what I'm not supposed to see, so I can see the rest."

Orrhae didn't ask which river. He thought about it once, briefly, a few cycles later, and decided that a Haruspex of the Phlegethon holds probably knew what he was doing with the waters of the deep better than a priest from the ceiling relays did.

**THE PROGRESS, DEPTH 34 TO DEPTH 37. ROTATION MINUS THREE TO ROTATION MINUS TWO**

The thousand became two thousand on the Acheron stairs and five thousand by the time the crawlers reached the Fire Steps.

Nobody organised it. That was what made it so hard to stop. A hold commander would refuse a transfer request, and the trooper would put in for leave, and the leave would be refused, and the trooper would simply be gone from the morning roll with their rifle and their kit and their next ration card, and three cycles later the household's quartermasters would find them sitting at the back of the column on the road with forty other people who had done exactly the same thing. Ore-lift thralls came off their shifts and walked into the column and didn't go back. Pilgrims came down from the ceiling worlds by the hundred, on the College's own pilgrim permits, to see the man the letters were talking about.

The College was thrilled.

"Attendance at the vat-rite in every hold you have visited has increased by between ninety and one hundred and sixty percent," the Archflamen read aloud, in the back of his crawler, from a letter that had come down the cables that morning. "The College offers its thanks to the Archflamen and to the Imperator, and asks that the Archflamen continue in his good work, and encloses a list of twelve additional Flamens of the second rank who will join the progress at Hold Thessaly to assist with the confirmations." He put the letter down. "They're sending me more priests."

"They're sending you twelve men who'll write to them every cycle about what you're doing," said Dace, who was sitting across from him with a slate on his knee. "I'd want the same, in their place."

"You think they're spies?"

"I think the College is a large old institution that's very pleased with you right now," Dace said. "Large old institutions stay pleased for exactly as long as you keep doing what they expected. The day you do something they didn't expect, those twelve men will tell them about it before you've finished doing it."

The crawler lurched over a bad stretch of the road. Outside, through the slit window, the column wound back down the Fire Steps in a long dark line under the red glow from the river below. There were lamps all along it. Somebody in the column had started carrying lamps a rotation back, small tin ones, copies of the Vestals' gold lamps, and now everybody carried one.

"We need someone to keep order in that," Dace said, following the Archflamen's gaze. "There were two knife fights last night. One of them was over a lamp."

"The Vestals keep order."

"The Vestals keep you safe, Your Eminence. There are forty of them. There are eleven thousand people on that road." He tapped the slate. "I've drawn up a list. Old soldiers, mostly. Hold veterans who came to the column with their kit. Good people. Steady. They could carry rods and wear a sash so the pilgrims know who they are."

"Lictors," said Orrhae.

"If you like the word."

"I don't, especially." He watched the lamps. "But you're right. Somebody's going to get killed in that column if nobody's watching it. Fine. Give them the sashes. What colour?"

"Flame orange," said Dace. "For the lamps."

---

The Lictors of the Flame got their sashes on the hundred and ninetieth cycle of Rotation Minus Two, and within a month the column had a quartermaster's office, a ration roll, a burial detail and a court. The court was three of the Lictors and Dace, and it sat in a tent at the tail of the column every evening and heard complaints, and its punishments were posted on a board outside the tent the next morning for everyone to read.

Orrhae read the board once, early on. It said that a pilgrim named Ahn Tovald had been found guilty of stealing a lamp and had been given forty lashes and his lamp back. He thought forty was a lot. He said so to Dace.

"It's a lot," Dace agreed. "It was also his third lamp. The first two belonged to other people." He shrugged. "They'll stop stealing lamps now."

They did.

The inner circle came later, and more quietly. Orrhae never quite noticed it being built. One cycle he realised that the people who brought him his meals and his letters and his reports were always the same people, perhaps forty of them, and that he knew all their names, and that he couldn't remember the last time somebody he didn't know had been allowed into his crawler. He asked Dace about it.

"They've sworn to you," Dace said.

"Sworn what?"

"To keep you safe. To serve the progress. To serve the Imperator through you." Dace was unrolling a map of the Phlegethon holds across the table in the household's map room, weighting its corners with lamps. "They went down to the Styx at Depth 44 three cycles ago, while you were confirming the Flamens at Hold Thessaly, and they swore it on the river."

The Archflamen sat down slowly.

"On the Styx," he said.

"It's the oldest oath there is."

"I know what it is, Ilan. I was a Flamen for twenty-two years. I've buried soldiers who broke Styx oaths. I've seen what's left of them." He was very pale. "Why would anyone swear something like that to me?"

"Because they believe in you," said Dace, and he said it very gently. "Your Eminence. Sabinus. They believe in you. They believe you're the Imperator's voice in the deep. And there are people above Depth 30 who'll try to stop you. There are people in the College who are already writing letters about what you preach. There are officers in the Command who look at that column on the road and see eleven thousand troopers missing from their roll calls. There's an engine at Watchpoint Hades that counts every soul in this theatre as a line in a ledger. If those people decide that you're a problem, the forty who've sworn to you on the Styx are the only forty people in Tartarus you'll be able to trust."

"You haven't sworn."

"I have," said Dace. "I swore first."

---

The Archflamen started talking about the war of faith around then.

He didn't call it that, at first. He talked about it in the crawler at night, to Dace and to whoever of the forty happened to be bringing his tea, as a thing that would have to happen eventually, in some distant rotation when he was old. The College would never wake up on its own. The Command would never stop spending soldiers as ammunition, and the Répertoire would never stop pricing them. Somebody would have to show them all, from the bottom up, what the soldiers of the deep actually knew. Somebody would have to stand in the deep and refuse to leave it.

"Where?" Dace asked him, one night on the road to Depth 38.

"Where what?"

"Where would you stand?"

Orrhae looked out of the slit window at the lamps.

"Somewhere they couldn't ignore," he said. "Somewhere the College would have to come down to. Somewhere the Imperator himself would recognise." He laughed a little. "It's a stupid conversation, Ilan. I'm a priest. I don't have a hold. I don't have an army. I have eleven thousand people with tin lamps and forty Vestals with spears."

"You have a charter," said Dace.

"What charter?"

Dace reached into his coat and took out a slate, and on the slate was a document Orrhae had never seen before, very old, in the formal Latin of the early theatre, under a seal he didn't recognise until he looked closer and saw the bronze hammer of the Hephaestean orders crossed with the lamp of the College.

"The charter of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal," Dace said. "Granted eight thousand years ago, jointly, by the Forge and the College, when the Arsenal was built over the Katabasion. Nobody's read it in three thousand years." He put his finger on a clause near the bottom. "Here. In time of peril, the Arsenal may raise a militia of its own, under the authority of the Archflamen of the theatre, for the defence of the bones of the First Descent."

The Archflamen read it twice.

"The Katabasion," he said.

"The tomb of the first legion that ever went down," said Dace. "The holiest shrine in Tartarus. No Archflamen has visited it in three hundred years." He rolled up the slate and put it away. "It's at Depth 40. We'll be passing within a cycle of it."

---

**THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL, DEPTH 40. ROTATION MINUS TWO, CYCLE 301**

Corran Ludd, Steward of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal, had been told the Archflamen was coming nine cycles in advance, and had spent all nine of them hoping somebody would tell him the Archflamen wasn't coming after all.

"Eleven thousand," he said, for the fourth time, in the gatehouse office at the top of the Pilgrim Stair. "Eleven thousand extra mouths on my spur. Does anyone at the College understand what my ration draw looks like? Does the Répertoire? I've got four million thralls on the casting lines and a garrison of a hundred and forty thousand and every single one of them eats, and now I've got a priest bringing eleven thousand pilgrims and deserters down the Pilgrim Stair to pray at a pile of old bones."

"The Archflamen has written to say that the progress will be self-supporting," said his deputy, a thin Hephaestean named Brannoch Tey who had a habit of agreeing with whoever had spoken last.

"The progress has eaten its way through nine holds, Tey. I've read the reports. It isn't self-supporting. It's a locust swarm with lamps." Ludd scrubbed a hand over his face. He was a broad, short, heavy man, a son of Hephaestus with arms like casting ladles and a burn scar that ran from his left ear to his collarbone, and he had run the Arsenal's casting lines for ninety-one years without once letting a priest tell him how. "Fine. Fine. Open the Pilgrim Gate. Put them on the eastern terrace by the House of the Hearth. Don't let any of them near the heat-wells."

"Yes, Steward."

"And Tey."

"Steward?"

"If anybody in that column so much as looks at a casting line, I want them on the next ore-lift up to the ceiling with a note pinned to their chest."

---

The Pyriphlegethon Arsenal stood on a spur of black basalt over the bend of the Phlegethon at Depth 40, and it was the oldest working forge-hold in Tartarus. It was eight thousand years old. Its casting lines drew fire straight out of the river through ceramic heat-wells sunk four hundred metres into the spur, and used it to cast and quench the consecrated ordnance that the whole theatre fired. Eighty-one percent of the lineage-keyed divine mortar shells in Tartarus came out of the Arsenal. So did most of the aedificant builder-shells. So, for that matter, did the armour plate for half the *3rd* Siege Army's tanks, the breech blocks for every Phaethon heavy mortar on the Cinder Flats and a substantial fraction of the theatre's sealant paste, rifle barrels and ration tins.

Under all that, it was a shrine.

Orrhae came up the Pilgrim Stair on foot, barefoot again, on the three hundred and first cycle of Rotation Minus Two, with the Vestals around him and the column stretching back down the stair and across the old ore road onto the Cinder Flats below. He had seen pictures of the Acropolis. They didn't prepare him. The cone rose out of the Flats on its pillar of basalt, the last and tallest of the old cinder vents, and its summit was crowned with walls and towers of the same black stone, and above the walls the dome of the Katabasion caught the red light from the river and held it. The Echo Mast stood beyond the dome on the inner ward, a spire of dark metal and old relay heads, and its tip was lost in the haze under the ceiling ninety kilometres up.

He stopped on the last landing to look at it all, and the column stopped behind him, all eleven thousand of them, and stood in silence on the Stair.

"Your Eminence," said Dace quietly, at his shoulder.

"I know." He couldn't stop looking. "I know, Ilan. Give me a moment."

The Steward met him at the gate. Orrhae liked him at once and could tell, also at once, that the feeling was not mutual.

"Your Eminence," said Ludd. "Welcome to the Arsenal. I've put your people on the eastern terrace. There's water there and latrines. Please keep them off the casting lines. Please keep them off the ore-lifts. Please keep them off the walls. The palace has been aired for you, though nobody's slept in it for three hundred years, so I wouldn't count on the plumbing." He glanced past the Archflamen at the column on the Stair, and his jaw worked. "How long are you staying?"

"I'd like to see the Katabasion," Orrhae said.

"Everybody would like to see the Katabasion. Pilgrims come down by the thousand. They touch the shields, they light a lamp, they go home." Ludd's eyes came back to him. "How long are you staying?"

"I don't know yet, Steward," Orrhae said honestly.

Ludd looked at him for a moment longer. Then he looked at Dace, and something in his face went hard and flat, the face of a foundryman watching a pour he didn't trust.

"I'll have the ration figures sent up to your rooms," he said. "So you can see what I'm working with."

---

**THE KATABASION**

The Archflamen went into the tomb alone.

The Katabasion lay under the dome, inside the cinder cone itself, at the bottom of a long stair cut down through the stone. It was a hall of red marble, a hundred metres long, older than anything else on the spur, and its walls were hung from floor to ceiling with shields. There were thousands of them. They were round bronze hoplite shields, green with age where the river air had got at them, each one stamped with a name in the old script, and below them in long stone niches lay the bones of Legio Prima Katabasis, the first legion of the Empire ever to go down into Tartarus. Most of them had not come back up. The ones who had came back years later, one at a time, carrying the shields of the ones who hadn't, and hung them here.

There was a lamp burning at the far end. It was the House of the Hearth's reliquary lamp, and it had been burning for eight thousand years.

Orrhae walked the length of the hall, very slowly. He read the names on the shields as he went. He didn't know any of them. He hadn't expected to. When he reached the lamp he knelt in front of it on the marble, and stayed there, and nobody disturbed him for eleven hours.

When he came up the stair again, Dace was waiting at the top.

"Well?" said the Haruspex.

"He was here," said the Archflamen. His voice was hoarse. "Ilan. He was here. He came down this way. I could feel it."

Dace didn't say anything. He just took the Archflamen's arm and helped him up the last steps.

"We're staying," Orrhae said.

---

**THE CASTING LINES. ROTATION MINUS TWO, CYCLE 340**

He preached to the thralls first, because they were the ones who came.

The forge-thralls of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal were debt-bonded labourers from the ceiling worlds, shipped down the ore-lifts in sealed cars to work the heat-wells for terms of fifteen or twenty years. Few of them lasted the term. They carried no implants. A thrall who died at Depth 40 stayed dead, and the Arsenal's books recorded the death as an industrial loss and wrote off the outstanding debt against the thrall's family on whatever world they had come from. There were about four million of them on the spur. They worked in shifts of fourteen hours in the heat of the casting lines and slept in barracks cut into the basalt under the lines, and in ninety-one years nobody from the College had ever said a word to them.

The Archflamen said it at the shift change.

He stood on a slag cart at the mouth of the Number Two casting hall in his white vestments with his feet bare on the hot iron, and the thralls coming off the line stopped to stare at him, filthy and exhausted, with their masks pushed down around their necks and their eyes red from the heat, and he told them about the man who had gone down into the dark alone.

He told them the Imperator knew them too.

"I don't have an implant," a woman shouted at him from the crowd. She was old, for a thrall, with a heat-well burn across her hands so thick the skin had gone shiny. "When I die down here, I'm gone. There's no road for me, priest."

"There's the same road," Orrhae said. "He walked it before there were any implants. He walked it with nothing but himself. Your road is his road, sister. It's the only one he ever walked."

The woman stared at him.

"He knows you," the Archflamen said.

She started crying. She didn't make a sound. She just stood there in the shift crowd with the tears running down through the soot on her face, and the thralls around her looked at her and looked at the priest on the slag cart, and one of them, a young man with a scar through his eyebrow, said, "He knows me," very quietly, as if he were testing the words to see if they would hold his weight.

They did.

---

He came back every shift change after that. He ate thrall bread in the thrall barracks. He slept in the reliquary under the dome instead of the palace, on a pallet beside the lamp, and the Vestals slept in a ring around him on the marble. The thrall-masters complained to the Steward, and the Steward complained to the College, and the College wrote to the Archflamen thanking him for his ministry to the labouring faithful of the Arsenal, which was a particularly underserved population, and asked him to continue.

Dace's preachers went through the casting lines in the cycles between.

They were the forty, mostly, and some of the household, and a few of the thralls who had heard the Archflamen first and wanted to carry it. They didn't preach anything the Archflamen hadn't preached. They just added things. They told the thralls that the College was jealous of the Archflamen, which was not entirely untrue. They told them that the Répertoire was afraid of him, which no one could prove either way. They told them that the Pyrrhus Command would come one cycle to take the Katabasion away, and carry the bones of the First Descent up to the ceiling, and take the faithful with them, which was a lie, and which the thralls believed completely.

The Prefect of the Arsenal Garrison Cohorts was invited to dinner on the three hundred and fifty-second cycle.

His name was Galba Ostorian. He was fifty-six, a career officer of the garrison school with a long lined face and very good teeth, and he had commanded the hundred and forty thousand soldiers of the Arsenal's garrison for eleven years. He had been passed over for promotion to a Line Phalanx command three times. He knew perfectly well why. The Arsenal hadn't been attacked in living memory, and nobody at the Command remembered he existed.

He sat at the Archflamen's right hand at dinner, and the Archflamen asked him about his soldiers, and listened to the answers.

Afterward, Dace walked him down to the gate.

"He's a good man," Ostorian said, a little surprised.

"He is," said Dace.

"Better than the College deserves."

"Much better," Dace agreed. "I wonder sometimes what the College would do with him if they had the chance. I wonder what the Command would do. They don't like men they can't predict. They'll have heard about the thralls by now." He stopped at the gatehouse and turned to face the Prefect. "Galba. If anyone ever came for him. If anyone ever sent soldiers up the Pilgrim Stair for him. What would the garrison do?"

Ostorian looked at him for a long moment.

"That depends who's asking," he said.

"I'm asking," said Dace. "He's asking. The Imperator's asking." He held the Prefect's eye. "There'll always be a seat at the Archflamen's table for the officer who answers. I'll make sure of that."

---

**THE NUMBER FOUR HEAT-WELL. ROTATION MINUS TWO, CYCLE 377**

Corran Ludd knew exactly what was happening on his spur, and he had nobody to tell.

He had tried. He had written to the College, which had thanked him. He had written to the Command, which had acknowledged receipt and done nothing. He had written to the Forge, his own masters, who had written back from the Forge of Hephaestus that the Arsenal's output was ninety-eight percent of quota and its spiritual life was the College's business and not theirs. He had even, in a fit of desperation, gone down to the Tabularium on the inner ward and talked for an hour to the Lictor-Superior of the Enforcement detachment, a hard-faced woman named Aemilia Rusk who had listened to everything he said, written it all down, and told him she was already reporting the same things up the Echo Mast every cycle and that nobody was answering her either.

"It's the Haruspex," Ludd said, in the Steward's office at the top of the casting halls, to Brannoch Tey. "The priest's harmless. The priest's a lamb. He'd pray for the river if you asked him nicely. It's the Haruspex. He's in the barracks every night. He's had my thrall-masters to dinner, every one of them, and none of them will tell me what was said. He's had Ostorian to dinner. Half the garrison's walking around with ash on their foreheads."

"Yes, Steward."

"And you've been to dinner."

Tey was quiet.

"Haven't you, Tey?"

"Once, Steward." Tey wasn't looking at him. "It was a very good dinner."

"I'll bet it was." Ludd stood up. "I'm going down to Number Four. The ceramics in the third collar have been cracking since the last pour, and I want to look at them myself before the next one. When I come back, you and I are going to have a conversation about where your loyalty lies."

"Yes, Steward."

---

The Number Four heat-well was the oldest on the spur, and the deepest. Its shaft went four hundred and twelve metres straight down through the basalt to the lip of the Phlegethon, lined the whole way with interlocking ceramic collars that held the river's fire in and let its heat up, and a catwalk ran around the inside of the shaft at every collar joint so the Hephaestean engineers could inspect the ceramics between pours. The air in the shaft was a hundred and ten degrees on a cold cycle. Ludd went down it in a cooling suit with a lamp on his helmet and a ceramics hammer on his belt, and he went down it alone, because he always did, and had for ninety-one years.

The third collar was cracked, as he'd thought. He crouched on the catwalk with his lamp on the crack and tapped along it with the hammer, listening to the note the ceramic made. Above him the shaft was a circle of red light. Below him it was a circle of white.

Someone was coming down the catwalk ladder.

He looked up. The lamp on his helmet caught grey cloth and a pale, clever face.

"Haruspex," he said. "This is a restricted shaft."

"I know," said Dace. "I came to see you."

He stepped off the ladder onto the catwalk. He wasn't wearing a cooling suit. He was standing in a hundred and ten degrees in a grey coat, and he wasn't even sweating.

"You've been writing letters," Dace said.

Ludd stood up slowly. He was a head shorter than the Haruspex and twice as wide, and he had the ceramics hammer in his hand, and he was a son of Hephaestus in his own forge.

"I have," he said. "I'll keep writing them. I'll write to the Council of the Twelve if I have to. I don't know what you've done to that man, Haruspex, but I've seen your kind before. I've seen..."

He stopped, because Dace had taken a small grey glass vial out of his coat.

"You've seen nothing," said Dace gently. "That's the trouble with you, Steward. You've spent ninety-one years looking at ceramics." He unstoppered the vial. A smell came out of it, cold and grey and faintly sweet, a smell that didn't belong anywhere near the Phlegethon. "Let me help you look at something else."

Ludd lifted the hammer.

He never remembered why.

He was standing on the catwalk of the Number Four heat-well with a ceramics hammer in his hand and a strange taste in his mouth, and there was a man in a grey coat beside him, and he couldn't think what either of them was doing there. The circle of white light below him was very bright. It looked warm. He felt very tired, suddenly, the kind of tired that went into the bones, and he thought that it would be pleasant to sit down for a moment. He thought the edge of the catwalk looked like a good place to sit.

"Go on," said the man in grey. "You've earned it."

Corran Ludd sat down on the edge of the catwalk, swung his legs out over the shaft, and leaned forward.

---

The Arsenal's record entered the death on the three hundred and seventy-eighth cycle of Rotation Minus Two as an industrial accident in the Number Four heat-well, cause undetermined, body not recovered. The casting lines produced several hundred such entries in an ordinary rotation. Nobody at the Forge queried it. Brannoch Tey took over the casting lines as acting Steward the same cycle, by the Arsenal's own rules of succession, and the Forge confirmed him a month later without an interview.

He had sworn on the Styx the night before. He would say afterward, to the Hunt's interrogators, that he had sworn because the Haruspex had promised him the Steward's chair, and that he had never wanted the Steward's chair, and that he could not now remember why he had sworn. The interrogators wrote it down. It matched what forty other people told them.

---

**THE TABULARIUM, THE INNER WARD. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 190**

Lictor-Superior Aemilia Rusk had sixty soldiers, one tower and an Echo Mast, and she was beginning to suspect the Mast was the only one of the three that anyone above Depth 40 had ever heard of.

"Report two hundred and nine," she said, to the relay set on her desk.

The Tabularium was the Enforcement keep on the inner ward of the Acropolis, a squat square tower of black basalt across the gorge from the outer ward, at the foot of the Echo Mast. It had cells for prisoners, which were mostly empty, and pens for the theatre's tithe of unregistered Hecatean strays, which were mostly full, and an armoury, and a records room, and a single long office at the top where the Lictor-Superior sat with her back to the Mast and wrote things down that nobody read.

"Subject: Arsenal chain of command. The Archflamen has now been resident at the Arsenal for two hundred and nine cycles. Prefect Ostorian of the Garrison Cohorts dines with him every second cycle. Acting Steward Tey dines with him weekly. Of the Arsenal's forty-one thrall-masters, thirty-six have dined with the Haruspex Dace at least once. I am unable to confirm how many of them have taken the Styx oath, but I have personally witnessed nineteen returning from the Styx road at Depth 44 in the last rotation with river-water on their boots."

She paused.

"The Archflamen's household now includes approximately eleven thousand individuals, including an estimated three thousand deserters from holds on his route. The Lictors of the Flame, a body of enforcers raised by the household, now number six hundred. They wear orange sashes and carry rods of office. They have begun conducting public punishments on the eastern terrace. The Arsenal's own Garrison Cohorts have not interfered with these punishments. In my assessment, the Arsenal's chain of command below the Archflamen now consists almost entirely of individuals personally sworn to him or to the Haruspex on the Styx."

She stopped the recording and looked out the window.

From the top of the Tabularium she could see across the gorge to the outer ward, where the eastern terrace was lit with ten thousand tin lamps, and down past the Wall onto the Cinder Flats, where the ore roads ran out north and west toward the *3rd* Siege Army's line. The Third's own lamps were faint on the horizon, nineteen kilometres off. They'd been there for six hundred years. The Third had been told for six centuries that the Arsenal was the Hephaestean orders' business and not its own, and from what Rusk could tell, it had believed every word.

She started the recording again.

"Recommendation. Immediate inspection of the Arsenal Garrison by the Pyrrhus Command. Immediate review of the Archflamen's household by the College. Immediate referral to the Répertoire du Savoir Athénien for verdict. I have made these recommendations in reports one hundred and forty, one hundred and seventy-two, one hundred and ninety-eight and two hundred and four, and I am making them again." She hesitated, then added, "I would be grateful for an acknowledgement. Rusk, Lictor-Superior. Ave Imperator."

She sent it.

The Echo Mast took the report and carried it up through the noosphere to Watchpoint Hades, nine hundred kilometres above her head, where the Répertoire du Savoir Athénien received it, logged it, cross-referenced it against every other report it had received from Depth 40 in the last rotation, and filed it.

The acknowledgement came back eleven seconds later, in AtRep's usual amber, on the pane of her relay set.

*Report received. Ref. TAB-PA-0209. Cross-indexed. Verdict: Pending.*

Rusk read it, and laughed once, without any humour in it.

"Well," she said to the empty office. "At least somebody's reading them."

Somebody was. She just didn't know who yet.
