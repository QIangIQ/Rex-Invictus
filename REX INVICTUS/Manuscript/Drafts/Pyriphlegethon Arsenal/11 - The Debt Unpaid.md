## Chapter 11: The Debt Unpaid

*"The Wheel keeps the count. It has never once lost a number."*

*Catechism of the sworn, Cohors Nemesis Prima*

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 30**

The Eighth Line Phalanx came down to Landing Shelf Kappa on the thirtieth cycle of Rotation Seven, and Leda Corvis learned about it the proper way, from a signal under the Directorate's seal that arrived on her desk four hours before the first transport touched the shelf.

She read it twice. Then she took it in to Castorre herself, which she did with perhaps one signal in fifty, and put it on the desk in front of him without a word.

He read it. He read it again, slowly, his finger moving down the order of battle, and when he got to the bottom he sat back in his chair and looked at the ceiling of his office, at the old pipework and the stain where the condensate had come through in Rotation Three.

"Three regiments," he said.

"The *50th*, the *52nd* and the *54th*, sir. Full strength, or near it. They were designated for a reformation peak on the upper Phlegethon. Your cousin at the College had them diverted."

"Which cousin?"

"Flamen Aurelia, sir. She wrote a covering note." Corvis put that on the desk too. "She says she called in every favour the family was owed on the upper river and some it wasn't, and that she'd be grateful if you'd write to your mother."

Castorre read the covering note with the expression of a man who had been told by a physician that the operation had gone well and that he would never again eat anything he liked. He put it down.

"The *54th*," he said. "I don't know the *54th*."

"Lethe Hold drafts, sir. Raised off the ration rolls in Rotation Two, when the shells stopped coming. Most of them have never been to Depth 40." Corvis paused. "The colonel's name is Corin Vadas. I've looked him up. He was an administrator in the Lethe Hold's ration office until the regiment was raised and nobody else would take it, so the Directorate gave him a colonel's grade and the regiment together."

"Gods below," Castorre said, without heat. He picked up the order of battle again. "Well. The *50th* and the *52nd* know their business, at least. Who has the *52nd*?"

"Colonel Damaris Lowe, sir. Thirty-four, a legacy of Athena from Ostia Minor. She's had the regiment four months. Before that she was its senior centurion, and she held a peak on the Acheron shelves for two rotations with half a regiment when the river came up and took her colonel."

"Good." He put the slate down, squared it against the edge of the desk, and for the first time in some weeks Corvis saw something in his face that she might, with charity, have called relief. "Good. That's something. We put them on the western Flats, between the Seventh and the Ninth, and we give them the rest of the rotation to dig in and learn the ground, and then..." He stopped. She hadn't moved. "What?"

"There's another thing, sir."

"There's always another thing."

"The Fleet's movement log for the southern Flats," Corvis said. "I check it every watch. You know I check it. There's an entry from the second watch of the twenty-ninth that I didn't recognise, so I asked the Fleet liaison what it was, and he said he wasn't sure he was allowed to tell me, so I reminded him that I'd signed his ration chits for three rotations." She laid a third slate on the desk. "A battle-carrier named the *Inexorable*, sir, with the strike cruisers *Adrasteia* and *Rhamnous* and nine escorts. They came down through the ceiling over the southern Flats yesterday and they're holding station beyond the lance envelope. They're flying the seal of the Pyrrhus Command."

Castorre looked at the slate. He didn't pick it up.

"Whose ships are they, Leda?"

"Cohors Nemesis Prima, sir. The Cohort of Nemesis."

There was a long silence in the office. Somewhere down the corridor a printer was chattering away to itself, spitting out the casualty returns of the morning, and Corvis listened to it and waited.

"How many?" Castorre said.

"The liaison thinks about two thousand. Half the Cohort. Under the Prefect himself, a man named Lucan Ardent." She hesitated. "Sir, they haven't signalled us. They haven't signalled anybody on the Flats. The liaison says their Lanterns have been going down to the ash south of the Kerberos Hills since the first watch, and nobody on the *Inexorable* has answered a single hail from the Fleet that didn't come under the Pyrrhus seal."

Castorre put his hands flat on the desk on either side of the three slates and looked at them for some time.

"Draft a protest to the Command," he said at last. "Courteous. Factual. Point out that a force of two thousand pure-bloods is operating inside the theatre of a Siege Army in the field without reference to its Lord-General, and that this is contrary to every regulation I've ever read and some I've written. Ask for the Cohort to be placed under my command for the duration of its operations on the Flats, or failing that for its Prefect to inform me of what those operations are."

"Yes, sir."

"They won't answer it."

"No, sir," Corvis said. "The Command will forward it to the Pyrrhus Command, and the Pyrrhus Command won't answer it, and in about a month we'll get a slate from the Directorate telling us that the matter has been referred."

"Send it anyway."

"I've already drafted it, sir."

He looked up at her, and for a moment there was something close to a smile on his tired grey face.

"Of course you have," he said.

**THE BATTLE-CARRIER *INEXORABLE*, ABOVE THE SOUTHERN FLATS. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 31**

There were ten centurion-masters in the Cohort of Nemesis, one for each century, and Lucan Ardent had known every one of them longer than most mortal men had been alive. He looked round the table at them now, in the planning room under the *Inexorable*'s bridge, with the map of the Phlegethon Docks spread out between them in pale green light, and thought that it was a pity they had to waste themselves on a place this ugly.

"Eleven kilometres of quays," he said. "Slipways, warehouses, shell sheds, barge hangars, repair shops. The ore comes up the river from the lower shelves to here, and the shells go out from here to the holds downriver, or they did until the Archflamen decided he'd rather keep them. It's the Arsenal's only door to the Phlegethon. Everything that comes up the river to help him comes through it. We're going to take it, and then we're going to break it so thoroughly that nobody will ever use it again."

Nobody at the table said anything. The centurion-masters of the Cohort did not as a rule say anything until the Prefect had finished, and Ardent had been Prefect for a hundred and nine years and had never once found this tiresome.

He touched the map. The light shifted, pulled back and showed the gorge.

"The docks sit at the south-eastern foot of the spur," he said. "They lie across the Demus Gorge from the Flats, which is why the Third has never fought there. Three chain-bridges carry the docks' roads across the Gorge to the spur. If the Archflamen wants to reinforce the docks, he does it over those bridges. So the first thing we do is take them away from him." He looked down the table. "Kett."

Ilario Kett, centurion-master of the Third Century, sat up a little straighter. He was the youngest of the ten, a hundred and forty years old and a grandson of the goddess through her son Adrastos, with a blood index of ninety-one that he had never quite forgiven his mother for. He had a narrow, clever face and the dry habit of speech that the older masters called insolence and the younger sworn called style.

"Prefect," he said.

"The three bridges are yours. Take them, hold them long enough to set your charges and drop them into the Gorge. Then come back and join the attack on the docks. I'll want you on the western side to stop the garrison getting out."

"How deep is the Gorge, Prefect?"

"Nine kilometres."

"Then it should take them a while to climb back up," Kett said, and one of the older masters at the far end of the table made a small sound through his nose that might have been disapproval.

Ardent let it pass. He turned the map again, south this time, down the river to a long grey shelf of shingle at the foot of a line of low dark hills.

"The Gravel Strand," he said. "Forty kilometres south of the docks, at the foot of the Kerberos Hills. That's where we land. The Lanterns come down through the ceiling cracks south of the lance envelope, in a long loop to keep clear of the silos, and put us on the shingle in a few hours. Then we go north along the riverbank, fast, with the striders out in front to find the blocking positions and the gunships over them. We envelop the docks and strike wherever the garrison proves weakest." He looked round the table again. "We can't afford a war of attrition. We have two thousand and forty sworn, eleven heavy carriers, two squadrons of assault tanks and a month of supplies, and the ships won't come down inside the lance envelope to support us. When we go into the docks, we go in without fire from above. So we go in fast and we keep going until it's finished."

"The Third, Prefect?" one of the masters asked. Galla Morn, of the Seventh Century, the oldest of them, who had been sworn under Ardent's predecessor.

"What about them?"

"Should they be informed?"

Ardent considered the question honestly, as he considered every question put to him at this table, and found that he had no answer to it that would not sound unkind.

"The Third is a Siege Army," he said. "It holds ground. It holds it well, by the look of it, and at a cost I'd find difficult to bear. That's its purpose, and it isn't ours. The Lord-General's staff will see the docks fall, and they'll know what it means for the second belt, and they'll make use of it or they won't. I see no reason to complicate their lives by asking their permission."

"The Lord-General has protested to the Command, Prefect. The Fleet liaison sent us a copy."

"I'm sure he has," Ardent said. "Has the Command replied?"

"It forwarded the protest to the Pyrrhus Command."

"Then I expect the Pyrrhus Command will reply in due course." Ardent touched the map once more and let it fade. "That's the plan, masters. Two reasons for it, both good. The docks are the Archflamen's only access to the river, and whatever's coming up from Depth 43 to help him has to come through them. And if we threaten the docks, the Archflamen has to bring troops back from the second belt to keep them, and the Third will find the second belt thinner for it, which is a kindness to them whether they asked for it or not."

He stood. The masters stood with him.

"Castigator," he said. "Stay a moment."

The others went out. Ennio Dask stayed by the table with his hands folded behind his back and waited, and Ardent looked at him for a while without speaking.

Dask was not a centurion-master. He held no century and commanded no sworn except when the Prefect lent them to him. He was the Cohort's Castigator, and his work for the Cohort was the questioning of oath-breakers, which he had done for two hundred and sixty years with a patience that Ardent had seen break men who had held out against fire. He was a big, heavy, quiet man with a broad face and thinning grey hair, a son of Nemesis by a mortal father, and he carried the rod of his office everywhere he went: a staff of black iron as long as his arm, banded with silver, that the sworn said had been forged from the chains of a man who had sworn falsely before the goddess herself. He had a soft voice and good manners and a fondness for the poetry of the Second Empire, and the sworn were more afraid of him than of Ardent, which Ardent had always thought entirely sensible of them.

"There's a third reason," Dask said.

"There is."

"The Forsworn."

Ardent went to the port and looked out. Below the *Inexorable* the southern Flats lay grey and dead under the ash cloud, and away to the north, at the furthest edge of seeing, the dull red line of the Phlegethon wound across the plain toward the dark mass of the spur. Somewhere down there, in that red light, was the oldest debt on the Cohort's books.

"Eleven hundred years," Ardent said. "Hesper Calloway broke her oath to the Command at the confluence in the last cycle of the Styx War, and then she stood in the river and swore on the Styx that she hadn't, and the river took her. Every Prefect of the Cohort since has had her name on his slate. Every one of them has died with it still there." He turned from the port. "I don't intend to."

"She may not be taken easily, Prefect."

"She won't be taken at all, I expect. Not by us, not this time. She's held the confluence for six hundred years and been in the river for eleven hundred more, and she knows we're here by now." Ardent was quiet for a moment. "But there are four hundred of her soldiers on the Flats, Ennio. Four hundred of the Forsworn. If we can take even one of them alive, and you can sit with them for a few weeks in the hold, we'll know more about her than any Prefect has known since the Styx War. Where she goes. What she wants. What she's promised the Archflamen, and what he's promised her."

"And the Mast."

"And the Mast," Ardent agreed. "If anyone knows what the Mast is listening for, it'll be one of hers."

Dask inclined his head.

"I'll want them taken alive, Prefect, wherever we find them. Whatever it costs."

"You'll have it," Ardent said. "Give that order to the masters before we go down. Every century. If they find Forsworn, they take them alive, and they send for you."

**THE SOUTHERN CHAIN-BRIDGE, THE DEMUS GORGE. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 34**

The southern chain-bridge was a kilometre and a half of iron deck hung on chains above a hole in the world, and Ilario Kett had been looking at it through the Lantern's nose glass for eleven minutes before he finally admitted to himself that it was the ugliest thing he'd ever been asked to steal.

It had been built by Hephaestean engineers in the first century of the Arsenal, when the docks were new and the spur had needed a road to them that would carry a tracked crawler with forty tonnes of ore on its back. They had hung it from eight anchor towers, four on each side of the Gorge, great squat things of black iron as tall as a regiment's barracks, and from the towers they had slung chains whose links were the size of a man, swooping down and out across the void, and from the chains they had hung the deck on rods, all of it black and rust-red and streaked with the yellow of the sulphur that came up out of the Gorge on the hot wind. It swayed. Even from three kilometres away, through the nose glass, Kett could see it swaying slowly in a long shallow arc, a few metres east and a few metres west, as the wind came up from below.

And below it there was nothing. There was the Gorge, nine kilometres deep, and at the bottom of it, so far down that Kett's eyes refused to believe it, a thin dull red thread that was a tributary of the Phlegethon, working its way out to the main river through the floor of Tartarus.

"Master," the pilot said. "Two minutes."

"Thank you, Tasso."

Kett turned from the nose glass and went back into the Lantern's hold, where the assault sections of the Third Century sat in two rows facing each other on the drop benches in their Nemesis plate, helmets on and weapons racked, two hundred and six of them across the four Lanterns of the first wave. The plate was dark grey, almost black, with the Wheel of the goddess picked out on each pauldron in dull silver and the sworn's name and oath-number on the breastplate. Most of them had been in the Cohort longer than Kett had been alive, and none of them looked up as he came back. They had done this before.

"Two minutes," he said. "Listen. We go out at low level over the eastern end of the deck. The checkpoint is a blockhouse with two heavy guns on the roof facing west, toward the spur, which is the direction the people who built it expected trouble to come from. We come from the east. We take the blockhouse before the garrison can turn the guns round, and then we take the deck. Lanterns Three and Four come down on the far end and rake it, and the line sections land behind them. Questions?"

"How long have we got, Master?" one of them asked. Optio Brenna Salt, his senior optio, who had been in the Third Century for two hundred years and had served under three masters and outlived two.

"As long as it takes the Archflamen's people on the spur to work out what's happening and send a column down the road to stop us," Kett said. "Call it twenty minutes. Call it fifteen to be safe. Call it ten if you want to be home for dinner."

A low sound went down the two rows of benches, something between a laugh and a growl. Kett pulled his helmet on and locked it down and felt the plate come alive around him, the soft hum of the servos and the cold breath of the air-feed on his face, and the Lantern's hold went from grey to green as his visor woke.

"Thirty seconds," Tasso said in his ear.

The ramp opened. The hot wind came roaring in, thick with sulphur, and below the ramp, sliding past at what seemed an impossible speed, there was nothing but the Gorge.

"Go," Kett said, and went.

He dropped forty metres. The jump pack fired at twenty, a hard brutal shove up through his spine that turned the fall into a long fast slant, and the eastern end of the bridge came rushing up at him, black iron deck and the squat grey blockhouse with the two heavy guns on its roof, their long barrels pointing the wrong way, out over the Gorge toward the spur. There were men on the roof. One of them was looking up. Kett saw his face, white and stupid with surprise under the brim of his helmet, and then Kett hit the roof beside him with both boots and a ringing crash of iron, and put his sword through him before the man had time to stop being surprised.

The others came down around him, onto the roof and the deck and the approach road, a hard rain of dark grey plate falling out of the sulphur haze, and for a few seconds the eastern end of the southern chain-bridge was nothing but noise. There were eleven men on the roof. They had not been expecting anyone. Two of them got as far as the traversing wheels of the guns before Salt's section reached them, and the rest went over the parapet into the Gorge or died where they stood. Kett went down the ladder into the blockhouse with his sword in one hand and his pistol in the other and found a guard room full of startled faces and a table with a game of dice on it, and he shot the first man who reached for a rifle, and the second, and the rest put their hands up.

"Keep them," he said to the sworn behind him. "Take their weapons. Sit them against that wall."

He went back up. The eastern end of the deck was his. Away to the west, the length of the bridge, the far end was already burning, where Lanterns Three and Four had come down over the western checkpoint and raked it with their rotary guns and rockets until it was a ruin of smoke and fire. Through the smoke Kett could see the line sections dropping from the gunships as they touched down on the approach road, and men in the brown-grey of the Arsenal's garrison running out of the burning checkpoint toward the middle of the bridge, toward him, away from the line sections, many of them throwing away their rifles as they ran.

"Salt," he said. "They're coming to us."

"I see them, Master."

"Let them come halfway. Then put them down."

The garrison came out along the deck in a long ragged stream, three or four hundred of them, running and stumbling on the swaying iron. The line sections came behind them at a steady walk, firing as they came, and the men at the back of the stream fell, and the men in front of them ran faster. When they were halfway across, Salt's sections opened fire from the eastern end. The garrison stopped. For a moment the whole stream of them stood there in the middle of the bridge, above nine kilometres of nothing, with the sworn of the Cohort walking toward them from both ends, and then some of them threw down their weapons and some of them knelt and some of them went over the rail, and the rest died in the middle of the deck where they stood.

"Time," Kett said.

"Eleven minutes, Master," Salt said.

"Good." He keyed the command channel. "Prefect, Kett. The southern bridge is ours. Eleven minutes. No losses."

"Thank you, Kett," Ardent's voice said, calm and distant. "Drop it."

"Yes, Prefect. One thing first, if you don't mind. I'd like to see who comes down the road."

He placed a heavy weapons section on the western end of the deck, by the burning checkpoint, where the road from the spur came down out of the rock in a long curve to meet the bridge. It was a good place. The road came down a cutting in the cliff, steep-sided, with a wall of black rock on one hand and the Gorge on the other, and anything that came down it would come in single file and in plain view for the last six hundred metres. The section set up its lance-cannon at the mouth of the bridge and waited.

They didn't wait long. Seven minutes after the bridge fell, the first vehicle came round the bend at the top of the cutting: a Thureos tank, squat and heavy, painted in the Arsenal's brown-grey, with the Hephaestean hammer on its glacis. Behind it came another, and then a pair of Hoplon carriers, then three walkers on their long jointed legs, then a battery of divine mortars on tracked crawlers, the whole column grinding down the cutting in a long rumbling line toward the bridge.

"Lead tank," Kett said. "When you're ready."

The lance-cannon fired. The beam went up the cutting in a white line that hurt to look at, and the lead tank stopped dead in the road with a hole through its glacis plate the size of a man's head and a moment later blew apart from the inside, its turret lifting off its hull on a column of white fire and coming down on the road behind it, upside down, blocking the cutting. The second tank tried to push past it on the inside and the lance-cannon fired again. The Hoplons tried to back up the hill and the walkers tried to step over the burning hulls and the section's gunners took them one by one, steadily, without hurrying, and within four minutes the road down the cutting was blocked from wall to edge by burning wrecks, and the column behind them had stopped and was trying to turn round in a road too narrow to turn in.

"That'll hold them," Kett said. "Salt, the charges."

The assault sections had been at the anchor towers since the bridge fell. They had climbed up the inside of the towers and out onto the great chains where they came down over the saddles at the top, and they had packed the saddles and the bases of the towers with keyed charges, the Cohort's own pattern, black discs the size of a dinner plate with the Wheel stamped into each. Kett checked them himself. He went up the ladder inside the nearest tower and looked at every charge, and the sworn who had set them stood back and let him. That was how the Third Century did things, and they had stopped minding a long time ago.

"Good," he said. "Everyone off."

The Lanterns came down onto the deck one at a time, and the sworn went up the ramps in their sections, and the prisoners from the guard room went up with them, sitting in a row against the hold wall under the guns of two of Salt's section with their hands on their heads. Kett went last. He stood on the ramp of the last Lantern as it lifted off and looked down at the bridge, at the burning checkpoint and the burning column on the road above it and the long black deck swaying gently over the void, and he pressed the detonator.

The charges went off together. The saddles at the tops of the eastern towers split apart, and the great chains, freed, came whipping up and over and out, and the deck dropped. It went slowly at first, the eastern end falling and the western end still held, the whole kilometre and a half of it swinging down and out from the western towers in a long, ponderous arc, and then the western charges went, and the whole bridge fell into the Gorge with its chains and its rods and its towers, and the burning hulls of the Arsenal's column went down the cutting after it, and the sound of it came up out of the Gorge for a long time afterward.

"Three wounded, Master," Salt said. "All fit to go on."

"Good," Kett said. "Next one."

They took the middle bridge before the end of the watch, and the northern one after the dark bell, and by the end of the cycle the Third Century had dropped all three bridges across the Demus Gorge into the dark, and the Arsenal's roads to the docks were cut. Nobody would cross from the spur or the Flats to the docks now except by air, and the Cohort owned the air.

Kett sent the prisoners up to the *Inexorable*. There were a hundred and eighty of them by the end of the cycle, thralls and garrison soldiers, and he looked them over himself before they went, walking down the line of them in the hold of the Lantern with his helmet off, studying their faces.

None of them were Forsworn. None of them had the cold about them that the briefings had described, or the scar at the nape, or the black swords.

He wasn't sure whether he was sorry.

**THE GRAVEL STRAND, NORTH OF THE KERBEROS HILLS. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 34**

The command carrier *Debt Unpaid* had been built on Rhamnous Primaris three hundred and twelve years before, to a pattern older than the Third, and Lucan Ardent had commanded the Cohort from its forward cabin for every one of the hundred and nine years he had been Prefect. It was a heavy tracked vehicle, slab-sided and low, painted the dark grey of the Cohort's plate, and it carried a full command section, a battle-map table, the relay sets that tied it to the *Inexorable* and to every century on the ground, and, in a locked rack beside Ardent's seat, his sword. The sword's name was the Scale. It had been his mother's.

The column came up the shingle in the dark of the first watch at the best speed the carriers could make. The Areion striders went out ahead of it in a wide fan, the fast bipedal mounts of the Cohort's scouts loping over the loose grey stones on their long backward-jointed legs, their riders crouched low in the saddles with their carbines across their knees. Over them, high up under the ash cloud, the Lanterns flew cover in pairs, their lamps dark, their sensors sweeping the ground ahead. Behind the striders came the heavy carriers and the assault tanks, then the Hoplons and the siege carriages, then the Furies, the mobile mortar crawlers that the Cohort used in place of artillery, which could fire a salvo of keyed shells on the move and be somewhere else before the shells landed.

Ardent watched it all on the map table. He liked the map table. It showed him the column as a long string of small grey lights crawling north up the shingle along the red line of the river, and the striders as a scatter of brighter lights fanned out ahead of it, and the docks as a dense dark block at the top of the table where the river met the spur. Every few minutes a new light appeared on the table in the Arsenal's brown, a picket or a crawler or a walker, and every few minutes one of those brown lights went out.

"Strider Nine reports a crawler picket at the river bend, Prefect," his signaller said. "Four light crawlers. Engaging."

The brown lights at the river bend flickered and went out.

"Strider Nine reports the picket destroyed. One rider wounded."

"Thank you."

The Archflamen's commanders knew where the Cohort was going by the end of the first watch. Ardent had expected them to. There was only one thing on the Gravel Strand worth going to, and it lay forty kilometres north at the end of the shingle where the river met the spur, and nobody who looked at the Cohort's line of march for more than a minute could have mistaken it. The signals intercepts began to come in shortly after the second bell. Reinforcements from the spur for the docks, ordered down the bridge roads, and then, an hour later, orders cancelled, and a long confused exchange of signals between the spur and the dock garrison about where exactly the bridges had gone.

Ardent allowed himself a small smile at that.

By the end of the first watch the *Debt Unpaid* was in sight of the docks.

He went up into the cupola to look. It was the first time he had seen them with his own eyes, and he took his time over it. The docks ran along the river's edge for eleven kilometres, from the Gorge in the north to the shingle in the south, a long dense jumble of iron and stone and concrete under the red light of the river: quays, cranes, slipways, warehouses, the great curved roofs of the barge hangars, the shell sheds with their rows of squat chimneys, all of it streaked with soot and rust and the white crust of the river's salts. Behind it the cliff of the spur went up and up into the ash cloud, and somewhere up there, far above, were the guns of the Arsenal's inner belt, which would be turning to look down at the docks about now.

"Prefect," the signaller called up. "Strider Four reports an armoured column leaving the docks to the south. Twenty-plus vehicles. Coming toward us."

Ardent dropped back into the cabin and looked at the table. The brown lights were there, a long cluster of them coming out of the southern end of the docks onto the shingle, moving fast.

"Lanterns," he said. "All of them. Then the column."

The Lanterns came down out of the ash cloud in pairs, their lamps lit now, and the Arsenal's armoured column on the shingle disappeared under a hail of rockets. Ardent watched the brown lights go out one by one on the table. By the time the *Debt Unpaid* and the heavy carriers came up, the shingle ahead of them was covered in the black smoke of burning hulls, and the surviving vehicles of the Arsenal's column were turning back toward the docks, and the carriers went through them as they turned, firing as they went, and kept going.

A signal came in from Kett. The first bridge had fallen.

Ardent swung the column east, round the burning wrecks, away from the southern end of the docks where the garrison would be waiting for him, and up toward the eastern approaches where the warehouses came closest to the cliff. If he hit the docks from the east, the garrison would have to come to meet him. When it did, he would put Kett's Century in from the west, behind it, and crush it between them.

The siege carriages went first. They were great slab-sided tracked machines with a single short-barrelled gun of enormous bore in each, and they went up to the edge of the docks with the striders guiding them and fired into the warehouses the garrison had fortified, one warehouse at a time, until each was rubble, and then moved on to the next. The sworn came out of the heavy carriers behind them and cleared the rubble. The Lanterns went back up to the *Inexorable* to rearm and came back down and went looking for the garrison wherever the striders found it, and the Furies fired salvo after salvo into the docks, and moved after every salvo, so that the gunners on the spur who were trying to find them across the Gorge could never quite catch up.

Ardent didn't halt at the dark watch. The Cohort didn't sleep on the first night of a battle and never had. By the first bell of the second cycle it had cleared the eastern approaches to the northern slipways, and the garrison had fallen back, and Kett's Century had landed west of the main slipways in the dark as a stop against its escape.

Ardent climbed back up into the cupola and looked at the docks again. There were fires burning all along the eastern edge of them now, red and orange under the red light of the river, and the smoke went up the face of the cliff in long slow columns into the ash.

"Signal from the Third, Prefect," the signaller said behind him. "Relayed by the Fleet. From the Lord-General's chief of staff. Asking whether the Cohort requires any assistance."

Ardent thought about it.

"Thank her," he said. "Tell her that the Cohort requires nothing."

**THE SHELL SHEDS, SOUTHERN PHLEGETHON DOCKS. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 36**

The Wheel was a disc of black iron two metres across, set on a pole of black iron three metres long, and it weighed as much as a grown man. It was the Cohort's war-standard and had been since the founding of the Cohort, and on its face, in silver, were the eight spokes of the goddess's wheel, and between the spokes, in letters so small that you had to stand close to read them, the names of every oath-breaker the Cohort had ever brought to account. There were a great many names. Some of them were nearly worn away.

Signifer Thessaly Kord carried it into the shell sheds at the head of the Sixth Century, on the third cycle of the battle, and Ennio Dask walked behind her.

He had not carried a sword in battle for a long time. He had his rod of office, the black iron staff banded with silver, and he held it in his right hand with its foot on the ground, walking with the unhurried calm of a man out on a country road on Rhamnous with nothing on his mind, and the Sixth Century fanned out through the ruins on either side of him in its sections. The siege carriages had been at the shell sheds since the dark watch, and most of the long low buildings were rubble now, broken brick and twisted iron and the stumps of the squat chimneys, with the shells that had been stored in them scattered through the ruins in their thousands, unfired, rusty, some of them split open and leaking yellow powder. The air tasted of brick dust and sulphur and the sweet chemical stink of the powder. Somewhere ahead, in the rubble, the garrison was waiting.

"Castigator," the Sixth's centurion-master said on the command channel. Mattius Vire, a short broad man of two hundred and thirty with a rasping voice. "The striders put about three hundred of them in the sheds ahead of us. Garrison and thralls both. They've had the night to dig in."

"Thank you, Master. Carry on as you think best."

"Yes, Castigator."

Dask had commanded nothing in battle for two hundred years, and he had no intention of beginning now. The Prefect had given him the Sixth and the honour of the Wheel for this cycle, and Dask had accepted both with gratitude, and then he had told Vire to fight the century as he would have fought it if Dask had stayed on the *Inexorable*. Vire had looked relieved.

They went into the sheds.

It was close work, from the first minute. The garrison had dug in among the rubble in small groups, two or three men in a hole with a rifle or a light gun, under the broken roof beams, in the cellars and the drainage ducts and the shell racks, and the Sixth had to find them one at a time and dig them out. The sworn went forward in pairs, one covering, one moving, through the heaps of brick and iron, and every few metres a rifle would crack from somewhere in the rubble and a round would spang off a pauldron or a helmet, and the pair would go to ground and the covering sworn would put a keyed grenade into the hole the shot had come from, and the hole would go up in a gout of white fire, and the pair would move on. When the garrison was in a cellar, the sworn burned it out with the flame lances. When it was in a shell rack, they went in with swords.

Dask walked through it all behind Kord and the Wheel at the same steady pace, watching.

He had always found battle interesting. He had seen a great deal of it in two hundred and sixty years, more than most of the sworn. The Cohort's work took it into the places where oaths were broken and oaths were mostly broken in wars, and he had never learned to like it, but he had never stopped finding it interesting either. He watched how the garrison fought, and where, and how they died. Most of them were thralls. He could tell the thralls at once. They fought hard and badly, without fear and without much sense, and when they were finished they died without any expression at all on their faces, and they stayed dead. The garrison soldiers were different. Some of them fought well, from good positions, with real skill. Some of them ran. A few of them, when the sworn came into their holes, put down their rifles and put up their hands.

Dask had those ones taken back to the carriers.

"For questioning, Castigator?" one of Vire's optios asked him, a lean dark woman named Ambrosia Hale, who was guarding a line of four of them squatting in the brick dust with their hands on their heads.

"For questioning, Optio. In due course."

"About what, Castigator? If I may ask. They're dock garrison. They won't know anything."

Dask looked down at the four prisoners. One of them was young, a boy of perhaps seventeen with a thin pale face and a cut over one eye, and he was looking up at Dask's rod with an expression of such complete and helpless terror that Dask felt, for a moment, something close to pity.

"Every one of these men swore an oath of service to the Empire, Optio," he said. "Every one of them broke it when they followed the Archflamen. They aren't Forsworn, and they won't be the ones the Prefect wants. But they're oath-breakers all the same, and the Cohort will want to know why they did it." He paused. "And they may know more than you'd think. Garrison soldiers see things. They hear things. They talk to each other in the barracks at night about things they've seen. Somebody in these docks has seen the Forsworn up close."

He went on. Ahead of him, Kord climbed a heap of rubble with the Wheel on her shoulder and planted its foot on the top, and the disc caught the red light of the river and shone, and the sworn of the Sixth Century, seeing it, gave a low hard shout and went forward again.

They cleared the shell sheds by the end of the cycle. At the dark bell Dask sat on an upturned shell crate in the ruins of the last shed, with his rod across his knees and a cup of the Cohort's bitter black tea in his hand, and reported to the Prefect.

"The southern docks are clear, Prefect," he said. "Fifteen of the Sixth transferred. None by Styx-iron. A hundred and four prisoners, garrison and thralls. The thralls are no use to us. I'd like the garrison sent up to the *Inexorable*."

"Do it," Ardent's voice said. "Thank you, Ennio. Well done."

"It was Master Vire's work, Prefect. I only walked behind it."

"Then thank Master Vire," Ardent said, and Dask could hear the smile in his voice. "Kord?"

"Unhurt, Prefect. The Wheel is unhurt."

"Good. Get some sleep."

Dask didn't sleep. He sat on the shell crate in the ruins with his tea going cold in his hand and looked across the docks to the north, to where the great curved roofs of the barge hangars rose up black against the red light of the river and the slipways ran down between them into the water. The garrison had pulled back into the slipways. Tomorrow the masters would agree a plan to take them, and Dask could guess the plan. He had known Ardent for a long time, and Ardent liked rings.

But the Forsworn had not come to the shell sheds. Fifteen of the Sixth transferred, and none by Styx-iron. The Archflamen had four hundred of the Forsworn somewhere on the spur or under it, and they had not come to defend the docks, which were the Arsenal's only door to the river.

Dask turned that over for a long time. He didn't like it.

**THE NORTHERN BARGE PENS, PHLEGETHON DOCKS. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 40**

The masters agreed their plan on the fourth cycle, and it was the plan Dask had guessed. Three concentric attacks on the slipways, from the east by the Prefect, then from the south by the Castigator with the Sixth, then from the west by Kett with the Third, each one tightening the ring, over three cycles.

On the seventh cycle of the battle, during the Third Century's attack from the west, Kett's sections overran the northern barge pens and found Forsworn there.

It happened fast. The barge pens were a row of long stone basins cut into the riverbank at the northern end of the docks, each one big enough to take an ore barge, with the river let into them through sluice gates and iron sheds roofed over them to keep the ash out. The Arsenal's garrison had turned the sheds into strongpoints and the basins into moats. Kett's sections had taken the first four pens in the morning and were going into the fifth, under the long iron roof, with the black water of the basin on their left and the walkway on their right, when Optio Salt, at the head of the leading section, stopped.

"Master," she said on the channel. "Something's wrong."

Kett was twenty metres behind her. He looked down the length of the shed. It was dark under the roof, lit only by the red glow that came in through the sluice gates from the river and the green of the sworn's visors, and it was cold, bitterly so, cold enough that he saw his own breath for the first time since he'd landed in Tartarus, a faint white smoke in front of his visor.

"Stand fast," he said. "All sections. Stand fast."

They came up out of the basin.

There were twenty of them. They came up out of the black water of the barge pen without a sound, onto the walkway between the Third Century's sections, in black armour that streamed with water and black cloaks that clung to them, with the black swords already drawn. They didn't hurry. They came up onto the walkway one after another and turned and walked toward the nearest sworn with all the time in the world.

Kett had heard the briefings. He had read the Ninth Styx Legion's records, all of them, on the long voyage down from Rhamnous. None of it prepared him for how they moved.

"Fire," he said.

The sworn fired. The rounds sparked off the black armour and fell away into the basin, and the Forsworn walked on, through the fire, and reached the first of the sworn, and the black swords went through Nemesis plate and the sworn inside it, and the sworn fell, and Kett saw on his visor the little blue spark of an upload that should have flickered at the edge of the display and didn't.

"Swords," he said. "Swords. Close with them. Pin them down. Take them alive. Prefect's orders. Take them alive."

He went forward himself.

He would remember afterward that he had never fought anything like them, and that he had fought a great many things in a hundred and forty years. They fought in silence. They fought without any wasted movement at all, the swords going in and coming out with a dreadful economy, and they fought as a unit, every one of them knowing where every other one was without looking, with the instinct that only soldiers who had stood in line together for centuries ever learned. The sworn of the Third Century were the best close fighters in the Empire, and they couldn't touch them. Kett closed with the first of them that came to him, a tall one with a centurion's crest on its helmet, and drove at it with everything he had, and it turned his sword three times without seeming to notice he was there and cut him across the thigh, through the plate, and the cold went into him through the wound and up into his belly and his chest. He went down on one knee.

Salt was there. She put herself between him and the Forsworn centurion and took its sword on her own, and held it, and two more of the sworn came in from the side with their swords and their weight and drove the centurion back against the wall of the shed, and pinned it there, three of them holding it against the iron with their swords crossed over its chest and their whole weight behind them.

"We have one, Master," Salt said, breathless. "We have one. Send for the Castigator."

The Forsworn centurion looked at her. It had no face Kett could see behind the black visor, and it said nothing. It stopped struggling. Then, quite slowly, it took its free hand off the hilt of its sword and reached up to its own throat, under the edge of its helmet, to the place at the nape of the neck where the implant would have sat, where the old scar was.

"Stop it," Kett said. "Salt. Its hand. Stop its..."

The Forsworn centurion did something there with its fingers that Kett couldn't see. It went limp. The black armour sagged between the three sworn holding it, and the water ran out of it in a long black stream onto the walkway, and when Salt tore its helmet off there was nothing inside but a smell of cold river and a little grey silt, and the black armour and the black sword lay on the walkway in a heap.

All along the shed it was happening. The Forsworn who had been pinned, or cut down, or cornered against the black water of the basin, reached up to their own necks and went limp and emptied, and the river ran out of them, and their armour fell in heaps on the stone. The ones who could still fight walked backward to the edge of the walkway and stepped off it into the basin and went under the black water without a ripple. In a minute it was over.

Kett sat on the walkway with his back against the iron wall of the shed and the cold in his leg and his chest and looked at the heaps of black armour lying along the walkway.

"Count," he said.

They counted. Eleven of the Third Century were down. Six of them had transferred. Five had been cut with the black swords, and there had been no flicker, and the medics who knelt by them didn't look up. Of the twenty Forsworn, nine lay on the walkway as empty armour. The rest had gone back into the river.

"Send for the Castigator anyway," Kett said. "He'll want to see this."

---

Dask came within the hour. He walked down the length of the shed slowly with his rod in his hand and stopped by every heap of black armour and looked at it, and then knelt by the one that had been the centurion and put out one big hand and touched the inside of the helmet, where the grey silt lay.

"Cold," he said.

"Yes, Castigator," Kett said. The medics had cut the plate off his thigh and bound the wound, which was neither deep nor bleeding, and gave him more pain than any wound he had ever taken. "They're all cold. It went into me through the cut. I can still feel it."

Dask didn't seem to hear him. He picked up the black sword that lay beside the empty armour of the centurion, carefully, by the hilt, with his gauntleted hand, and held it up to the red light from the sluice gates and looked at it for a long time.

"I've read about these," he said softly. "In the Ninth's records. I never thought I'd hold one." He laid it down again exactly where it had been. "Styx-iron, Master Kett. Forged at the confluence, in the Styx War, from metal that came up out of the river. It cuts the pattern before the implant can save it. Your five won't be coming back."

"I know."

"I'm sorry." Dask stood up. He looked down the shed at the black water in the basin, still and dark under the iron roof. "They won't be taken. Not like this. They'd rather go back into the river than sit with me in the hold, and the river will have them back." He was quiet for a moment. "She knew we were looking for them. She sent twenty of them here to show us we can't take them alive, and to cost us five, and to make the Prefect angry."

"Did it work?" Kett asked.

Dask looked at him with his heavy, patient face, and for a moment Kett thought he wasn't going to answer.

"We'll see," Dask said, "on the tenth cycle."

**THE SLIPWAYS, PHLEGETHON DOCKS. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 43**

On the tenth cycle of the battle Lucan Ardent ordered the final assault on the slipways, and led it himself, on foot.

He was the Prefect and the Cohort expected it. The last of the dock garrison was holding out in the ruins of the slipways and somebody had to go in and finish it. And his sword had been in its rack in the *Debt Unpaid* for ten cycles and he was tired of looking at it. He took the First, Second and Fifth Centuries, six hundred of the sworn, and went in from the east through the rubble of the barge hangars, with Kord and the Wheel at his side.

The slipways were the heart of the docks. There were nine of them, great sloping ramps of stone and iron that ran down from the yards into the river, where the barges had been built and repaired and launched, and between them were the dry docks, long stone basins below the level of the river with gates at their river ends that could be closed and pumped out. Over all of it, the cranes stood in a long row along the waterfront, black and rusted and most of them broken now, leaning at odd angles where the Furies had hit them. The garrison was in the yards at the top of the slipways, in the ruins of the repair shops and the barge hangars, about four hundred of them, the last of the dock garrison, with their backs to the river.

They broke before him.

He had expected a hard fight. He got none. The sworn of the First Century went into the ruins of the first repair shop and the garrison came out of the other side of it and ran, down the slipways toward the river, throwing away their rifles, and the sworn of the Second Century cut off the ones who tried to go north and the sworn of the Fifth cut off the ones who tried to go south, and in a few minutes the yards were full of running men and the slipways below them were full of men running down toward the water, toward the dry docks, toward nowhere.

Ardent stopped at the top of the fourth slipway and watched them go, and something cold went down his spine that had nothing to do with the Styx.

"Kord," he said. "Stay close."

"Prefect."

"Vire, Morn, Salvatore. All centuries. Halt. Halt where you are. This is..."

The Forsworn came up out of the dry docks.

They had been waiting in the culverts and the drainage channels beneath the slipways, in the flooded dry docks with the black water over their heads, under the feet of the garrison that had been placed above them as bait. They came up now all along the waterfront, out of the black water of the docks and the grated mouths of the culverts, a hundred of them, two hundred, in black armour streaming with river water, with black swords in their hands, and they came up the slipways in silence into the middle of the Cohort's assault.

The cold came with them. Ardent felt it hit him, across the whole length of the waterfront, a wave of it, and saw his breath go white in front of his visor.

Kord was killed in the first minute.

She was beside him. She had the Wheel on her shoulder and her pistol in her free hand, and she fired into the first of the Forsworn coming up the slipway toward them, three shots, all hits, all sparking off the black armour and falling away. Then the Forsworn was on her, and its black sword went through her breastplate, and she went down without a word, and the Wheel fell with her, the great black disc crashing down on the stone of the slipway and rolling, slowly, a little way down the slope, toward the water, and stopping on its edge against a ringbolt sixty metres below.

Ardent didn't remember deciding to go after it. He only remembered that the Wheel was on the ground, and that it could not be left on the ground. Nobody in the Cohort's history had ever left it on the ground, and he would not be the first, and he crossed sixty metres of open slipway under fire to reach it with the Scale in his hand while the Forsworn came toward it from below.

He got there first. Barely. He reached the Wheel and set his back against it and turned with the Scale up, and the first of the Forsworn reached him, and he killed it. He had never killed one before. He drove the Scale through the gap under its arm where the black plate met, and felt the cold go up the blade into his hand and his arm, and felt something go out of the black armour, and it fell.

Then the sworn were there around him, his sworn, the First Century and the Second, coming down the slipway in a rush of dark grey plate, and they closed around him and the Wheel and Kord's body, and the Forsworn came up the slipway against them, and they fought over Kord's body on the slipway without either side giving a metre.

Ardent fought with them. He would remember little of it afterward. He would remember the cold, and the silence of the Forsworn, and the terrible economy of the black swords, and how every one of the sworn who fell went down without a flicker. He would remember killing two more of them, or three, he was never sure. He would remember the voices of the masters on the command channel behind him, Vire and Morn and Salvatore, pulling their centuries back out of the trap, up the slipways into the yards, slowly, in good order, fighting for every metre. And he would remember that at the end of it, when the last of the Forsworn around the standard fell, he stood alone on the slipway over Kord's body with the Scale in his right hand, and that he bent and took up the Wheel in his left, and raised it.

The sworn on the slipway above him saw it, and roared.

And Hesper Calloway came up out of the river.

She came up the slipway from the water's edge, walking. She was tall and narrow in the black parade armour of the *9th* Styx Legion, armour that had last been worn on a parade ground eleven hundred years ago, and it was still wet with Styx-water, the water streaming off it as she walked and running back down the slope behind her to the river. Her helmet was open-faced, with the black crest of a Legate, and under it her face was long and pale and calm, and she carried a black sword. One of the sworn of the First Century saw her and went down the slope at her, and she cut him down without breaking stride, and stepped over him, and came on.

Ardent knew her at once. He had never seen her face. Nobody living had. But he had seen her name every day for a hundred and nine years, on the slate on his desk aboard the *Inexorable*, at the top of the list, where every Prefect of the Cohort since the Styx War had seen it.

She stopped five metres from him on the slipway and looked at him. Her eyes were grey and worn with tiredness.

"Prefect," she said. Her voice was low and hoarse, the voice of somebody who had been under water for a long time. "You've come a long way."

"Legate Calloway," Ardent said. "You owe a debt."

"I've heard." She lifted her sword. "Come and collect it."

He went for her.

He was a son of Nemesis with a blood index of ninety-seven. He had served with the Cohort for three hundred years and been its Prefect for a hundred and nine of them, and in all that time he had never met anyone he could not fight to a standstill. He had the Wheel in one hand and the Scale in the other, and the Scale had been his mother's, and it was the finest sword in the Cohort.

He was outmatched from the first stroke. Hesper had held the confluence of the Styx for six hundred years, against everything that came up out of the river, and she had been in the river for eleven hundred more. She met the Scale with her black sword and turned it aside, and turned it again, and again, without any effort he could see, and every stroke she made came at him from somewhere he wasn't looking. She drove him back up the slipway one step at a time. He used the Wheel as a shield and she cut through the rim of it. He drove at her with everything he had left and she stepped inside the stroke and struck him across the chest with the flat of her black sword, and his breastplate split from shoulder to hip, and the cold went into him through the split, and he felt his legs go. She struck the Scale out of his hand, and it went spinning away across the slipway, ringing on the stone. He went down on his back on the wet stone with the Wheel across his body, and she stood over him and raised her sword.

"Eleven hundred years," she said. "It's a long time to keep a name on a slate, Prefect. You should have let it go."

A heavy gun hit her in the side.

The round struck her low, under the arm, with a flat hard crash that rang across the whole waterfront, and spun her half round, staggering, and she nearly fell. Ennio Dask came up over the top of the slipway behind it at the head of a section of the Cohort's heavy sworn in their great slab-sided armour, the heavy guns at their hips still smoking, and he didn't stop. He walked straight down the slipway toward Hesper with his rod in his hand, at the same steady pace he had kept through the shell sheds. Hesper turned to meet him, with her sword up, still unsteady on her feet. He didn't give her time to steady. He swung the rod in a long flat arc, two-handed, and the black iron and silver of it, forged from the chains of a man who had sworn falsely before the goddess herself, hit her full in the chest.

Her breastplate split. The black parade armour of the *9th* Styx Legion, which had turned the Scale and every round the sworn had fired at it, cracked across from side to side under the rod with the crack of a breaking bell, and Hesper was hurled back down the slipway and fell, and lay still on the stone.

Dask turned at once to the Prefect. He knelt by Ardent on the wet stone and put one big hand on his split breastplate and looked into his face through the visor.

"Prefect."

"I'm all right." Ardent's voice was faint. He could barely feel his legs. "Ennio. She's..."

Dask turned back.

The slipway where Hesper had fallen was empty. There was a smear of black water on the stone where she had lain, and beside it an iron grating, the cover of a culvert, standing open. Below the grating there was nothing to see but darkness and the sound of running water.

**THE PHLEGETHON DOCKS. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLES 43 TO 45**

The ambush cost the Cohort dearly. When the last of the sworn had fought their way back up the slipways into the yards and the masters had counted their centuries, the Cohort of Nemesis had lost two hundred and twelve of its sworn in the battle for the docks. A hundred and twenty-four had transferred to the vats aboard the *Inexorable* and would wake there in a few weeks. Eighty-eight had been killed by Styx-iron, Signifer Kord among them, and they would not wake anywhere at all.

The Forsworn did not try to hold the slipways. They could not hold them alone, and Hesper had never meant them to. When she went down the culvert, they went after her, into the dry docks and the drainage channels and the black water, back into the ground beneath the spur, and the Cohort hunted down what remained of the dock garrison in the ruins and found nothing else.

It had not taken a single one of the Forsworn alive.

Dask told the Prefect so in the *Debt Unpaid*, where Ardent lay on a stretcher in the forward cabin with his chest bound and his split breastplate on the floor beside him and the Wheel propped against the wall, its rim cut half through, where Hesper's sword had gone into it.

"Nine of them gave themselves back to the river in the barge pens rather than be taken," Dask said. "Every one we pinned on the slipways did the same. We have their armour, Prefect. We have eleven of their swords. We have nothing else."

"The prisoners?"

"Three thousand of the dock garrison, in the *Inexorable*'s holds. I'll take them to Rhamnous. Some of them will know something." Dask paused. "Most of them won't. They'll know what the barracks knew, which is that there were soldiers under the docks who were cold and didn't speak, and that the Archflamen's officers told them not to go near the dry docks."

Ardent lay looking at the ceiling of the cabin.

"She was waiting for me," he said.

"Yes, Prefect."

"She knew I'd lead the last assault myself. She knew I'd go after the Wheel." He was quiet for a long time. "She knew more about me than I knew about her."

"She has had eleven hundred years to learn about us," Dask said gently. "We've had eleven hundred years to learn about her name."

The Cohort's engineers spent two more cycles demolishing the docks. They dropped the quays into the river one by one with keyed charges and brought down the roofs of the barge hangars and blew the gates of the dry docks so that the river flooded them, and they burned the warehouses and the shell sheds and the repair shops and broke the slipways with the siege carriages until there was nothing along the eleven kilometres of the waterfront that a barge could tie up to or a crawler could drive on. When they were finished, the Phlegethon Docks were a long black ruin at the foot of the spur, smoking under the red light of the river, and the river ran over the broken quays.

Then the Cohort of Nemesis went back up through the ceiling. The Lanterns lifted from the Gravel Strand in a long line, carrying the carriers and the tanks and the striders and the sworn, and the *Inexorable* and her escorts left their station over the southern Flats and climbed back through the ash cloud toward Rhamnous, and were gone.

The last thing Ardent did before they left was send a signal to the Third.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 46**

The signal came under the Cohort's seal, relayed by the Fleet, and Corvis read it at her desk at the start of the morning watch with her first cup of coffee in her other hand. Then she put the coffee down and read it again.

She took it in to Castorre. He was at his desk, shaving, with a mirror propped against the stack of slates and a basin of grey water at his elbow, and he didn't stop shaving while she read it to him.

"*From the Prefect of Cohors Nemesis Prima to the Lord-General commanding the 3rd Siege Army. The Phlegethon Docks have been destroyed. The Arsenal is cut off from the river. The Cohort will be back for the Legate. Ardent.*"

Castorre finished shaving the left side of his jaw. He rinsed the razor in the basin, and shook it, and wiped it on the towel across his knee, and looked at her in the mirror.

"Which Legate?" he said.

Corvis looked down at the slate in her hand. She thought about the slate she'd brought him on the forty-first cycle of Rotation Six, the archive's file from the *9th* Styx Legion, with Hesper Calloway's name at the top of it.

"I think we're about to find out, sir," she said.

He nodded slowly and went back to his jaw.

"Did they say anything else? Losses? What they found? Anything about the second belt?"

"No, sir. That's the whole signal."

"Did they tell us they were leaving?"

"No, sir. The Fleet logged the *Inexorable* leaving the theatre at the second bell. I found out from the movement log."

"Of course you did," Castorre said. He put the razor down and looked at his own face in the mirror for a while, the grey stubble on the right side of his jaw, the hollows under his eyes. "Two thousand of them. Ten cycles. And they took the docks and broke them and went home, and we didn't know they were coming, and we didn't know they'd gone, and we've no plan at all to make anything of it." He picked the razor up again. "Write it down, Leda. Write down what they did. Somebody in the Command is going to ask me one day what the Cohort of Nemesis achieved on my Flats, and I'd like to be able to tell them more than 'they didn't tell me'."

"Yes, sir."

She went back to her office and sat down and wrote it down, the whole of it, as much as the Fleet's logs and the intercepts and the striders' traffic could tell her, which was a good deal more than the Cohort had meant to tell anyone. The bridges. The column on the shingle. The shell sheds and the barge pens. The slipways, and the two hundred and twelve, and the black water. She filed it under the Cohort's name, with a cross-reference to the *9th* Styx Legion's archive and another to the file marked RUSK that she was not supposed to know Archmagister Varros was keeping.

It would be the end of the rotation before the Third's own spotters counted the cohorts on the second belt and found thirty thousand of them gone. Two thousand of the Cohort of Nemesis, in ten cycles, without a trench and without a word to anyone, had moved more of the Archflamen's army than the whole of the Third's general attack in Rotation Four.

Nobody had told them that either.
