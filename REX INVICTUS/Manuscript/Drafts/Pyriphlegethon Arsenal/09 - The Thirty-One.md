## Chapter 9: The Thirty-One

*"Each of them bore a dressing at the base of the skull, where the implant sat."*

*Medicus Ines Valcourt, 29th Trench Regiment, dissent to the 9th Line Phalanx, Rotation Five*

---

**THE RECOVERY HALLS, WATCHPOINT HADES. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 312**

The null list for the Cinder Flats was four metres long, and Breacher Iska Tamm read every name on it on her way out of the recovery hall, because the clerk at the door told her to.

"Read the list, Breacher," the clerk said. She was a thin grey woman behind a desk at the end of the long white hall, with a slate and a stylus and the face of someone who had said the same thing eleven thousand times. "Before you go down. Everybody reads the list."

"Why?"

"Because you'll want to know who isn't coming back," the clerk said, "and because I've been told to ask you a question about it, after."

So Tamm read the list.

It hung on the wall beside the door of the recovery hall, on a long strip of printed paper that came down from a slot in the ceiling and ran down the wall and across the floor and halfway back up the other side. It had been one sheet, once. The older transfers said so. In Rotation One, the list of the Third's nulls for a whole rotation had fitted on a single sheet of paper pinned beside the door, and soldiers coming back from the vats had glanced at it on their way out and shrugged. Now it was four metres for the Cinder Flats alone, in small type, three columns, and the Watchpoint's clerks printed a fresh one every rotation and pinned it up over the last.

She found eleven names she knew. Two from her own section. One from her old cohort, from before she went to the breachers, a big laughing trooper named Garro who had taught her to play dice in the support line in her first year. She stood in front of his name for a while.

Then she went back to the desk.

"Read it," she said.

"Thank you, Breacher." The clerk picked up her stylus. "The question is this. Of the names you know, how many of them died near a relay pylon?"

Tamm stared at her.

"I don't know," she said. "I wasn't there when most of them died. I was in a vat."

"Yes. Most people say that." The clerk wrote something. "Any of them?"

Tamm thought about it. She thought about Garro, who had gone up on Leaf 61 in the Saturation, in the wire, three hundred metres from the nearest pylon. She thought about the two from her section, who'd gone up together in a dugout on the support line when a shell came through the roof, right beside the pylon.

"Some," she said. "Some near, some not. I don't know."

"Thank you, Breacher." The clerk made another note and looked up. "That's what everybody says."

"What's it for?" Tamm asked. "The question. Who wants to know?"

The clerk looked at her for a moment over the slate, with her tired face.

"The technical office," she said. "They think the pylons have something to do with it. They've thought so for two rotations." She put the stylus down. "Nobody's given them a better idea."

---

Tamm went down the Cinder Stair that afternoon, as she had gone down it nineteen times before.

She was twenty-four. She'd been in the *29th* Trench Regiment for seven years, and in its heavy maniples for four, and in that time she'd been killed nineteen times, and she knew the number exactly, because the medici had started asking.

They asked everybody now. It was a new rule, from the beginning of Rotation Five. Every soldier who came down the Stair from the vats reported to the regimental medicus at the foot of it and gave a count, and the medicus wrote it down in a register, and when the count reached twenty, the soldier was supposed to be posted out of the forward trenches altogether and sent to the support line or the rest camps behind the railheads, for good.

She was at nineteen.

She'd thought about that a great deal on the Stair. She'd thought about it on every switchback, all forty-one of them, going down past the Quiet Ones' rail and the long column of small letters cut into the shelf wall at the top. Nineteen. One more, and she'd never have to go over a parapet again. One more, and she could sit in the support line for the rest of the siege, mending wire and hauling shells, and never be killed again.

She didn't know whether she wanted it.

---

**THE REGIMENTAL AID POST OF THE 29TH TRENCH REGIMENT, NORTHERN FLATS. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 314**

Medicus Ines Valcourt kept the register in a black ledger on the folding table of the regimental aid post, and she was beginning to think it was the most useless document in the *3rd* Siege Army.

"Nineteen," the breacher said.

Valcourt wrote it down. *Tamm, Iska. Breacher, Heavy 1st Maniple. 19.* Then she looked up. The breacher was standing in front of the table in her fatigues, without her plate, a tall broad-shouldered young woman with cropped dark hair and the slightly dazed look of a fresh transfer, still getting used to a body that had been grown nine days ago.

"Any trouble sleeping?" Valcourt asked.

"No, Medicus."

"Tremors? Headaches? Waking at the same hour?"

"No, Medicus."

"Do you remember your death?"

The breacher was quiet for a moment.

"Some of it," she said. "The shell. I remember the shell coming through the roof of the dugout. Not after."

"That's normal." Valcourt made a note. "You're at nineteen, Breacher. You know what that means?"

"One more and I'm off the line."

"One more and you're off the line." Valcourt looked at her. "Is that what you want?"

The breacher looked back at her for a long time, as if she'd been waiting for somebody to ask, and hadn't expected it to be the medicus.

"I don't know, Medicus," she said. "I really don't."

"No," Valcourt said. "Most of them don't." She closed the ledger. "Report to your maniple. And Breacher, if you start waking at the same hour every night, come and see me."

The breacher saluted and went out. Valcourt sat at the folding table in the hot, crowded dugout of the regimental aid post with the black ledger in front of her and looked at it for a while.

She was forty-one. She'd been the senior medicus of the *29th* for nine years, and a medicus of the Third for nineteen, and she'd come to the Third from a hospital on a ceiling world called Selinus where she had treated the dying for six years and never once seen any of them come back. She'd thought the Third would be easier. She'd thought that a war in which nobody stayed dead would be a war in which a medicus had very little to do.

She'd been wrong. She had more to do than she'd ever had in her life.

The vats made a soldier's body new every time. Valcourt had read the papers on it, the great thick technical treatises of the Watchpoint's vat-masters, and she understood the process as well as anyone outside the Watchpoint did. The implant at the base of the skull recorded the pattern of a soldier's mind, continuously, every moment of his life, and at the moment of death it fired, and sent the pattern up through the relays to the noosphere and from the noosphere to the Watchpoint, where the vat-masters wrote it into a new body grown in a vat of nectar. Nine days. Four hundred litres. Eleven seconds of the Watchpoint's bandwidth. And the soldier woke, whole and new, with everything he had ever known.

That was the trouble. He woke with everything he had ever known, including the moment he died.

Most soldiers came back from a first transfer fit for duty. Valcourt had seen thousands of them. They woke in the vats screaming, or fighting, or weeping, and the vat-masters held them until they stopped, and nine days later they came down the Stair and reported to the aid post and gave their count, *one*, and went back to their sections, and most of them were fine. A second transfer, the same. A third. By the fourth or fifth, some of them came back slowed. By the tenth, more of them. By the twentieth, half of them were waking every night at the same hour, the hour of their deaths, screaming the same thing, and some of them had tremors that never stopped, and some of them couldn't sleep without a light, and some of them couldn't sleep at all.

By forty, they were Old Ladders. Steady as stones. Afraid of nothing. Some of them, Valcourt suspected, not entirely there.

The register had been her idea, or at least she had been one of the medici who proposed it. She'd written to the Third's chief medicus in Rotation Three, and again in Rotation Four, and in Rotation Five the Command had taken note of it at last and approved the register, and every regiment of the Third now kept one, and any soldier who reached twenty transfers was to be posted out of the forward trenches altogether.

It was a good rule. It was also, she thought, looking at the black ledger, entirely useless.

More than a fifth of the Third's soldiers on the Flats had already passed twenty transfers. In the *29th* it was nearer a quarter. The regiment had been in the line for five rotations without a break, and it had been through the Drum and the outer belt and the Saturation, and its soldiers had been killed and come back and been killed again, over and over, until the register in front of her was full of names with numbers beside them in the twenties and thirties and forties. If she obeyed the rule, the *29th* would have no forward cohorts at all. So the *29th*'s colonel had quietly told her to ignore it, and she had quietly agreed, and the register sat on her table every cycle, filling up, while the soldiers in it went back over the parapet.

She opened the ledger again and turned to the back, where she kept her own notes, and wrote under the date: *Tamm, I. 19. Says she doesn't know if she wants it.*

She'd been writing that line, or lines like it, for most of the rotation. It had started to frighten her a little, how many of them didn't know.

---

**THE BRIEFING DUGOUT OF THE 29TH'S HEAVY MANIPLES. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 399**

"Lance Seven," said the centurion of the heavy maniples, and put his finger on the map.

There were two hundred and sixty breachers of the *29th* crowded into the briefing dugout on the northern Flats in the third watch, packed shoulder to shoulder along the walls and on the floor and up the steps of the stair, and Tamm was sitting on the floor near the front with her knees drawn up, looking at the map.

Everybody on the northern Flats knew about the lances. There were sixty of them, in silos across the spur and the inner and second belts, Phlegethon-fed beams built eight thousand years ago to keep monsters out of the geometry above Depth 40, and they could reach anything holding station over the Flats. Every soldier of the Third had seen them fire. They fired every cycle, at the change of the second watch, at whatever was passing overhead, Stymphalian swarms or Harpy flocks or things out of the deep with no names, a long, thin, blinding beam of orange light that went up from the ground into the dark and touched the underside of the ceiling and was gone. They'd been doing it for eight thousand years. The Archflamen hadn't stopped them. Why would he?

"Ceiling-Lance Seven," the centurion said again. His name was Barrow, and he was an Old Ladder, forty-four transfers, a heavy, slow, scarred man with a voice like gravel being stirred. "Silo cut forty metres into the basalt on Leaf 50-27, in the second belt, opposite the *29th*'s front. Nearest lance to our line on the whole of the northern Flats. Protected on the surface by a belt of razor-wire, a dense minefield, and four gun emplacements, here, here, here and here." He tapped them. "The Répertoire wants it dead."

"Why that one?" somebody asked, from the stair.

"Because it's the nearest. And because if Lance Seven goes, a corridor opens through the lance envelope over the northern Flats, right over Ore Head." Barrow traced it on the map with his finger. "The Fleet's been holding a heavy cruiser at readiness in the low geometry above Ore Head for three cycles, waiting for that corridor. The *Thunder of Iolcus*. The moment Lance Seven's down, she comes down through the corridor and parks over the second belt, and she brings her main guns to bear, and she does in a cycle what the Phaethons have been trying to do for a rotation and a half." He looked around the dugout. "That's why. Lance Seven goes, the cruiser comes, the second belt breaks, and we all get to go home."

There was a silence in the dugout. Tamm could feel it. Two hundred and sixty breachers who had been in the line for five rotations and had heard *we all get to go home* before.

"How?" somebody asked.

Barrow told them.

---

There would be no bombardment. That was the first thing. Every assault the Third had made on the Archflamen's belts in five rotations had been preceded by a bombardment, and every bombardment had told the Archflamen's gunners exactly where the assault was coming and when, and the Répertoire had decided, at last, to try something else. The raid on Lance Seven would go in silent.

Two hundred and sixty breachers of the *29th* would go out across no-man's land at the start of the dark watch. They would go without their plate. Two hundred kilograms of steel couldn't crawl across a kilometre of mud in silence. They'd go in fatigues, with their faces blacked with cinder, carrying carbines, grenades, trench knives, wire-cutters and breaching charges, and they would crawl from crater to crater through the ground between the lines until they reached the wire around Lance Seven. They'd cut through the wire, and seize the surface works of the silo at the first bell, and kill the gun emplacements, and then go down into the silo itself and destroy the lance with breaching charges. When the lance was dead, they would send a signal. On the signal, the *29th*'s cohorts would advance in daylight and occupy the ground around it.

"No plate," somebody said, when Barrow had finished.

"No plate."

"Forty metres down a silo. In the dark. Without plate."

"Yes."

"Against the garrison."

"Against whatever's down there," Barrow said. "Nobody knows what's down there. Nobody's been inside a lance silo in eight thousand years. The Arsenal's plans show the silo, the access shaft and the emitter chamber at the bottom. They don't show the garrison." He looked around the dugout again, slowly, at all of them. "I won't lie to you. The Répertoire's estimate is a hundred and eighty transfers among the breachers. It thinks we'll lose most of the first wave at the wire. It's priced it. It thinks the lance is worth it."

"Is it?" Tamm said.

She didn't know why she said it. She'd been sitting on the floor near the front, listening, and it came out of her mouth before she'd decided to say anything. Barrow looked down at her.

"Breacher Tamm," he said. "How many?"

"Nineteen, Centurion."

"Nineteen." He nodded slowly. "You know what happens at twenty."

"Yes, Centurion."

"Then you know that if you go up on this raid, you'll be off the line for good. Nobody'll blame you for that. Nobody'll blame you if you'd rather not go. You can stay in the support line tonight and I'll put somebody else in your place."

Tamm looked up at him. She thought about the register on Valcourt's table, and the four-metre list on the wall at the Watchpoint, and Garro, who had taught her to play dice.

"I'll go, Centurion," she said.

---

**NO-MAN'S LAND, LEAF 50-27. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 401, DARK WATCH**

They went out at the start of the dark watch, in sections of ten, over the parapet of the *29th*'s forward trench and down onto the ash, and Tamm crawled for three hours through mud and the dead.

There had been a seep-storm two cycles before. The ash between the lines on Leaf 50-27 had turned to a grey paste a metre deep, and it hadn't dried, and it sucked at her elbows and her knees and her belly as she crawled through it and filled her sleeves and her collar and the legs of her fatigues until she weighed twice what she should have. She crawled from crater to crater. Every crater on Leaf 50-27 had water at the bottom, black and warm and stinking, and every crater had the dead in it. Some of them were the Third's, from the Saturation, the ones the vats hadn't needed to retrieve because their patterns had already gone up. Most of them were thralls. They'd been lying there for a rotation, in the mud, and the Phlegethon rats had been at them, and Tamm crawled over them and through them and once, for a long minute while a flare hung overhead, lay among them with her face a hand's breadth from the face of a thrall who had been dead for a very long time.

The flares came every few minutes. The Archflamen's gunners on the second belt fired them over the ground between the lines all night, white magnesium flares that hung on little parachutes and lit the whole of Leaf 50-27 in a hard white light, and whenever a flare burst, every breacher of the *29th* lay still, wherever they were, in whatever was under them, and waited for it to burn out.

Halfway across, they heard shooting off to the left.

It was short and sharp, a few seconds of carbine fire and a grenade and then silence, and Tamm lay in the mud of a crater with her section around her and waited for the flares and the guns. Nothing came. She found out later what it had been. One of the sections on the left had crawled straight into an Arsenal patrol coming the other way, in the dark, at three metres, and both sides had fought it out with knives and carbines in a crater for a minute until the patrol was dead and so were four of the section. The sentries on the second belt heard it, and took it for the usual business of the dark watch, a patrol fight, which happened every night along the whole of the arc, and went back to their flares.

At the third hour, Tamm's section reached the wire around Lance Seven.

---

It loomed up out of the dark in front of her, a low black wall of razor-wire that ran away to her left and right as far as she could see, three belts of it, each belt two metres deep. Beyond it, two hundred metres off, she could see the surface works of the silo, a low squat block of concrete on a mound of slag, with the black square shapes of the four gun emplacements around it. And beyond that, faint and far off, the long dark line of the second belt.

There were six other sections at the wire already. She could hear them, the soft *snick* of the cutters going through the wire, one strand at a time, wrapped in cloth to muffle them. Her own section's cutters went forward on their bellies, two of them, and began to cut.

It took an hour to get through the first belt. She lay in the mud at the foot of the wire with her carbine under her and her face in the ash and listened to the cutters work, *snick*, *snick*, *snick*, one strand at a time, and to her heart, which was going so loudly she was sure the sentries on the emplacements could hear it, and she thought about nothing at all, and waited.

They were through the first belt. They were into the second.

A flare burst directly overhead.

It burst with a soft pop, high above Lance Seven, and the hard white light came down on the wire and the ash and the seven sections of breachers lying at the foot of it and in the gaps of the first belt, and Tamm pressed herself into the mud and held her breath and waited for it to burn out, and it didn't. It hung there. It hung over Lance Seven on its little parachute and drifted slowly toward the second belt on the hot air from the Phlegethon, and burned, and burned.

In the nearest gun emplacement, a hundred and fifty metres away, a sentry looked out over the wire, and saw them.

She heard him shout. She heard a bell, a big brass alarm bell, start clanging in the emplacement. She heard the gun.

"Up!" Barrow was roaring somewhere to her left. "Up, up! Through the wire! At them! At the emplacements! If you lie there you're dead! Go!"

She went.

---

The rest of the night was the emplacements. She remembered pieces of it, afterward, in the vats, and later. She remembered tearing through the second belt of wire with her hands, as the cutters hadn't finished, and the wire opening her palms and her forearms. She remembered the third belt, which the breachers blew with a breaching charge, and the blast of it throwing her flat in the mud. She remembered running at the nearest emplacement across two hundred metres of ash with the gun in it firing at her, and the breachers around her going down, and the minefield. She remembered the minefield. The breacher running beside her, a quiet older man named Doss who had been a stonemason on a ceiling world before the Third, stepped on a mine twenty metres from the emplacement and was there, beside her, and then wasn't.

She remembered throwing a grenade through the slit of the emplacement and the gun stopping.

She remembered fighting in the emplacement in the dark, with the garrison soldiers who had manned it, with her carbine and then her knife, and she remembered killing two of them, and she remembered the long confused fight across the surface works of the silo that came after, from one emplacement to the next, with the alarm bells clanging and the flares going up and the whole of Leaf 50-27 lit by it, and the garrison coming up out of the silo's access shaft to meet them in the dark.

She remembered the second emplacement going quiet. And the third. And then the garrison coming up out of the silo in its hundreds, and the breachers falling back toward the second emplacement, and Barrow shouting, and then Barrow not shouting.

And she remembered, near the end, lying behind the wrecked gun of the second emplacement with four rounds left and her carbine jammed and her knife gone, looking out at the smoke that was drifting up off the burning surface works and covering everything, and thinking, quite clearly, *twenty*. *This is twenty. I'm going off the line.*

Then something hit her on the back of the head, and the smoke went black.

---

**THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE 29TH. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 402, FIRST BELL**

At the first bell the silo was hidden in smoke, and no signal had been received, and Ines Valcourt stood in the forward aid post of the *29th* with her medici around her and listened to the regimental net.

The breachers' bands were jammed. They'd been jammed since the third hour, when the shooting started at Lance Seven. Nobody in the *29th* knew whether the surface works had been taken or not. The colonel had asked the *9th* Line Phalanx what to do, and the *9th* had asked the Répertoire, and the Répertoire had estimated, from the duration and intensity of the fighting, that the surface works had probably been taken and the breachers were probably in the silo, and that the attack should continue as planned.

"*All cohorts, Twenty-Ninth*," the colonel said on the net. "*Advance on Leaf 50-27 at the second bell. Occupy the ground around Lance Seven. The breachers are in the silo.*"

Valcourt looked at the overlay. Leaf 50-27 was a thick grey smudge of smoke, with the red line of the second belt behind it.

"They're not in the silo," she said.

The orderly beside her looked at her.

"Medicus?"

"If they were in the silo, the lance would be dead, and they'd have sent the signal on a runner. It's been five hours." She looked at the smoke. "They're not in the silo. They're lying in that smoke somewhere, what's left of them. And the garrison knows we're coming."

At the second bell, the *29th*'s cohorts went over the top toward the smoke.

---

The Arsenal's gunners on the second belt had been watching Leaf 50-27 since the third hour of the dark watch. They had seen the flares and heard the bells and the shooting at Lance Seven, and they knew exactly what the Third was trying to do, and when the cohorts of the *29th* came out of their trenches in daylight and started across the ash toward the smoke, the Arsenal's gunners turned Leaf 50-27 into a slaughterhouse.

Valcourt didn't see it. She heard it. She heard it for two hours from the forward aid post, the guns of the second belt firing without pause, and then she saw the wounded start to come back, crawling, walking, carried, in a long broken stream down the communication trenches into the aid post, and after that she didn't hear anything for a long time because she was working.

At the end of the second hour, the colonel called the survivors back. They came back across ground covered with their own dead.

---

The next cycle the Third's spotters found the surface works of Lance Seven scorched and two of its emplacements wrecked. The emitter was intact. At the change of the second watch it fired, as it had every cycle for eight thousand years, a long thin blinding beam of orange light that went up from the second belt into the dark and touched a Stymphalian swarm passing over the northern Flats, and burned it out of the air.

Six hundred and forty soldiers of the *29th* had been transferred in the raid. Of the two hundred and sixty breachers, nineteen came back. The Répertoire listed thirty-one as taken.

Iska Tamm was one of them.

---

**THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE 29TH. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 406**

The thirty-one reappeared four cycles later, at first light, tied upright to the wire stakes in front of Lance Seven, three hundred metres from the *29th*'s forward trench.

Valcourt saw them through a periscope in the forward trench, where she'd gone because the sentries had called for a medicus and nobody else would come. They stood in a long line along the front of the wire, thirty-one of them, a few metres apart, lashed to the iron stakes at the chest and the waist and the ankles, in their filthy fatigues with their faces still blacked with cinder. Some of them had their heads down. Some of them were looking straight across the three hundred metres of ash at the Third's line.

They were alive. She could see them breathing. She could see one of them, near the middle of the line, move her head slowly from side to side.

"Gods," said the sentry beside her. "Gods. What are they doing? Why are they still alive?"

Valcourt didn't answer. She was looking at the back of the nearest one's head, through the periscope, at the highest magnification it had. The breacher's head was down, chin on chest, and the back of the neck was exposed, and on it, at the base of the skull, where the implant sat, there was a dressing.

It was a clean white dressing, a pad of lint held on with a strip of bandage around the neck. Somebody had put it there with care. And it was stained through, all over, with something dark.

She moved the periscope along the line. The next one had a dressing too. And the next. Every one of them, thirty-one of them, had a clean white dressing at the base of the skull, stained through with something dark that wasn't blood.

"Get me the colonel," she said.

---

The colonel came. He looked through the periscope for a long time, and then he went back to his dugout and asked the *9th* Line Phalanx for permission to make a rescue, and the *9th* asked the Répertoire, and the Répertoire answered in eleven seconds.

*Query received. Rescue of captured personnel, Leaf 50-27. Assessment: enemy has ranged all approaches. Probability of recovery of any captive: under 0.05. Estimated cost: 2,400 transfers. Captives will transfer in any case on death. Permission refused. Ave Imperator.*

Valcourt read it on the pane in the colonel's dugout, standing behind his chair.

"They won't," she said.

The colonel turned in his chair.

"Medicus?"

"They won't transfer in any case. Sir, I've looked at them. Every one of them has a dressing at the base of the skull. Over the implant. Somebody has done something to their implants, sir, surgically, and dressed the wound, and kept them alive. You don't keep thirty-one prisoners alive for four cycles and tie them to your wire and dress their wounds unless you want something from them when they die." She was surprised at how steady her voice was. "The Répertoire's assessment is wrong. If we leave them there, they won't come back."

The colonel looked at her for a long time. He was a decent man, she thought. He'd told her to ignore the register because the alternative was having no regiment, and she had agreed with him, and she still did.

"Write it down," he said quietly. "All of it. Everything you've seen. I'll send it up to the Phalanx with my own request, and they can send it up to the Répertoire, and the Répertoire can read it." He hesitated. "I can't order a rescue, Medicus. You know that. Not against a refusal. Not with that cost on it."

"I know, sir."

"Write it down."

She wrote it down. It went up to the *9th* Line Phalanx that watch, and from there to Quarterdeck Nineteen, and from there to the Répertoire.

The answer came back eleven seconds after it arrived.

*Dissent received. Ref. 9LP-29-0406. Noted. Assessment stands.*

---

They died on the wire one at a time over the following week.

The first died on the second night. Valcourt was in the forward trench when the *29th*'s relay operators logged it. She'd asked them to tell her, and they did. At the fourth hour of the dark watch, the implant of a breacher of the *29th* named Kerris fired, on Leaf 50-27, and the operators' instruments followed the upload into the noosphere, as they always did, a tiny bright blip on the pane leaving the relay band and going up.

It went up for seven seconds. Then it was gone.

It reached neither the next pylon nor the Watchpoint. It went into the noosphere over the second belt and stopped, as if a hand had closed around it, and the operators' instruments lost it, and it never came back.

"Null," the operator said quietly. He was a young man, a signaller, and he was looking at the pane with his mouth slightly open. "It's... Medicus, it's gone. It went up and then it just... stopped."

"I know," Valcourt said. "Write it down."

She watched them die for a week. She watched through the periscope in the forward trench in the daylight, and she listened to the relay operators in the dark. She watched them hang on the wire in the heat, without water, slowly, one after another. She watched the Phlegethon rats come to the ones who had stopped moving. Each time one of them died, the relay operators logged the implant firing and the upload entering the noosphere, and each time the upload went up for seven seconds and stopped.

On the fourth night, she went out.

---

She went out without telling or asking anybody, over the parapet of the forward trench at the third hour of the dark watch, alone, in fatigues, with her face blacked with cinder and a medical satchel on her back and a canteen of water, and she crawled for three hours through the mud and the dead of Leaf 50-27, as the breachers had crawled, from crater to crater, lying still under the flares.

She knew which one she was going to. She'd been watching her through the periscope for four cycles. She was near the middle of the line, a tall broad-shouldered young woman with cropped dark hair, the one who moved her head slowly from side to side, and on the second day Valcourt had looked her up in the regimental roll and found her name and her count in the black ledger on the folding table of the aid post.

*Tamm, Iska. Breacher, Heavy 1st Maniple. 19. Says she doesn't know if she wants it.*

She reached the wire at the sixth hour. She lay in the mud at the foot of the stake for a long time, waiting for a flare to burn out. When it was dark again, she got up on her knees.

"Breacher," she whispered. "Breacher Tamm."

Tamm's head came up slowly. Her eyes were open. They were red and swollen and almost shut, and her lips were cracked and black, and her face under the cinder was grey. She looked down at Valcourt for a long time before she knew her.

"Medicus," she said. Her voice was a dry thread. "Medicus. What are you... you shouldn't..."

"Drink." Valcourt held the canteen up to her lips. Tamm drank, choking. Valcourt let her have a little, and then a little more, and then took it away. "Slowly. Slowly."

Tamm drank again. Then she leaned her head back against the iron stake and closed her eyes.

"You came out," she said.

"I came out."

"Nobody else came."

"No." Valcourt reached up and touched the dressing at the back of Tamm's neck. It was dry, and stiff, and the dark stain on it had set hard and glossy. She lifted the edge of it, gently, with her fingertips, and smelled it.

Cold. Cold, and something like a river in winter, and under that, something worse, something old and black and heavy that went into her nose and down into her chest and made her want to be sick.

"What did they do?" she whispered. "Tamm. What did they do to you?"

Tamm didn't open her eyes.

"A man," she said. "In a grey coat. And a woman. A keyer. Hephaestean. She was crying." Her voice faded and came back. "They cut. At the back. They put something in. Black. It was cold. The man said..." She stopped.

"What did he say?"

"He said it wouldn't hurt when it was over." Tamm opened her eyes and looked down at Valcourt. "He said we'd go somewhere else. Not the vats. He said there was somewhere else that wanted us more."

Valcourt knelt in the mud at the foot of the stake and looked up at her.

"I can't cut you down," she said. "I'm sorry. I can't carry you back alone. If I try, they'll see us, and they'll shoot us both, and you'll go up anyway, and I'll go up with you." She found that her voice was shaking now. "I can stay a while. That's all I can do."

"Medicus."

"Yes."

"Have you got a grenade?"

Valcourt stared at her.

"If I go up quick," Tamm said, "before the black stuff... maybe it doesn't get me. Maybe I get away." Her voice cracked. "I don't want to go somewhere else, Medicus. I want to go home. I want to go to the vats. I want to be twenty."

Valcourt didn't have a grenade. Medici didn't carry them. She knelt at the foot of the stake in the dark and held Tamm's tied hand through the wire as far as she could reach it, and stayed with her until the first grey of the next watch began to show on the horizon over the second belt, and then she had to go.

---

Iska Tamm died on the wire on the sixth night, at the second hour of the dark watch. The *29th*'s relay operators logged her implant firing and her upload entering the noosphere.

It went up for seven seconds. Then it stopped.

---

The thirty-first died on the eighth night. Their bodies remained on the wire for a rotation afterward, until the Arsenal's thralls took them down and threw them into the ditch in front of Lance Seven.

The Répertoire's technical officers reported to the Command at Watchpoint Hades that the Archflamen's forces had found a means to interfere with the transfer of captured soldiers through work on the implant. They reported that the work was beyond the competence of a forge-hold's armourers. They attached Medicus Valcourt's dissent, and the *29th*'s relay logs, and asked whether the Arsenal was receiving help.

The Command noted the report.

The Tartarus Fleet had held the heavy cruiser *Thunder of Iolcus* at readiness in the low geometry above Ore Head for three cycles before the raid, waiting for the corridor that the destruction of Lance Seven would open. On the cycle after the raid, Navarch Iole Saturnin withdrew her, with a note to the Command that the Fleet would not bring ships into the lance envelope until the Third could guarantee a corridor. The Third's hopes of naval support for the second belt were closed.

The fate of the thirty-one became known along the whole of the arc within a rotation. Raids for prisoners continued. But soldiers on the line began to carry a last grenade, on a cord around the neck, under the fatigues, for themselves. The Third's officers forbade it. Its centurions ignored the order, and many of them carried one too.

Ines Valcourt carried one for the rest of the siege. She never told anyone why.

**THE GUEST QUARTERS OF THE TABULARIUM. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 410, DARK WATCH**

Ilan Dace sat on the floor of Mirren's room in the dark with his back to the wall and his notebook on his knee, and waited for the last of the thirty-one to die.

There were five hundred and twelve strays in his care now. He had counted them that morning. They lived in the guest quarters of the Tabularium and in the old Enforcement barracks beside it, which Dace had had whitewashed and furnished with beds, and the Archflamen sent them food from his own table every night, and the Flamens said the rite over them every morning. Some of them were the original three hundred and eleven from the pens. Most of them were new. They were the thralls' children, from the barracks of the casting lines and the camps on the slopes of the cone, and Dace had found them himself, one by one, by going among the thralls' families with a lamp and asking the mothers whether their children ever heard things.

A great many of them did. The witch-blood showed in the thralls' children far more often than the Arsenal's registry had ever recorded. It had never been looked for. The Hephaestean orders had bought the thralls by the shipload from the debt-rolls of the ceiling, and nobody had ever thought to check whether a forge-thrall's daughter could hear the noosphere, because nobody had ever imagined it would matter.

It mattered now. Through Rotations Three and Four, and into Five, the strays had listened to the Third's traffic on the noosphere bands for him, every cycle, for hours. They told him when the traffic grew heavy before an attack, and on which stretch of the line, and he passed it to the Prefect, and the Prefect, who was told that the warnings came from prayer, had learned to trust them. The Arsenal's warning of the Third's assaults came as often from the guest quarters of the Tabularium as from its own sentries.

Some of the strays had sickened under the work. A few had died. He had replaced them.

And since the night Mirren heard Colonel Halde calling out his number, they had been learning to listen for something else.

---

It was harder than he'd hoped. He had known it would be. Mirren had heard Halde go up, and heard him call his number, and heard him caught, and that night in the records room Dace had thought, for an hour or two, that the thing he wanted might be close.

It wasn't. It had taken two rotations.

An upload in the noosphere carried the whole pattern of a soldier, everything he had ever known. A stray who could hear one could tell whose it was, and in time, with practice, could tell some of what it carried. A stray who could hear one could also, it turned out, reach for it. Mirren had been the first to try, and for a long time she had failed every time. The uploads went past her too fast. She heard them go up, and reached, and they were gone, up into the noosphere, toward the Watchpoint, or toward whatever else it was that caught them.

Then, in the fourth rotation, she had touched one.

It was only for a moment. A trooper of the *44th*, killed in a raid on the second belt, going up off the Flats in the dark watch, and Mirren had reached for it and felt it, felt the whole of it brush against her as it went past, and she had screamed and screamed and not stopped screaming for an hour. When she could speak, she told Dace the trooper's name, and his mother's name, and the name of the ceiling world he had been born on, and the colour of the door of the house he grew up in. Dace checked the Tabularium's files. Every word was true.

After that she had touched them more often. She held one for two seconds, then three, then five. The other strays learned from her, slowly, a few of them at a time, the most sensitive. And then, at the Saturation, with eleven thousand uploads going up off the Flats over six cycles, Mirren had reached for one and held it for twenty seconds before it tore itself free.

That was when Dace had understood what he needed.

He'd gone to the Prefect, without telling him why, and asked for prisoners.

---

The thirty-one had been the answer to a question. He'd needed soldiers of the Third who would die slowly, in a known place, at a known distance from the Tabularium, one at a time, so that Mirren and the others could listen for them and be ready. He had needed them to die close. The Third's soldiers who died in the trenches died eleven kilometres away, without warning, and their uploads went up past the strays in an instant. The thirty-one would die three hundred metres in front of Lance Seven, slowly, over a week, and every one of them would be heard coming.

And he had needed the black.

He thought about the black now, sitting on the floor of Mirren's room in the dark. He thought about the Hephaestean keyer who had cut the backs of thirty-one necks for him in the cellars of the Tabularium, weeping the whole time, and about the black stuff from the dark flask that he had poured into each cut. He'd had the first flask from the Styx road at Depth 44 in the months before the revolt, when the Sworn still went down to the river to take their oaths, and he had gone on sending parties of a dozen down the road ever since, and most of them came back with river-water on their boots and fewer men than they'd left with. Whatever waited at the bottom of the road wanted things that Dace was still learning to name. In exchange, it gave him the black.

The black didn't stop an upload. Lictor-Superior Rusk had shown him that, on the bridge, on the first night. The implant still fired, and the pattern still went up into the noosphere. But it went up slowly, and heavily, and it went up *marked*, and something in the noosphere over the Arsenal that was not the Répertoire knew the mark and closed its hand around it. He'd thought, at first, that the something was the river. He'd thought so for a long time.

He had begun, lately, to suspect that it was the Mast.

---

Across the room, Mirren sat cross-legged on her bed with her eyes closed and her hands on her knees, listening.

She was thirteen now. She had grown, a little, over two rotations, but not as much as she should have. She was thin, and pale, and there were dark marks under her eyes that never went away, and she slept badly and woke screaming at least once every night. She rarely laughed anymore, though she still let him hold her hands.

"He's going," she said suddenly. "The last one. He's going now."

Dace put down his notebook.

"Listen for him," he said softly. "Reach for him. Like we practised. Like the others."

She'd reached for the other thirty. She'd held most of them for a few seconds, and a few of them for longer, and every one of them had torn free and gone up and been caught by whatever caught them over the Arsenal, before she could keep them. But she had been getting better. She had been getting better every night of the week.

Mirren's face went still. Her hands tightened on her knees.

"I've got him," she said.

Dace didn't move.

"I've got him. He's... he's big. He's heavy. He's a breacher. He's frightened." Her voice was strange, flat and far away. "He's called Edric. Edric Tash. He's from a place called Tyre's Fall, on a ceiling world, there's a harbour, there's boats... he's thinking about his sister. He's trying to go up. He's pulling. He's pulling so hard, Ilan, he's pulling..."

"Hold him," Dace said.

"I'm holding him." Tears were running down her face. "I'm holding him. He's screaming. He wants to go home. He wants to go home to the vats and I'm holding him and he's screaming..."

"Hold him, Mirren."

She held him.

Dace sat on the floor and watched her, with his notebook closed on his knee, and counted under his breath. Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty. Mirren's whole body was shaking. Her lips were moving, saying something over and over that he couldn't hear. Forty. Fifty.

At fifty-four seconds, she screamed, and let go.

He felt nothing. He never did. He had none of the witch-blood, and had never had any; he heard nothing of the noosphere and never would. But he saw Mirren's face, and how her hands came open on her knees, and he knew that Edric Tash of Tyre's Fall had torn himself free and gone up, into the noosphere, over the Arsenal, toward whatever was waiting.

Mirren fell sideways on the bed and lay there, sobbing.

Dace got up and went to her and sat on the edge of the bed and held her hand, gently, as he always did, until she stopped.

"Fifty-four seconds," he said quietly, when she could hear him.

She didn't answer.

"Mirren. Do you understand what that means?"

She shook her head against the pillow.

"An upload passes seven seconds in the open," Dace said, "between the implant and the first relay. Seven seconds. That's all the time anything has to reach it, before it's safe in the relays and on its way home. And you held one for fifty-four." He stroked her hair. "If you can hold one for a minute, you can hold one until it's empty."

She looked up at him then, from the pillow, with her red swollen eyes.

"Empty?" she said.

"Empty," Dace said. "Until there's nothing left in it to go home."

He wrote it in his notebook, under a long column of other numbers, in his neat clerk's hand. *R5 c410. M. Last of the 31. 54 seconds.* Then he closed the book, and kissed her on the forehead, and went to tell the Archflamen.
