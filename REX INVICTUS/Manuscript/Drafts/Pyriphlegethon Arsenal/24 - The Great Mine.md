## Chapter 24: The Great Mine

*"My father made the first hammer. I've only got the one hand to hold it with, so I made a lever instead. It does the same job."*

*Sapper-Major Emlyn Gault,* 61st *Sapper Company, Fourth Sapper Regiment of the Forge*

---

**BENEATH THE MORA CUT. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 120**

The ground in the north was better, and Sapper-Prefect Hadria Voss went to see it for herself on the hundred and twentieth cycle of Rotation Eleven, eleven cycles after the first battle for Vault 9 had gone onto the Deep List.

She went down the shaft of the *14th* Sapper Company on Leaf 41-19, behind the *2nd* Line Phalanx's front on the edge of the Mora Cut, at ninety-one years old, on the iron ladders, barefoot, with a hooded lamp on her belt and the company's sapper-captain going down ahead of her and looking up every few rungs in a state of visible terror. She ignored him. She'd been going down ladders since she was fourteen. At the bottom she stood in the gallery and put her palm flat against the wall, and kept it there, and closed her eyes.

"Dry," she said.

"Yes, Prefect."

"Cool. Cooler than the south. The basalt's nearer the surface." She opened her eyes. "Forty metres?"

"Thirty-eight, Prefect, at the shaft head. It comes up toward the Cut. Under the Archflamen's pits on Leaf 41-19 it's thirty-one."

Voss nodded slowly.

"Good," she said. "We'll do it the old way here, then. The way the schools teach it."

The old way was the classic one of the Hephaestean schools. Small mines, a great many of them, driven separately beneath the enemy's strongpoints in the line in front of an attack, each one aimed at one pillbox or one gun pit or one bunker, laid in silence and tamped and wired, and all of them fired together at the moment the infantry went over the top on the surface. Every sapper of the Forge learned it in the sapper hall before they learned anything else. It was how the Forge had taken the Ice Gate at Hold Charybdis, and the Lantern Bastion on the Acheron, and the nine hundred strongpoints of the Grey Stairs; and the Cinder Flats, Voss said, were going to learn it too.

Between the hundred and twentieth and the three hundred and fortieth cycles of Rotation Eleven, the northern sapper regiments fired forty-one mines on the front of the *2nd* and *5th* Line Phalanxes, under the Archflamen's works between the inner belt and the Cinder Wall along the Mora Cut.

Twenty-nine destroyed their targets. Eight were found by the Frost Masons and destroyed by counter-mines before they could be fired. Four were fired too late to help the attacks they had been dug for.

On the leaves where the mines worked, the northern Line Phalanxes gained more than two kilometres in those two hundred and twenty cycles, at a cost in transfers less than a third of what the same ground would have cost them on the surface alone. Kastellan read the figures on Quarterdeck Nineteen at the end of the rotation, and Dray watched him read them, and saw him put the slate down and sit back in his chair with an expression Dray had never seen on his face.

"Well," Kastellan said. "There it is."

It was the clearest evidence in the whole siege that the war beneath could succeed. And it put eleven thousand names on the Deep List.

Kastellan read those too.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 350**

"No," Voss said.

She was sitting across the desk from him again, as she had on the two hundred and fiftieth cycle of Rotation Ten, with a roll of survey sheets under her arm, and this time there were two other Sapper-Prefects of the Forge sitting beside her, the commanders of the Second and Sixth Sapper Regiments, and none of them looked pleased.

"I haven't told you what I want yet," Kastellan said.

"You want the Wall." Voss unrolled the top sheet of the survey on the desk and weighted it with the lump of slag. "Everybody on the Flats knows you want the Wall, Marshal. You've wanted it since the cycle you got here. And you want to do it with one mine."

Kastellan looked at her for a moment. Then he looked at the survey.

The Cinder Wall ran across it in a long dark curve round the foot of the cone. Thirty metres high and twelve thick, built of the Arsenal's cast basalt by the Arsenal's founders, with bunkers and barracks inside it and guns along its top, and over the rotations of the siege the Frost Masons had laid trenches and pillboxes in front of it, so that it was now one more line of works in front of the Third, thinner than the three belts and still needing to be assaulted. Behind it, the shanty-town. Behind that, the cone, and the Acropolis.

He'd always meant to mine it. He'd said so to Voss on the first cycle, and he'd been planning it since. Phaethon fire and the Wrath bombers might bring it down in time, but the Phaethons were needed on the front and the bombers were needed over the Nest, and until the Third's trenches were close enough to engage the Wall over open sights, a great mine was the best chance of a breach he had.

"Here," he said, and put his finger on Leaf 57-44.

"One mine," Voss said.

"One mine."

"Under the Wall's footings." She looked at the survey, and back at him. "Marshal, the Wall's footings were cast by the Arsenal's founders to stand on the shelf for ten thousand years. They go down eighty metres into the spur. One mine big enough to break them would be the largest charge the Forge has ever laid. It'd take a rotation to dig and another to load, and it'd empty the *7th* Line Phalanx's stores to fill it, and the Frost Masons would hear it coming before it was half done. They hear everything. You'd get one attempt, and if they found it, you'd have nothing."

"And the alternative?"

"A series," said the Prefect of the Second Regiment, a heavy square man with a burned face named Owain Teller. "Small mines along the Wall's length. As we've done in the north. Twenty of them, thirty. The Frost Masons will find some. They'll never find all. Whatever they miss, we fire together, and we get breaches all along the Wall, and the Third goes through all of them at once."

Kastellan was silent for a while.

"They'll find most of a series long before it's ready," he said at last. "Prefect, you've told me yourself, every one of you, that the Frost Masons are the best miners in Tartarus. Better than any enemy you've ever fought. In the north they were stretched thin, and you beat them. Under the Wall they won't be. They'll have everything they've got under the Wall, because they know as well as I do that the Wall's what I want. A series means thirty galleries under the Wall, all being dug at once. Thirty sets of picks. Thirty chances for them to hear you." He tapped Leaf 57-44. "One mine dug in silence, behind a diversion loud enough to hold their attention somewhere else, has a better chance of reaching the footings without being found than thirty mines dug anywhere."

"A diversion," Voss said.

"Somewhere else on the Wall. Heavy borers. Loud ones. Driven straight at the Wall where they can be heard from the cone. Make the Frost Masons think it's the main effort, and make them throw everything they have against it, and while they do..." He moved his finger eight hundred metres along the Wall. "One company, at Leaf 57-44, digging in silence."

The three Sapper-Prefects looked at each other.

"The company on the diversion," Voss said slowly. "Do you know what you're asking of it, Marshal?"

"Yes."

"The Frost Masons will throw everything they have at it. You've said so yourself. That's the point." Her voice was quite level. "It'll be destroyed."

"I know."

Voss looked at him a long time.

"You'll get your mine," she said at last. "We'll dig it. We'll dig your diversion too. And I'll write to my lords at the Forge tonight and tell them that the three of us objected, and why, and they'll put it in the record. That's my right." She began to roll up the survey. "I hope you're right, Marshal. I truly do."

"So do I," Kastellan said.

**THE SHAFT HEAD BEHIND LEAF 57-44. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 360**

Sapper-Major Emlyn Gault of the *61st* Sapper Company had one hand, and he made the most of it.

He'd lost the other in the first rotation of the war beneath, on the three hundred and ninetieth cycle of Rotation Ten, under Leaf 55-38, to a Frost Mason's ice dart that had come through the gallery wall beside him with no warning at all. It had taken his left hand off at the wrist, cleanly, and sealed the stump with frost as it went, so that he'd never bled; and the medicus at the *22nd*'s aid station who had looked at the stump afterward had said that she'd never seen such a wound, and that he was the luckiest man on the Flats. Gault had told her that he had a different word for it. In place of the hand, now, he wore a thing of bronze and steel he'd made for himself in the Forge's field workshops on the outer belt: a cuff over the stump, with a socket in it, into which he could fit a hook or a clamp or a pick-head or a short bronze lever as the work required. He was a son of Hephaestus. He made things. It was what his father had made him for.

He was a big, heavy, slow-moving man of fifty, black-bearded, with the god's limp in his right leg that all of Hephaestus's children seemed to have in some degree, and huge, scarred, blunt-fingered hands, or had had, and a habit of humming tunelessly under his breath when he was thinking. The *61st* had learned to take the humming as a sign. When the major stopped humming, the *61st* said, it was time to start worrying.

He stood at the shaft head behind Leaf 57-44 on the three hundred and sixtieth cycle, behind a false wall of sandbags in the *35th* Trench Regiment's support trench, and listened to Voss explain to him what Kastellan wanted.

He didn't hum. Voss noticed.

"Major?" she said.

"Under the Wall's footings," Gault said. "Eighty-six metres down, by the survey. A chamber under the footings. Big enough to take..." He frowned. "What's the charge?"

"The Forge's engineers say nineteen hundred tonnes of compound. Nothing less will break the footings."

Gault was silent a long time.

"Nineteen hundred tonnes," he said. "Carried down by hand."

"Carried down by hand. In silence. Twenty kilograms at a time." Voss looked at him. "Ninety-five thousand trips down the incline and along the gallery, Major, by sappers walking barefoot, without a sound, for however long it takes. With the Frost Masons listening on the other side of the rock the whole time."

"And they mustn't hear one of them."

"Not one."

Gault looked at the false wall, and the shaft head behind it, and the incline going down into the dark.

He started humming again.

"All right," he said. "When do we start?"

**THE GALLERY BENEATH LEAF 57-44. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 362 TO ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 118**

The *61st* began the gallery under Leaf 57-44 on the three hundred and sixty-second cycle of Rotation Eleven, and it took them a hundred and twenty cycles to dig it.

They dug it as no gallery had ever been dug on the Flats. The incline went down from the shaft head behind the false wall in the *35th*'s support trench at a long slow slope through the cinder and the pumice and the old lava, braced every metre of the way through the soft ground with props wrapped in sacking so they wouldn't creak, and at eighty metres it reached the basalt of the spur, and there the gallery began. Two sappers wide. A metre and a half high. It ran west under no-man's land toward the Wall, nine hundred metres, and it was cut by hand, by sappers working barefoot with picks wrapped in three layers of cloth, taking the basalt down in flakes no larger than a fingernail, in absolute silence.

There were no borers. There couldn't be. A borer could be heard from the cone.

There was no talking. Nobody in the *61st* spoke a word below ground in the whole hundred and twenty cycles; it was a rule, and Gault enforced it with his hook. They spoke by hand signs. The spoil went back along the gallery in sacks carried on the shoulder, never dragged, and up the incline, and was used to build the *35th*'s trenches above, and nobody in the *35th* was told where it came from. The listeners sat in their niches every ten metres along the gallery with their geophones against the rock, through every watch, listening, and twice a watch they came out and signed what they'd heard to Gault. Nothing. Nothing. A pick, far off, to the south; that was the diversion. Nothing.

Gault went down every watch. He walked the length of the gallery with his hooded lamp and his cuff fitted with the short bronze lever, touching the walls, and looked at every metre of the face, and listened. He was the only one who didn't go barefoot. He couldn't; his limp wouldn't let him. So he'd made himself a pair of boots in the field workshops with soles of layered felt three fingers thick, and they made no more sound on the basalt than a cat.

On the hundred and eighteenth cycle of Rotation Twelve, the face of the gallery reached the place where the survey said the Wall's footings began, eighty-six metres down, nine hundred metres from the shaft, and Gault knelt at the face and put his palm flat against the rock and held it there.

It was different. He felt it at once. Harder than the basalt round it, and smoother, and denser, with a strange faint warmth in it that didn't come from the river. He'd felt its kind once before, at the Forge, in the oldest of the deep cradles, where the god himself had worked. It was cast stone. The Arsenal's founders had poured it into the spur ten thousand years ago, to stand forever, and it had stood.

He knelt at the face for a long time with his hand on it, and the sappers of the face squad knelt beside him and waited.

Then he took his hand away and signed to them. *Chamber. Here. Under it.* And then, after a moment: *Start the carry.*

**THE INCLINE. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLES 118 TO 230**

The carry took a hundred and twelve cycles.

The compound came down the cables at Kappa from the Forge in sealed packs of twenty kilograms, grey and dense and heavy, each pack stamped with the hammer of the Forge and wrapped in felt, and went forward to the *35th*'s support trench by night in covered rail wagons, and was unloaded behind the false wall by sappers of the *61st* in silence. And from there it went down the incline on the sappers' backs.

Ninety-five thousand packs. One at a time. Down the long slope of the incline in the dark, barefoot, through the cinder and the pumice and the old lava, eighty metres, and along nine hundred metres of gallery a metre and a half high, bent double, to the chamber under the footings, where it was set down without a sound and stacked by hand. And back again. And again. Every sapper of the *61st* made the carry four times a watch, every watch, for a hundred and twelve cycles. When the *61st* couldn't do it alone, Voss lent Gault two more companies from the reserve, and they made it too.

They carried it as the chamber was cut. The chamber went in under the Wall's footings as the carry began, a vault in the basalt of the spur thirty metres long and twelve wide and four high, cut by hand with wrapped picks below the cast stone, and the packs went into it as fast as the face squads could clear the space, stacked from the floor to the roof in long grey walls. A second detonator was wired to the first, and a third to the second, in case the Frost Masons cut one of the cables. The firing cables ran back along the gallery and up the incline and out to the firing station, a dugout cut into the side of the *35th*'s support trench behind Leaf 57-44, with a pair of firing handles of bronze on its wall that Gault had made himself in the field workshops, and tested, and tested again.

It was the largest single charge the Forge's sappers had ever laid.

Gault went down every watch, and walked the gallery, and looked at the chamber filling, and listened.

He heard nothing. The listeners heard nothing. For a hundred and twelve cycles the Frost Masons never once drove a counter-mine toward the gallery under Leaf 57-44, and Gault walked it every watch and heard nothing, and knew why.

They were eight hundred metres to the south, under Leaf 56-46, killing the diversion.

**THE INCLINE. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 171**

Sapper Wynn Tallis was nineteen, and the carry was the first work she'd ever done for the Forge, and on the hundred and seventy-first cycle of Rotation Twelve she made her four hundred and twelfth trip down the incline with a pack of compound on her back and counted the steps.

She always counted them. Everyone in the *61st* did, she'd found, though nobody admitted it. Three hundred and eighty steps down the incline from the false wall to the foot, through the cinder and the pumice and the old lava, on the sacking that covered the floor, with the wrapped props going past her lamp on either side. Then the gallery. Two thousand and forty steps along it, bent double, to the chamber, if you kept the pace the major liked. Then the pack off your back and into the hands of the stackers at the chamber mouth without a sound, and the long walk back, and the next pack.

She'd come down the cables from Hold Ferrum in Rotation Eleven, on the last draft of the Fourth Regiment, with a hundred and nine other recruits who had all, like her, signed the roll at the sapper hall the cycle they turned eighteen, and been told by the sapper optio at the hall what the roll meant. *If you die deep, you stay deep.* She'd signed it anyway. Her mother had been a sapper, and her mother's brother. The pay was three times what the foundries paid, and the foundries at Hold Ferrum killed you slower but they killed you just the same.

She'd thought she'd be fighting. Everyone on the draft had. They'd heard about Vault 9 on the cables coming down, and the Cinderborn, and the Frost Masons, and half of them had been terrified and the other half had been pretending not to be. Instead the major had put them on the carry. For a hundred and seventy cycles she'd walked up and down the incline and the gallery with a pack on her back, four times a watch, and never seen an enemy, and never fired a shot, and never said a word below ground.

She'd stopped minding. That was the strange part. She'd found, sometime in the first fifty cycles, that she liked it.

It was the silence. She'd never known anything like it. In the foundries at Hold Ferrum you couldn't hear yourself think, and on the Flats it was worse; but on the carry, in the gallery under Leaf 57-44, there was nothing at all, only her breathing and the soft pad of her bare feet on the sacking and the breathing of the carrier in front and the carrier behind, a long slow line of them going down into the dark with their packs, and coming back. After a while the silence stopped being frightening and became something else, a thick soft quiet that wrapped her round, and she walked through it counting her steps and thinking about nothing, and it was the most peaceful she had ever been in her life.

She was at step one thousand six hundred and four along the gallery when the carrier in front of her stopped.

She stopped. Behind her, she felt the carrier behind stop too, and the one behind him, all along the line. Nobody made a sound. Ahead of her in the lamplight she could see the carrier in front, a sapper named Doul, standing bent double under his pack with his hand raised in the sign for *halt*, and beyond him, in a niche in the gallery wall, a listener with her geophone pressed to the rock and her eyes wide open.

The listener signed. Tallis read it over Doul's shoulder.

*Sound. Above. Close.*

Tallis stood in the dark under her twenty kilograms of compound and didn't breathe.

The listener held up her hand, flat, palm down. *Wait.* Then she shut her eyes again and pressed the geophone harder to the rock.

They waited. All along the gallery, the line of carriers waited, bent under their packs, in the silence. Tallis felt her legs begin to shake. She thought about the compound on her back, and the compound on the backs of the forty carriers in the gallery round her, and the eleven hundred tonnes of compound stacked in the chamber at the end of the gallery four hundred metres ahead. She thought about a Frost Mason somewhere in the rock above her, kneeling in a gallery of its own, with its grey hand pressed flat to the stone, listening.

A minute. Five. Ten.

Somebody came along the gallery behind her, from the direction of the shaft, slowly, with a hooded lamp. She didn't turn her head. She heard the soft felt tread of the boots, and the faint uneven rhythm of the limp, and knew who it was. Gault came past her in the gallery, sideways, pressed against the wall, and stopped at the listener's niche and knelt and put his good hand on the rock beside the geophone, and held it there.

He stayed like that for an hour.

At the end of the hour he took his hand off the rock and signed to the listener, and the listener signed back, and Gault nodded slowly. Then he turned and signed down the line of carriers. Tallis read it.

*Thrall. Lost. Walking. Gone now.*

And then, after a moment, a sign she'd never seen him make before, a short downward chop of the bronze lever in his cuff, that the older sappers told her afterward meant *carry on* in the sign-speech of the old sapper halls, an old sign that also meant, roughly, *we are still here.*

The line moved. Tallis walked on. She counted her steps to the chamber, all two thousand and forty of them, and set her pack down in the stackers' hands without a sound, and walked back.

That night, in the *61st*'s dugout on the surface, she found she couldn't stop shaking, and an old sapper named Meriel Toft sat down beside her on the bunk and put an arm round her shoulders and didn't say anything at all until it stopped.

"Is it always like that?" Tallis asked her, at last.

"No," Toft said. "Usually nobody comes to tell you it's only a thrall."

**BENEATH LEAF 56-46. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 190**

The *66th* Sapper Company had been given the diversion on the three hundred and sixtieth cycle of Rotation Eleven, the same cycle the *61st* was given the mine, and Ansel Rook had known what it meant from the moment the sapper-captain read the order out at the shaft head.

He'd been a sapper of the Forge for twelve years by then. He knew what a diversion was for.

They drove it with the borers. Four of the Forge's boring engines, the great tracked machines that could cut a gallery three metres wide through basalt at forty metres a cycle, brought down the inclines behind Leaf 56-46 in sections and reassembled at the foot, and set going west under no-man's land toward the Cinder Wall in four parallel galleries a hundred metres apart. They were loud. They were meant to be. They screamed through the basalt with a long grinding howl that came back up the galleries and out of the shafts and could be heard in the *35th*'s trenches on the surface eighty metres above, and the sappers who worked behind them stopped up their ears with wax and talked to each other in shouts, and nobody wrapped a pick.

For the first rotation of it, the Frost Masons ignored them.

They were far back. The borers were still cutting through the old lava under the inner belt's ruins, kilometres from the Wall, and the Frost Masons let them come. Rook knew they were listening. Everybody knew. But they let the borers come, and they didn't drive a single counter-mine toward them, and in the *66th*'s galleries the sappers began to make jokes about it, and to wonder aloud whether the Frost Masons had gone deaf.

On the hundred and ninetieth cycle of Rotation Twelve, the borers reached the basalt of the spur, eleven hundred metres from the Wall, and turned toward it.

On the hundred and ninety-first, the Frost Masons came.

---

The first counter-mine came up under the southernmost borer gallery at the second hour of the dark watch, and the listeners didn't hear it coming. Nobody could have. The borers were screaming. Rook was at the shaft foot of the second gallery with Mattias Cole, checking the power lines, when the whole of the deep jumped round him, a single great soundless blow through the rock, and the lamps swung, and dust came down from the roof in a long sifting rain, and then the shouting started on the band.

The southernmost gallery had gone. A dart the size of a cargo car had been fired into the rock beneath it, and brought down two hundred metres of gallery roof onto the borer and the crew behind it. Thirty-one sappers. They dug for them for two cycles and found nine, and only one of the nine was alive, and she died on the incline before they could get her to the surface.

Thirty-one names went onto the Deep List from the *66th* that cycle.

The borers kept going. That was the order. The three that were left kept boring toward the Wall, screaming, louder than ever, and the *66th* drove new listening galleries out to either side of them and manned them every watch with every listener the company had, and drove counter-mines of its own out toward the sounds the listeners heard, when they could hear anything.

The second counter-mine came on the hundred and ninety-eighth cycle. The third on the two hundred and second. The fourth and fifth together, on the two hundred and sixth, from the north and the south at once, and they took the second borer gallery and the crew of the second borer and forty sappers in the side galleries round it.

Rook went down every watch. He'd been made sapper-captain of the *66th* on the two hundred and second cycle, when the old captain went onto the List under the third counter-mine, and he went down every watch and walked every gallery and talked to every squad, by shouts and by signs, and looked at their faces in the lamplight, and saw what was in them.

They knew. Every sapper in the *66th* knew by then what the diversion was. Nobody had told them. Nobody had needed to.

"It's the major's mine, isn't it," Cole shouted at him over the scream of the borer, on the two hundred and tenth cycle, at the foot of the third gallery. "Gault's. Up north. Under 57-44. That's the real one. We're just the noise."

Rook looked at him.

"Yes," he said.

Cole nodded slowly. He was a big quiet man of forty-eight who'd been with the *66th* for twenty-one years, and he'd been beside Rook in the enemy gallery under Leaf 54-47 when Crisp died, and he'd dragged Rook back along the gallery with a pick-head in his side.

"Well," he shouted, at last, "somebody's got to be. Might as well be us. We're good at noise." And he went back to the borer.

---

The Frost Masons sent eleven counter-mines against the diversion under Leaf 56-46 in forty cycles.

Rook would remember all of them. He'd remember the sixth, on the two hundred and fourteenth cycle, when the ice came up through the floor of the third gallery behind the borer in a long grey wave and closed over the crew of the borer where they stood, eleven sappers frozen into the gallery floor up to the waist, still alive, and the *66th* chipped at the ice round them for nine hours with picks and torches while the ice crept up toward their faces, and got five out. He'd remember the eighth, which came through the wall of a listening gallery as a long slow seep of black boiling water from somewhere above, and drowned four listeners at their posts before anyone knew it was there. He'd remember the ninth, on the two hundred and twenty-second cycle, when the Frost Masons themselves came up into the last of the borer galleries out of the rock, through a hole they'd cut from below in absolute silence while the borer screamed above them, a dozen of them, tall and pale and gleaming in their grey ice, and their cold blew down the gallery ahead of them in a bitter wind.

Cole died in the ninth.

Rook saw him go. He saw him stand at the foot of the borer with his shotgun as the Frost Masons came up the gallery out of the dark, and fire it into the first of them at five metres, and the round go into its ice and stop there, and the Frost Mason put out its long grey hand. He saw the frost go over Cole in long white feathered lines, as it had gone over Hollis at the hole under Leaf 54-47 a rotation and a half before, and he saw Cole stand there frozen with the shotgun still raised, and then fall, stiff, onto the gallery floor, and break.

The *66th* fired the dart they'd laid in the gallery behind the borer against exactly this, and brought the roof down on the Frost Masons, and on the borer, and on Cole.

The borers reached within ninety metres of the Cinder Wall before the last of them was buried, on the two hundred and twenty-eighth cycle, by the eleventh counter-mine.

When it was buried, Rook went up the shaft to the surface and sat down on the fire-step of the *35th*'s trench on Leaf 56-46 and looked at the slate of the company's returns that his optio handed him, and read it twice.

The *66th* Sapper Company had gone into the diversion with six hundred sappers. It had sent four hundred names to the Deep List in forty cycles, more than two thirds of its strength. And by the end of it, the Frost Masons had committed most of their strength on that whole stretch of the Wall to stopping it.

Eight hundred metres to the north, under Leaf 57-44, the *61st* Sapper Company had finished laying its mine without a single counter-mine being driven against it.

**THE GALLERY BENEATH LEAF 56-46. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 226**

Voss came down to the diversion on the two hundred and twenty-sixth cycle.

Rook didn't know why. Nobody did. She came down the shaft of the third gallery on the iron ladders at ninety-two, barefoot, alone, without a word to the shaft head guard, and walked along the gallery to the foot of the last borer, where Rook was, and stood beside him in the scream of it and looked at the face for a long time.

"Captain," she shouted at last.

"Prefect."

"I wanted to see it." She looked round at the gallery, at the sappers of the *66th* working behind the borer in their wax ear-plugs, grey with dust and exhaustion, and at the props and the power lines and the long dark tunnel running back toward the shaft. "I objected to this. I wrote to my lords and said so. I said the Marshal was asking a company to die so that another could live, and I said it wasn't the Forge's way." She was quiet for a moment. "It is the Forge's way. It's always been the Forge's way. I've been sending sappers into galleries to die for seventy-five years, Captain. I've just never had to watch the Marshal of the Third decide it across a desk."

Rook said nothing.

"He reads the names, you know," Voss shouted. "Every one. I send them up and he reads them. He sits at his desk until the first bell. Dray told me." She shook her head. "I didn't believe him."

She put her palm flat against the gallery wall beside the borer and closed her eyes, and held it there, as she had at the shaft foot of the *14th* in the north a rotation before.

"Cold," she said. "Getting colder. They're close." She opened her eyes. "Get your people out of this gallery, Captain. Now. All of them."

Rook stared at her.

"Now," Voss said.

He got them out. He shouted it down the gallery and signed it, and the sappers of the *66th* left the borer screaming at the face and ran back along the gallery toward the shaft, all of them, forty-three sappers, and Rook ran with them. He looked back once, at the hundred-metre mark. Voss was still standing at the face beside the borer with her palm against the wall.

The tenth counter-mine went off forty seconds later.

It came up through the floor of the gallery at the face, in a long grey surge of ice that closed over the borer and the face and the first sixty metres of the gallery in about four seconds, filling it from wall to wall and floor to roof, and stopped, ten metres short of the last of the running sappers of the *66th*. Rook felt the cold of it on his back. He turned round. The gallery behind him was a solid wall of grey ice, cloudy and thick, with the dark shape of the borer frozen in the heart of it, and something else, beside the borer, that might have been a small grey figure standing with one hand raised.

He never knew why she'd stayed. Nobody did. Teller said afterward that she'd been listening, that she'd known the counter-mine was coming and had stayed at the face to hear exactly where it was coming from, so the *66th*'s listeners could find the gallery it had come out of. Teller was probably right. The *66th*'s listeners found the Frost Masons' gallery that night, from the bearing of the counter-mine, and fired a dart into it, and that was the last counter-mine but one.

The Deep List recorded her name on the two hundred and twenty-seventh cycle of Rotation Twelve. *Hadria Voss, Sapper-Prefect, First Sapper Regiment of the Forge. Beneath Leaf 56-46, the third borer gallery.*

Kastellan read it at his desk that night. Dray said afterward that he'd sat with the slate in his hand until the first bell, and hadn't read any of the others.

**BENEATH THE CINDER WALL. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 229**

The Frost Mason that the thralls of the Ninth Casting Hall called Old Rime had been listening to the Third's borers under Leaf 56-46 for forty cycles, and on the two hundred and twenty-ninth cycle of Rotation Twelve it stood in the deepest of its galleries beneath the Cinder Wall with its grey hand flat against the stone and listened to the silence where the borers had been.

It had buried the last of them on the two hundred and twenty-eighth. It had felt the eleventh counter-mine go off through the rock under its palm and the last borer go quiet, and since then there had been nothing from Leaf 56-46 but the faint scrape of the Third's sappers drawing back toward their shaft. Eleven counter-mines in forty cycles. The thralls under its command had died in the galleries by the hundred to drive them. Twelve of the Masons had gone into the last of the borer galleries in the ninth counter-mine, and four had come back. No Mason grieved, and Old Rime spent no grief on them, but it had marked the four hundred names the Third would be sending to its list, and it had marked the old woman of the Forge who had stood at the face with her hand on the wall and listened back to it, and it had marked the four Masons who hadn't returned, and it had found that the marks sat heavier in it than it had expected.

The Third had wanted the Wall at Leaf 56-46. It had thrown four borers and a whole company at it and lost them all. The Master of the Ice had told the Archflamen so, on the two hundred and twentieth cycle, and the Archflamen had sent the Masons his thanks, through Dace, who had looked at the Master of the Ice with an expression Old Rime had not been able to read.

It listened. It heard the rock cooling. It heard the river, far down, moving in the dark under the spur. It heard the guns of the Third on the surface, slackening as they always slackened in the hours before the first bell.

And under that, at the farthest edge of its hearing, eight hundred metres to the north, beneath Leaf 57-44, it heard something it had heard for a hundred and twelve cycles without once understanding what it was.

A soft rhythmic sound. So faint it was barely a sound at all. A slow pad, pad, pad, deep in the basalt, coming and going, hour after hour, watch after watch, that the Masons' listeners had logged in Rotation Twelve and set aside as the seep. The seep moved in the rock like that, through the old channels in the lava, in slow pulses. Every Mason knew it. Old Rime had set it aside a hundred times.

It stood in its gallery with its hand on the stone, and listened to the pad, pad, pad of the seep beneath Leaf 57-44, and for the first time in a hundred and twelve cycles, the seep stopped.

It went on listening for a long time. Nothing came. The soft pulse that had gone on under Leaf 57-44 every watch for a hundred and twelve cycles was gone, and in its place was silence, a silence so complete that it had a shape, and Old Rime, which had been listening to stone for longer than the Empire had held the Cocytus, knew that shape.

It was the silence of a gallery that has been emptied.

It took its hand from the wall, and turned, and began to walk north along its gallery, fast, toward Leaf 57-44, and as it walked it called to the other Masons in the deep as Masons call to one another, through the cold, without a sound.

It had walked four hundred metres when the first bell rang on the surface.

**THE FIRING STATION, LEAF 57-44. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 230**

Zero hour for the Great Mine was the first bell of the two hundred and thirtieth cycle of Rotation Twelve.

Every sapper was withdrawn from the galleries under Leaf 57-44 at the dark watch. Gault went down one last time first, alone, and walked the nine hundred metres of the gallery in his felt-soled boots with his hooded lamp, and stood at the end of it in front of the tamping, three hundred metres of spoil and sandbags packed solid from wall to wall between the chamber and the gallery to drive the force of the charge upward, and put his hand on it. Then he walked back. The listeners came out of their niches behind him one by one as he passed and followed him back to the shaft, and up the incline, and the gallery was empty.

He went to the firing station.

It was a small dugout in the side of the *35th*'s support trench, with a lamp and a table and the two bronze firing handles on the wall that he'd made, with the cables running into them from the floor. The *35th*'s colonel was waiting for him there. His name was Vidar Osk, and he was a tall gaunt grey-faced man of fifty-five with a dueling scar across his chin, and the *35th* Trench Regiment was going to go first through the breach the mine made and force its way through the last works between the Third and the Acropolis.

"Major," Osk said.

"Colonel."

"Is it ready?"

"It's ready." Gault looked at the handles. "Nineteen hundred tonnes of compound under the footings, Colonel. Eighty-six metres down. Three detonators, wired in series and in parallel, three cables. If one goes, the others take it. If two go, the third takes it." He paused. "If all three go, I'll go down and light it with a match."

Osk almost smiled.

"Will it break the footings?"

"I don't know," Gault said. "Nobody does. The founders cast them to stand for ten thousand years. Nobody's ever put anything under them as big as this. Nobody's ever put anything this big under anything." He touched one of the handles with the bronze lever in his cuff. "We'll know in about four seconds."

He looked at his watch. Osk looked at his.

Outside the dugout, along the *35th*'s trenches on Leaf 57-44, the regiment was waiting. Gault had seen it as he came along the trench. Every bay full, every fire-step, soldiers standing shoulder to shoulder in their plate with their rifles and their meltas, faces grey in the lamplight. Behind them in the assembly trenches the Hoplons of the first wave were drawn up with their engines idling, a long line of them, blunt and heavy, ready to carry the first wave across no-man's land to the breach. Behind them, the Thureos. Behind them, every gun of the *7th* Line Phalanx, laid on the ground beyond the Wall, waiting.

None of them had been told what was under them. They'd been told to keep their heads down at the first bell.

"Thirty seconds," Gault said.

He fitted his cuff over the first firing handle. The bronze lever went into the socket of it with a soft click.

"Major," Osk said quietly. "What was her name? The Prefect. The one who died in the diversion."

"Voss," Gault said. "Hadria Voss. She was my Prefect for nine years." He looked at his watch. "She objected to this. She said one mine was a mad idea. She wrote it to the Forge."

"Was she right?"

"We'll know in about four seconds," Gault said again, and threw the handle.

---

He felt it first.

The whole of the Flats lifted under him. The floor of the dugout came up under his boots and hit him in the soles of his feet, and the walls jumped, and the lamp went over and out, and through the soles of his boots and the bones of his legs and his spine and his skull came a single vast deep blow out of the earth, so deep it was below sound, a shock that went through him and on and on.

Then the sound came.

He had no words for it, then or afterward. Nobody on the Flats did. The *35th*'s soldiers in the trenches on Leaf 57-44 who heard it spoke of it for the rest of their lives and never found the words. A roar out of the ground that went up and up and filled the whole of the cavern at Depth 40 from the Flats to the roof, and went on, and shook the trenches and the dugouts and the Hoplons in the assembly trenches and the guns in their pits for twenty kilometres in every direction, and was heard, the Fleet's crews reported, in the ships in the low geometry two thousand metres above.

Gault went out of the dugout into the trench, and looked over the parapet.

A column of fire was going up out of the Flats in front of the Cinder Wall. It went up out of the ground eight hundred metres away, a great boiling pillar of fire and smoke and broken rock, red and black and orange, rising, a hundred metres, two hundred, three hundred, four, up and up into the ash cloud, lighting the ash from beneath for kilometres round, until it reached the cloud and spread under it in a vast rolling canopy of fire and smoke beneath the ceiling, and began to fall back. Rock fell out of it. Basalt, in blocks the size of Hoplons, falling out of the smoke onto the Flats for a kilometre in every direction, onto no-man's land and the Archflamen's pits and the Wall, and into the *35th*'s trenches, so that the soldiers of the *35th* threw themselves down in the bays with their arms over their heads and the rocks came down among them.

The shock rolled across no-man's land through the trenches of the *35th* in a long rippling wave, and Gault saw the ground move with it, saw the earth of no-man's land heave and roll under the shock, and the parapets of the trenches crack and slide, and men and women fall flat in the bays as it passed under them.

Then it was over, and the smoke was coming down, and in the silence afterward he could hear soldiers screaming somewhere along the *35th*'s line where the rocks had come down.

He waited for the smoke to clear. Osk came out of the dugout behind him and stood beside him at the parapet, and they waited together.

It took four minutes.

When it cleared, a long stretch of the Cinder Wall in front of Leaf 57-44 was gone.

There was nothing where it had been. The Wall ran in from the north toward the place where the mine had been fired, thirty metres high and dark and whole, and stopped, in a ragged broken end of cast basalt with its bunkers torn open to the air and its guns thrown down. And then nothing. A crater. A great raw smoking pit in the Flats, a hundred and ninety metres across from rim to rim, its slopes of shattered basalt and fused glass glowing red in places with the heat of the blast, and on its far side, two hundred metres to the south, the Wall began again in another ragged broken end, and ran on.

Through the gap, beyond the crater, in the smoke, he could see the shanty-town of the cone's foot, burning. And beyond it the cone, rising, and the Acropolis on top of it under its wards, gold in the smoke.

Osk looked at it for a long time.

"Well, Major," he said at last. "I'd say she was wrong."

Gault didn't answer. He was looking at the crater. He'd begun, quietly, under his breath, to hum.

The *35th*'s guns opened up at once behind them, every gun of the *7th* Line Phalanx at once, on the ground beyond the crater, to prepare it for the first wave in its Hoplons. Osk turned and went back along the trench toward the assembly trenches at a run, shouting for his adjutant. Gault stayed at the parapet. He stood there until the first wave of the *35th* went over, and watched the Hoplons come grinding up out of the assembly trenches past him and across no-man's land toward the breach through the falling ash, and then he went back to the firing station and sat down at the table in the dark and took the bronze lever out of the socket of his cuff and held it in his good hand, and looked at it, for a long time.

**THE WAR BENEATH. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 231**

By the time the Great Mine was fired, the Hephaestean sappers of the Forge and the Third's trench volunteers had driven more than three hundred kilometres of galleries beneath the Cinder Flats. They had fired two hundred and seventy mines and some four thousand darts, and met the Archflamen's counter-mines in more than nine hundred recorded actions.

The Deep List held one hundred and forty thousand names.

Among them were two Sapper-Prefects of the Forge, one of them Hadria Voss. Among them were the colonels of three sapper regiments. Among them were Renée Marchand, and Brin Hollis, and Ivor Lund, and Mattias Cole, and three hundred and ninety-six other sappers of the *66th* Company, and Abelard Crisp of the *37th*, whose name was on the roll of the sapper hall at Hold Pyrgos and nowhere else in the Forge.

The Archflamen's losses below ground could never be counted. His miners carried no implants. The dead of both sides lay where the galleries had buried them, and there they stayed. The sappers estimated that they had killed three of the enemy for every one of their own, and that the Frost Masons had killed four sappers for every one of theirs. Both figures were guesses, and everyone knew it.

The war beneath had won the Third ground on the northern leaves, and a breach in the Cinder Wall. It had cost the Third a kind of loss it had never had to count before, a kind that the vats at Watchpoint Hades couldn't repair.

Ansel Rook read the figures in the *66th*'s dugout on Leaf 56-46 on the two hundred and thirty-first cycle, with the slate in his hand and the two hundred survivors of his company asleep in the bays outside. Then he took out the letter from his mother that he'd kept in the pocket of his tunic since Rotation Twelve began, and read that too, and then he began to write to her, with the list of the *66th*'s four hundred names beside him on the table, one name at a time, with the galleries beside them, so she could put them on the roll.
