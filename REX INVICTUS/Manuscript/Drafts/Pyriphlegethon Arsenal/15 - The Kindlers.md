## Chapter 15: The Kindlers

*"Keep the flame. Whatever is fed to it, keep the flame."*

*Rule of the House of the Hearth, first article*

---

**THE TERRACES OF THE ACROPOLIS. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 170**

The Archflamen led the procession out onto the terraces at the first bell, as he had every cycle since the second belt fell, and Vestal Theodora Kyme walked in it with her lamp, eleven places behind him, and waited for the shells.

They came at the same time every cycle. The Third's gunners on the old second belt had found the Acropolis on the hundred and sixty-second cycle, and since then they had fired at it every watch, a shell or two at a time, the great Phaethon shells that came down out of the ash cloud with a long rising scream and burst on the terraces in fountains of white fire and broken stone. They fired seldom, and seldom was enough. Theodora had heard one of the garrison's gunners explain it to another on the stair, a cycle after the first one landed: the Third had all the time in the world now, and every shell that landed on the Acropolis was a reminder.

The procession went out anyway. The Archflamen would not hear of stopping it.

He walked at the head of it in his white vestments with his staff in his hand, slowly, at the pace of the old liturgy, and behind him came the bearers with the bones. They had brought them up from the Katabasion for the first time on the hundred and sixty-third cycle, the bones of Legio Prima Katabasis that had lain in their niches under the Cinder Cone for longer than the Arsenal had stood, and now they were carried out every cycle onto the terraces on biers draped in red, skull after skull, a long pale line of them in the grey light of the first bell. Behind the bones came the Haruspices, and behind the Haruspices the Vestals of the House of the Hearth with their lamps, forty of them in two files, and behind the Vestals whatever soldiers of the garrison could be spared, which was fewer every cycle.

Theodora had been a Vestal for forty-one years. She had come to the House of the Hearth from a farming hold on the upper Phlegethon when she was nine, sent by her parents for the honour of it, and she had kept the lamps of the Katabasion since she was twenty, and she had never once, in thirty-two years of keeping them, let one go out. It was the first rule of the House. *Keep the flame.* Whatever happened, whatever was fed to it, you kept the flame.

The lamp in her hands now was the oldest in the Katabasion. It had stood in the niche of the First Descent at the head of the long hall since before anyone could remember, and it had never gone out, and she carried it on the terraces every cycle with both hands, under the shells, with her body bent around it to shield it from the wind.

The Archflamen stopped at the edge of the third terrace and turned to face the procession.

He spoke every cycle. Theodora had heard him speak for most of her life, in the Katabasion and the great hall and the courts of the Acropolis, and she had loved to hear it. His voice was soft and warm and carried a long way, and when he spoke of the Imperator and the First Descent and the dark and the coming back up out of it, she had always felt something go through her, a kind of shiver, that she had never felt anywhere else. She had felt it in the Katabasion when he told Dace about the bones. She had felt it on the walls when he declared the Hold.

She didn't feel it now.

He spoke of the dark. He spoke of it every cycle now, and nothing else. He didn't speak of victory any longer, or of defeat, or of the Third, or of the Empire. He spoke of going down into the dark, as the First Descent had gone down, and of the Imperator coming back up out of it, and of the end that he had preached all his life being at hand. He spoke softly and warmly, and his voice carried, and the procession stood on the terrace in the grey light and listened, and Theodora held her lamp and looked at his face.

It was a kind face. It had always been a kind face. It was tired now, more tired than she had ever seen it, and the eyes in it were fever-bright.

"The slaughter must continue," he said. "It is not slaughter. It is the descent. Every one who goes down goes down for him, and every one who goes down makes the road shorter for his return. We will not stop. We will not hold back. We will go down, all of us, one by one, and when the last of us has gone down, he will come up."

The scream of the shell began, high up in the ash cloud.

Nobody in the procession moved. Theodora didn't move. She stood with her lamp and listened to the scream rising, louder and louder, coming down, and she stayed where she was. Nobody else moved, the Archflamen was still speaking, and she didn't know where to go.

The shell landed on the fourth terrace, below them, eighty metres away. The terrace jumped under her feet. A great white column of fire and stone went up into the grey air and came down again in a long rattling rain of fragments, on the terrace, on the procession, on the biers with the bones. Something struck her shoulder, hard, and she staggered, and bent around her lamp.

The flame shivered and held.

When she looked up, the Archflamen was still speaking. He hadn't paused. There was dust on his white vestments and a cut on his cheek from a stone fragment, bleeding a little, and he hadn't noticed it.

Beside him, at his left hand, where she had stood every cycle since the docks, Hesper Calloway stood in her cracked black armour and looked out over the terraces toward the Flats, and the dust had settled on her too, grey on the black, and she hadn't noticed it either.

Theodora looked at her for a long time. Then she looked down at her lamp, and kept the flame.

---

**THE KATABASION, BENEATH THE CINDER CONE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 172**

She was trimming the wicks in the long hall two cycles later when she heard them talking.

It was the dark watch, and the Katabasion was empty except for the lamps and the bones in their niches, those that were left. Half the niches had been emptied for the procession, and the empty ones gaped along the walls like open mouths. Theodora went down the long hall with her shears and her oil-flask, niche by niche, as she had every dark watch for thirty-two years, and at the far end of it, at the long table under the niche of the First Descent, the Archflamen sat in his chair with Hesper Calloway at his left hand and Ilan Dace at his right.

They didn't look up when she came near. Nobody ever did. A Vestal trimming the lamps was part of the Katabasion, as much as the bones.

"The second belt is gone," the Archflamen was saying. His voice was quite calm. "The Third is on the old second belt, and its guns are on the Acropolis, and it will be before the inner belt within the rotation. Prefect Ostorian tells me he can hold the inner belt for a rotation, perhaps two. After that..." He spread his hands.

"The war isn't lost, Holiness," Hesper said.

Theodora went on trimming the wick of the lamp in front of her, slowly.

"The Empire's army is closing its ring," Hesper said. "That's true. It has guns and it has soldiers and it has the vats, and it'll go on closing its ring until there's nothing inside it. But there are powers in the river greater than any army with guns, Holiness. Older than the Empire. Older than the Arsenal. Older than the First Descent." She paused. "I've been down there. I've been down there for eleven hundred years. I know what's there."

"And they'll stand with us?"

"They'll stand with you," Hesper said. "If you hold firm. And if you know how to call them."

"Do you know how to call them?"

"I know who does."

There was a long silence at the table. Theodora finished the wick and moved on to the next niche, slowly, with her back to the table.

"What will it cost?" the Archflamen said.

"That's not for me to say, Holiness," Hesper said. "The Kindlers will name the price. It's theirs to name."

Theodora's hands stopped on the shears.

She knew the name. Everyone on the spur knew it, as they knew the name of the river and the name of the Cinder Cone. The Kindlers were the Cinderborn, the priests of the Red Furnace, the Phlegethon's own congregation, who lived down at the bend below the spur where the river ran hottest and had lived there for as long as the Arsenal had stood. Nobody from the Arsenal went down to the bend. The Hephaestean engineers who had built the docks had built them a long way upriver from it, and they had built the smugglers' stair down the cliff to the bend and then sealed it, and when Theodora was a girl in the House of the Hearth the older Vestals had told the younger ones stories about the Kindlers to frighten them at night. How they burned themselves in the river and healed, and burned again and healed again, until their skin was black and grey and rough as slag. How they sang to the river and the river sang back. How they fed things to it.

"Bring them," the Archflamen said.

---

**THE SMUGGLERS' STAIR, THE EASTERN CLIFF OF THE SPUR. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 175**

They sent her to light the stair.

It was an honour, the senior Vestal told her. The Archflamen had asked for a Vestal to light the Kindlers up the stair, as the Kindlers' own custom required, and he had asked for the oldest lamp, and the oldest lamp was Theodora's. She took it down through the vaults and the old tunnels under the eastern side of the spur to the head of the smugglers' stair, with two soldiers of the garrison to carry the oil and Dace walking beside her in his grey coat with his notebook in his pocket, and at the head of the stair she stopped and looked down.

The stair went down the face of the cliff into the dark, a narrow iron ladder-stair bolted to the rock, zigzagging back and forth across the cliff face, down and down, past the ruins of the docks, toward the bend. At the bottom of it, a long way down, she could see the river. It was red. It was always red, at the bend. But tonight there was something else in it, deep under the surface, a darker glow, slow and pulsing, the beat of something vast asleep under the water.

"They're coming up," Dace said beside her. "Light the way, Vestal."

She went down the first flight with the lamp held out in front of her, and set it in the iron bracket at the first landing, and lit the lamp waiting there from it, and went down to the next landing, and lit that one. It took her two hours to reach the bottom. When she got there, they were waiting.

There were nine of them.

They stood on the narrow shelf of black rock at the foot of the stair, with the river running past behind them three metres away, so hot that the air above it shimmered and the rock of the shelf was warm through the soles of her sandals. They were tall and thin and quite naked except for loincloths of some dark woven stuff, and their skin was exactly as the older Vestals had told her when she was a girl: black and grey and rough as slag, cracked and seamed and puckered, scar laid over scar. It had been burned and had healed and been burned and healed so many times that there was nothing else left. Their faces were the same. Only their eyes were different. Their eyes were bright and clear and dark and quite calm, and they looked at her lamp.

The tallest of them stepped forward. It was a woman, or had been, once. She was old, so old that Theodora couldn't begin to guess her age, and she put out one slag-black hand toward the lamp.

Theodora didn't move. It was the first rule of the House. You didn't give the flame to anyone.

The Kindler didn't take it. She held her hand over the lamp, close, a hand's breadth above the flame, and the flame rose up to meet her hand. Theodora felt it go. She felt the flame in her lamp, the flame that had never gone out, lean up out of the wick toward the black palm above it, long and thin and bright, and stretch, and touch it.

The Kindler closed her eyes.

"Old," she said. Her voice was a cracked whisper, dry as ash. "This is old fire. This came down with the First Descent." She opened her eyes and looked at Theodora. "You keep it well, sister."

Then she took her hand away, and the flame sank back into the wick, and Theodora stood on the warm black rock at the foot of the stair with her lamp in her hands and found that they were shaking.

"Ashmother," Dace said, from behind her. He had come down the stair after her. He bowed, low. "The Archflamen thanks you for coming."

"We came for the river," the Kindler said. "Not for him. The river is ready." She looked up the long zigzag of the stair, the little lights going up and up the cliff face into the dark, to the vaults and the Acropolis and the Mast. "Take us to the well."

**THE HEAD OF THE NUMBER FOUR HEAT-WELL, BENEATH THE ACROPOLIS. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 180 TO 189**

They kept her at the well for nine cycles.

The Kindlers had asked for it. A Vestal of the Hearth was to keep a lamp at the well-head, they said, for as long as the calling lasted, and the lamp was not to go out. Theodora kept it. She sat on a stool by the iron rail at the head of the Number Four heat-well, with her lamp on a stand beside her, through nine cycles and every watch of them, and slept when the other Vestals came to relieve her and came back when they had gone, and she kept the flame.

The Number Four heat-well was the oldest on the spur, and the deepest. It went down from the vaults beneath the Acropolis into the rock for a long way, a round black shaft forty metres across with an iron rail around its rim and an iron gallery running down its walls in a long spiral, until the gallery stopped and the shaft went on without it, down into the dark, toward the heat. You could feel the heat at the rail. It came up out of the shaft in long slow breaths, hot and dry and smelling of sulphur and iron, and on the walls of the shaft below the gallery the rock glowed a dull red.

The Kindlers went down the spiral gallery on the first cycle with Dace and stood on the last landing, where the gallery ended and the shaft dropped away beneath them into the red dark, and they began to sing.

Theodora heard them from the rail for nine cycles, a singing far from the hymns of the House or the liturgy of the Katabasion. It was a low continuous sound, rising and falling, nine voices together, cracked and dry, without words, and under it, after the first watch, she began to hear something else. It came up out of the shaft from a long way down, faint at first and then louder: a deep slow sound, rising and falling with the Kindlers' voices, answering them.

The river was singing back.

On the second cycle they brought the thralls.

They came down from the vaults in a long line, in their grey thrall-smocks, with their blank faces and their empty eyes, and the garrison soldiers led them along the rail to the head of the spiral gallery and down it, one by one, in a long slow line spiralling down the walls of the shaft into the red glow, toward the Kindlers on the last landing. Theodora sat by her lamp and watched them go past her. They didn't look at her. They didn't look at anything. They walked where they were led, and they went down the gallery, and when they reached the last landing where the Kindlers stood singing, they went on, off the edge of the landing, into the dark.

She didn't see them fall. The landing was too far below. She saw them reach it, one after another, and then she didn't see them any more.

She sat by her lamp and kept the flame.

They brought thralls for four cycles. She didn't count them. She found afterward that she knew the number anyway, and she never knew how: four thousand. On the fifth cycle they brought the Laistrygons. There were forty of them, the last of the Arsenal's breeding pen, great grey hairless creatures three metres tall, led down from the pens on chains by their keepers. They were not blank-faced. They knew. Theodora heard them before she saw them, a long low moaning, coming along the passages from the pens, and when they came out onto the rail and saw the shaft they stopped, all forty of them, and pulled back against their chains, and their keepers had to drive them. It took the whole of the fifth cycle and most of the sixth. When the last of them went off the landing into the dark, the sound from the shaft changed. It grew louder and deeper, and the red glow on the shaft walls brightened, and the heat that came up past the rail made Theodora's eyes water.

On the seventh cycle they brought the strays.

---

She knew the strays. Everyone in the Acropolis knew the strays, though nobody spoke of them much. They were the Hecatean strays, children of the Hold Kestrel line and the other old Hecatean families of the upper Phlegethon, who had been brought to the Acropolis over the last rotations for what they could hear. Dace had gathered them. Five hundred and twelve of them, by the end. Theodora had seen them in the corridors of the Acropolis, in their grey smocks, walking in pairs with the Haruspices who looked after them, and some of them had come to the Katabasion to sit with the lamps, and she had let them, and shown them how to trim the wicks.

Mirren had come most often.

Theodora had liked Mirren. Everybody had. She was small and thin and pale, with dark eyes, and she talked about birds. She said she could hear them, the birds that went up when a soldier of the Third died, and the birds that didn't. She had sat in the Katabasion with Theodora on a great many dark watches, trimming the wicks, and told her about the birds, and Theodora hadn't understood half of it and had listened anyway.

They came down the passage from the vaults on the seventh cycle in their grey smocks, in pairs, holding hands, with the Haruspices beside them and the garrison soldiers behind. Five hundred and eleven of them.

Mirren wasn't with them. Theodora looked for her. She looked at every face as it went past her along the rail, every small pale face, some of them crying and some of them quite blank and some of them looking at her lamp with wide dark eyes, and Mirren wasn't there.

She found out afterward that Dace had kept her. That he had taken her up to her room in the Acropolis on the morning of the seventh cycle and locked the door and sat outside it on the floor with his back to the wall, and stayed there until the ninth cycle was over. She never knew why. She thought about it a great deal, afterward, in the long cycles when she had nothing to do but think. She thought that it was the only thing she had ever known Ilan Dace to do that she didn't understand.

Theodora sat by her lamp and watched the strays go down.

She wanted badly to do something. She wanted to stand up from her stool and take the nearest of them by the hand and run, back along the rail, up through the vaults, anywhere. She wanted to shout at the Haruspices and the soldiers. She wanted to go down the spiral gallery to the last landing and stand in front of the Kindlers and tell them to stop.

She sat on her stool by the rail and kept the flame. It was the first rule of the House, she was a Vestal of forty-one years who had never in her life done anything but keep the flame, and she was afraid.

She did one thing. She took the lamp-book out of the pocket of her robe, the little bound book that every Vestal of the Hearth carried, in which the lamps were recorded, which lamps had been lit and when, which trimmed, which refilled. And as each pair of strays went past her along the rail, she wrote a line in it. She didn't know their names. She wrote what she could see. *A boy, perhaps eight, with red hair, holding a girl's hand.* *A girl, small, with a cut on her knee.* *Two boys, brothers, crying.* She wrote five hundred and eleven lines in the lamp-book over the seventh and eighth cycles, in the light of her lamp, in her small careful hand, and when the last pair had gone past and down the gallery she closed it and put it back in her pocket and sat with her hands folded on it.

The sound from the shaft was loud now. The Kindlers were still singing, on the last landing, their nine cracked voices rising and falling, and the river was singing back, and the red glow in the shaft had become orange, and the heat came up past the rail in long scorching waves, and Theodora sat by her lamp and couldn't stop shaking.

On the ninth cycle the Kindlers came back up the gallery.

They came up slowly, all nine of them, in a line, the old woman at their head, and they came along the rail past Theodora's stool. They didn't look at her. They didn't look at anything. Their eyes were half closed, and they were still humming under their breath, softly, the long low wordless song, and their slag-black skin was hot, so hot that Theodora felt the heat of them as they went past her, and smelt it, burnt stone and sulphur.

The old woman stopped beside her.

She looked down at Theodora, and at her lamp, and at the hands folded on the pocket of her robe where the lamp-book was.

"You kept it, sister," she said.

Theodora said nothing.

"Good," the Kindler said. "Keep it. You will need it." She looked back down into the shaft, at the orange glow. "The river is coming up now. All of it. Everything it has kept." She looked at Theodora again, with her bright dark calm eyes. "Keep the flame, sister. When it comes, keep the flame. Whatever is fed to it."

Then she went on along the rail after the others, and up into the vaults, and was gone.

Theodora sat by her lamp until the relief came. Then she went up through the vaults to the House of the Hearth, and to her cell, and lay down on her bed in her robe without taking it off, with the lamp-book in her pocket, and lay there in the dark with her eyes open for a long time.

She kept the lamp-book. She kept it through everything that came after. It was one of the things they found, at the end, when they found her.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 190**

The sentries' reports came in at the start of the morning watch, from every regiment on the old second belt between the Mora Cut and the Lethe Cut, and Corvis put them on the Lord-General's desk in a stack without bothering to summarise them. They all said the same thing.

The Phlegethon was rising.

Castorre went out onto the observation deck to see it for himself. Corvis went with him. The observation deck was a long open gallery on the southern face of Quarterdeck Nineteen, high up on the ceiling, looking down over the whole of the Flats, and from it, on a clear watch, you could see from the Mora Cut in the north to the Lethe Cut in the south, and the spur, and the river.

The watch was hazed, as it had been for twenty cycles.

The river had burned red at the edge of the Flats for six hundred years. Corvis had looked down at it from this gallery almost every watch for eight rotations, a long dull red line running across the bottom of the Flats beneath the spur, and she had stopped seeing it a long time ago, as everyone stopped seeing the ceiling. She saw it now. Along the whole stretch of it below the spur, from the Demus Gorge to the bend, it had turned orange, and at the bend itself, directly beneath the spur, it was yellow, a fierce bright sulphurous yellow that hurt to look at. The heat came off it in waves. She could see them from the gallery, kilometres away and kilometres above, great slow ripples in the air going out across the Flats from the river and making the trenches and the guns and the spur beyond them waver and shimmer.

And above the spur, under the ceiling, there was cloud.

This was something new, separate from the ash cloud, red and black, in great slow heavy coils, gathered beneath the ceiling directly over the Acropolis and turning, slowly, around a centre she couldn't see. Nobody in the Third had ever seen cloud like it on the Flats. Nobody in six hundred years.

"What is it?" Castorre said quietly.

"The Répertoire's working on it, sir," Corvis said. "They've had the heat curves since the first watch, and the soundings from the river bed. They say..." She looked at her slate. "They say the river bed under the bend has risen eleven metres in nine cycles, sir. They say the heat at Depth 40 has doubled. They say they've never seen anything like it."

"A reformation."

"They think so, sir. They're issuing a warning to every hold in the theatre."

Castorre stood at the rail of the gallery and looked down at the river for a long time.

Every soldier of the Third knew what a reformation was. It was the reason the Third existed. The monsters of Tartarus didn't stay dead. Whatever the Third killed went back into the deep, into the rivers and the dark below the rivers, and was made again, and sent back up, within a rotation or a century. A reformation peak happened when the deep did it faster than usual and sent many at once, and when it happened the Siege Armies of the Empire went to meet it. The Third had met a great many of them, in six hundred years. It had met one on the Acheron shelves in the year Castorre was born. It had met one at Depth 38 that had taken a whole Line Phalanx into the river.

"How big?" he said.

Corvis didn't answer at once.

"Leda."

"The largest of the Third's six hundred years on the Flats, sir," she said. "That's the Répertoire's assessment. And they assess..." She looked down at the slate. She read it exactly. "They assess that the Third cannot meet it with what remains after the Big Push."

Castorre said nothing for a long time.

"Nobody knew," he said at last. "Six hundred years. Nobody in the Third ever knew that you could call one." He looked down at the yellow river. "That's what this is, isn't it? He's called it. Somebody's called it for him."

"The Répertoire doesn't say so, sir."

"No," Castorre said. "But you think so."

"Yes, sir," Corvis said. "I think so."

---

The Command sent the Third a reserve.

It came by signal under the Command's own seal in the evening watch of the hundred and ninety-fifth cycle, and Corvis read it twice before she took it in, and then sat for a moment at her desk with her hands flat on either side of it.

Then she took it in to the Lord-General.

"Four million soldiers, sir," she said. "Raised for the peak. With their armour and their guns and their Behemoths, and the supplies for a theatre. Two ships of the Fleet to carry them. The *Axis Dawn* and the *Warbringer Fury*. They'll come down through the ceiling over the south-western end of the arc within five cycles."

Castorre took the slate. He read it slowly.

"Four million," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Who commands?"

Corvis had known he would ask. She had read the name three times.

"The Command's sent its most senior available field commander, sir. To take the peak in hand." She paused. "A Senior Commander of the Waves of Poseidon. Ranked nine thousand and fifty-first on the Unified Leaderboard. Callsign Dulaberre-Six." She paused again. "Marien Pyrrhus, sir. Daughter of Stephanos Pyrrhus."

Castorre looked up from the slate.

He didn't say anything. Corvis hadn't expected him to. Every officer in the Empire knew the name Pyrrhus. The Pyrrhus Command, which commanded the Empire's pure-blood cohorts in the theatre and had sent the Cohort of Nemesis to the docks without a word to the Third, carried it. Stephanos Pyrrhus carried it. And now his daughter was coming down through the ceiling over the Third's south-western flank, with four million soldiers, to take the peak in hand.

"She'll be senior to me," Castorre said at last. "In the theatre. For the peak."

"For the peak, sir. Yes."

He looked at the slate for a while longer. Then he put it down on the desk, carefully, and squared it against the edge.

"Good," he said.

Corvis looked at him.

"Good, Leda," he said again, quietly. "Four million soldiers and a Pyrrhus. Let her take it in hand. Somebody should." He sat back in his chair. "Where will she land?"

"Segment Twelve, sir. At the south-western end of the arc. The Répertoire predicts the peak at the bend, under the spur, and Segment Twelve is the nearest stretch of the line to the bend that isn't on the Flats. The ships can hold station over it outside the worst of the lance envelope, and the reserve can come down onto the landing shelf behind it and move up the Gorge rim to the bend."

"Segment Twelve," Castorre said. "That's a single battalion."

"Of the Ninth Line Phalanx, sir. Under a major. It's been a fringe position for two hundred years. Its walls are old. Very old. Dug before the Third came to the Flats."

"Tell the major he's about to have company."

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 200 TO 201**

The beacon from Segment Twelve came in at the third bell of the dark watch on the two hundredth cycle, and Corvis was at the map table when it did.

She had been there for most of the watch. Everyone had. The Répertoire had predicted the peak at the bend, under the spur, and every eye on Quarterdeck Nineteen had been on the bend since the hundred and ninetieth cycle, on the yellow river and the red-black cloud turning over the Acropolis, waiting for it to come up. Nobody had been watching the south-western end of the arc. Nobody had any reason to.

The beacon was a single red light on the map table, at the extreme south-western end of the Third's line, where the trenches ran along the rim of the Demus Gorge and stopped. It pulsed slowly, on and off. Under it, in small white letters, the augur overlay printed the identifier the beacon carried, the same identifier that had stamped itself on the visors of Segment Twelve's soldiers whenever the alarms sounded, for two hundred years.

SEGMENT TWELVE.

"Sir," Corvis said.

Castorre was beside her in a moment. He looked at the red light.

"That's not the bend," he said.

"No, sir."

"What's happening there?"

The traffic came in fast. Corvis read it off the relay as it came, and the picture built itself on the map table in front of them, one light at a time.

It had come up the Gorge. The Répertoire had predicted the peak at the bend, and the Kindlers had called it somewhere else, beneath the Demus Gorge, nine kilometres down, where nobody was watching. It had climbed the Gorge walls in the dark watch, and come over the rim into Segment Twelve.

And Segment Twelve held.

Corvis never forgot that. She read it off the relay with Castorre beside her, the reports coming in from the segment's centurion in short, flat, clipped bursts, and she found that she was holding her breath. Segment Twelve held against the first wave and against the second. Its walls were millennia old, dug by soldiers who had known the work long before the Third came to the Flats, and its battalion held behind them, with rifles and keyed mortars and a legacy of Poseidon who flooded the kill corridor with brine from somewhere nobody could afterward explain. It held when a breach opened in front of it: an absence, the centurion's report called it, where none should have been, with forms marching through it almost in order. It held when something came out of the breach, something tall and armoured, that raised its hand and folded the ground in front of the parapet upward, turning the range stakes to dust.

Then the segment's centurion triggered the emergency beacon.

"Where's the reserve?" Castorre said.

"Coming down, sir." Corvis had the Fleet's traffic on the second relay. "The *Axis Dawn* and the *Warbringer Fury*. They came through the ceiling an hour ago. They're..." She stopped. She read it again. "Sir. They're holding station over Segment Twelve. In the low geometry. Inside the reach of the ceiling-lances."

Castorre stared at her.

"That's against every rule the Fleet has for Depth 40."

"Yes, sir. The reformation warning stated that the reserve was needed at once. They've come down to where it's needed." She looked at the Fleet's own figures. "The *Axis Dawn*'s augur puts her at twenty-three percent operational after the passage through the ceiling. The *Warbringer Fury*'s at forty-one."

"Gods below," Castorre said softly.

On the map table, two great grey shapes appeared above the red light of Segment Twelve, and as Corvis watched, the heavy batteries of both ships fired on the breach.

She saw it on the map table only as a change in the lights, a flicker and then a gap where the breach had been. She heard it in the centurion's next report, which was a single line, and then a pause, and then another. The breach and what was in it were gone. Taken out of the world, the report said. Corvis didn't know what that meant, and she didn't think the centurion did either.

"She's with them," she said. "Sir. Dulaberre-Six. She came down with the first pallets to see the ground for herself. She's on the landing shelf behind the segment now, with the major."

"Good," Castorre said. "Good. Then she can..."

The landing shelf behind Segment Twelve stopped existing.

---

Corvis would read the reports of it a great many times in the cycles that followed, and she would never be sure that she understood any of them.

The shelf ceased to exist from its edge inward. That was what the reports said, and it was all they could say. One moment it was there, on the map table, a long grey shape behind the red light of the segment with the little lights of the first pallets on it and the little light that was Dulaberre-Six. The next moment its edge was gone. Then the next metre was gone, and the next, and the next. It went inward into an absence that took the light and returned nothing. That was how the centurion's report put it, and the major's, and the ships'. An absence that took the light and returned nothing.

Marien Pyrrhus fell into it.

She had been pulling soldiers clear. That was in every report. She had seen the shelf begin to go, and she had turned back toward it, toward the edge, where soldiers of the segment's battalion and the first pallets of the reserve were running from it, and she had pulled them clear, one after another, and the absence had come to her, and she had gone into it. Her bodyguard had gone in after her.

Sector Command ruled at once. It was in the traffic, a single line under the Command's seal. If Dulaberre-Six went in, the ships could not leave her.

The major put his battalion and its wounded into the ships' holds while the absence ate the rest of the shelf. Corvis watched the little lights of them move on the map table, crowding together, going up into the two great grey shapes over the segment, the last of them going up as the absence reached the segment's walls. Then the *Axis Dawn* and the *Warbringer Fury* went in after Marien Pyrrhus.

The two great grey shapes on the map table moved down into the place where the shelf had been, and went out.

The Répertoire's log for Segment Twelve came up on the relay a few minutes later, and Corvis read it aloud to the Lord-General. She didn't know what else to do.

"*DULABERRE-SIX. NO SIGNAL.*

*UOE AXIS DAWN. NO SIGNAL.*

*UOE WARBRINGER FURY. NO SIGNAL.*

*RESERVE: NOT AVAILABLE.*"

There was nothing else in the log. She read it again, to be sure, and there was nothing else.

Castorre stood at the map table and looked at the place where Segment Twelve had been. There was nothing there now. The augur overlay had already redrawn the line, and the south-western end of the Third's arc ended in a blank space at the rim of the Demus Gorge, with nothing behind it.

"Four million," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"The ships. Her. All of it."

"The Répertoire's classing it as a subreality shear, sir," Corvis said. She was reading the next signal as it came in. "The loss as total. They're striking the reserve from the books. Its officers, its soldiers, its guns."

Castorre didn't say anything for a long time.

"Where did they go, Leda?"

"Nobody knows, sir."

"Somebody went in after her," he said. "Two ships of the Fleet went in after her, on purpose, because she was in there. They didn't think she was dead. You don't follow somebody into a thing like that unless you think they're still alive on the other side of it." He looked at the blank space on the map. "Find out where they went. One day. When this is over. Find out."

"Yes, sir."

He turned away from the map table. Corvis saw him put his hand to the inside pocket of his coat, where the two letters were, and leave it there, without taking them out.

"And the peak?" he said.

Corvis looked at the map. At the blank space at the south-western end of the arc, where the trenches stopped at the rim of the Gorge, with nothing behind them.

"It's coming up the Gorge, sir," she said. "Tonight. Onto a front with nothing behind it."

**THE KATABASION, BENEATH THE CINDER CONE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 201**

Theodora was trimming the lamps in the long hall when the river began to give up its dead.

She didn't see it. Nobody in the Katabasion could see anything of the outside. But she heard it. Every Vestal in the House of the Hearth heard it, and every soldier in the Acropolis, and every thrall in the vaults. It came up through the rock from below, from the bend, from the river, a long deep groaning, iron on iron, and under it the singing, the same singing she had heard from the Number Four heat-well for nine cycles, the river's voice, deep and slow, rising and falling.

But louder now. Much louder. And there were other voices in it.

She stood in the long hall with her shears in her hand and listened. The lamps along the walls were all flickering, every one of them, in a draught that came from nowhere, and in the empty niches where the bones had lain the shadows moved.

She went to the head of the hall, to the niche of the First Descent, where the oldest lamp stood. It was flickering too. The flame leaned, and shivered, and leaned again, as she had seen it lean toward the Kindler's black hand on the warm rock at the foot of the stair. But it was leaning down now. Toward the floor. Toward the rock, and the river beneath it.

Theodora took the lamp-book out of the pocket of her robe and held it in her hand for a long time. Then she put it back.

She put her hands around the lamp, gently, on either side of the flame, to shield it.

"Keep the flame," she said aloud, to nobody, in the empty hall. "Whatever is fed to it."

Far below her, at the bend, under the yellow water, something enormous began to rise.
