## Chapter 7: The Pourer

*"The Third does not count its dead. It counts the ones who do not come back. Learn the difference, and you will understand the Third."*

*Phalanx-General Oren Callix, 2nd Line Phalanx, to the Command at Watchpoint Hades*

---

**LEAF 58-31, SOUTH OF THE NORTHERN SPUR. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 210, THIRD WATCH**

The engines started at the eleventh bell, and Colonel Brannoc Halde knew exactly what they meant and couldn't get anybody to believe him.

"Engines," he said into the relay for the third time. "Heavy. Tracked. A lot of them. Behind the second belt, opposite my left, about eleven kilometres out. They've been running for an hour and they're getting louder."

"*Acknowledged, Forty-First,*" said the duty officer at the headquarters of the *5th* Line Phalanx, in the patient tone of a man who had acknowledged the same thing twice already. "*The Phalanx-General has your report. He considers it a local matter.*"

"A local matter."

"*Repositioning, he thinks. Or a supply column. The Arsenal moves its armour between sectors at night. We've seen it before.*"

"Not this much of it."

"*Do you have a request, Forty-First?*"

Halde closed his eyes for a moment. He was sixty, a big slow heavy man with a white moustache and a voice that carried down a trench from one end to the other, and he had commanded the *41st* Trench Regiment for fourteen years, and in all fourteen of them he had never been called an alarmist. He had a feeling he was about to be.

"Harassing fire on the second belt," he said. "Opposite my left. Every gun the *5th* can spare, for the rest of the watch. Whatever's forming over there, I want it disrupted."

"*I'll pass it on, Forty-First.*"

The relay went dead. Halde put the handset back in its cradle and stood at the map table of his command dugout and looked at the overlay for a long time.

The *41st* was where it was supposed to be, which was the problem. The *5th* Line Phalanx had come round the end of the second belt in the first weeks of Rotation Three, after the Archflamen's army gave up the northern spur without a fight, and found the spur's trenches empty. It had advanced along the lip of the Mora Cut against nothing but rearguards and marksmen and the usual harassing fire, faster than anybody had expected, and by the ninetieth cycle its forward regiments were digging in on the northern face of the second belt. The Third had been attacking at every point of the arc for two rotations. It had got used to the Archflamen's army sitting in its holes and waiting to be dug out of them.

The *41st* was the *5th*'s left flank. It was holding overnight positions on Leaf 58-31, eleven kilometres short of the second belt, in trenches it had dug in a cycle and a half on its way in. They were shallow. The ground was flat, a long open stretch of ash between the end of the northern spur and the second belt, with nothing on it to hide behind, and the *41st* had halted there because the advance had reached the limit of a day's march and the *5th*'s orders said halt and dig.

Halde had looked at the ground when they arrived, and not liked it, and said so. He'd been told that the Archflamen's army never came out of its trenches.

He keyed the internal net.

"All cohorts," he said. "This is the Colonel. General stand-to at the first bell. Forward cohorts, reinforce your fire bays from the support line. Reserve cohorts, be ready to move. Heavy weapons, ready ammunition up." He paused. "And somebody find me Tribune Oake. I want her in here."

---

Tribune Senna Oake came down the dugout stair ten minutes later, pulling her helmet on. She was thirty-four, his second, a quick sharp-tongued woman from a ceiling-world family of Pyrrhus Command officers who had refused a staff posting on the Quarterdeck to come to the *41st*, which Halde had always considered the best evidence of her judgement.

"Engines," she said, before he could speak. "I heard them from the support line. You've told the *5th*?"

"Three times. They think it's a supply column."

"It's not a supply column."

"No."

She came and stood beside him at the overlay.

"They're going to come," she said. "Aren't they. Out of the belt. At us."

"I think so."

"They've never done that. Two rotations and they've never once come out."

"No," Halde said. "And for two rotations we've been telling ourselves that means they can't." He tapped the overlay, where the *41st*'s line sat on the flat ash of Leaf 58-31. "Look at the ground, Senna. Look at where we are. Eleven kilometres short of the belt, on the flattest stretch of ash on the northern Flats, in holes we dug yesterday. Ostorian has been watching us come round the end of his spur for two months. If I were him, I'd have ranged every gun in the northern half of the second belt on this exact stretch of ash before we ever got here, and I'd have waited for a regiment to stop on it."

Oake looked at the overlay for a while.

"Fuck," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"Harassing fire?"

"Requested. I'll believe it when I hear it." He straightened up. "Get on the regimental net and tell every cohort centurion what I just told you. Then go up to the support line and stay there. If this goes the way I think it'll go, I want one of us forward and one of us back."

---

The harassing fire came at the second bell. It was eleven rounds from a single battery of the *5th*'s divine mortars, spread over half an hour, on the second belt opposite the *41st*'s left. Halde heard them go over. Then nothing.

The engines kept running.

---

**LEAF 58-31. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 211, FIRST BELL**

At the first bell the *41st* stood to, and at the first bell plus four minutes the Archflamen's gunners opened with their customary scattering of rounds, a few shells here and there along the *41st*'s line, gold and red, the way they did every morning.

Then every gun in the northern half of the second belt fired at once.

Halde had been under bombardment before. He'd been under the Phlegethon's fire on the Mora Cut in the Hundred-and-Twelfth Reformation, when the river came up out of its bed in a wall of flame and the Third's guns fired into it until their barrels melted, and he had thought that was the worst thing a soldier could be under. He found out on Leaf 58-31 that it wasn't. The Archflamen's barrage came down on the *41st*'s trenches from one end of the regiment's front to the other in a single unbroken sheet of fire, every metre of it ranged, every metre of it known, solar fire and iron and the dull orange clots of Hephaestean forge-fire that set the ash burning where they landed, and with them came the smoke.

The smoke-shells burst at head height. They threw out great rolling clouds of thick grey-white smoke that lay over the Flats a metre and a half off the ground, at exactly the height of a soldier's eyes on a fire-step, and in five minutes Halde couldn't see anything through the periscope of his command dugout but grey.

The barrage went on for an hour.

He spent the hour on the relay. He spent it telling the *5th* Line Phalanx that the *41st* was under heavy bombardment along its entire front and that he expected an attack, and he spent it listening to his cohort centurions tell him what was happening to their cohorts, which was the same thing all along the line. The forward trenches were collapsing. The fire bays were full of the dead. The reinforcements he had brought up from the support line at the first bell to thicken the forward cohorts were dying in the bays they'd been brought up to thicken. The smoke was so thick that nobody could see the ground in front of the wire.

At the end of the hour, the barrage lifted.

"Here it comes," Halde said quietly, to nobody.

It came out of the smoke.

---

The first he knew of it was a centurion of the *3rd* Cohort screaming on the relay. Then the centurion of the *5th* Cohort. Then all of them, all at once, a babel of voices on the regimental net, shouting over each other, and through the periscope of the command dugout, through the smoke, he saw the first of the Arsenal's tanks.

They were Thureos tanks from the vaults, the same pattern as the Third's, squat and grey and heavy with the Hephaestean forge-hammer painted on their glacis where the Third's had its crest. They came out of the smoke four hundred metres in front of the *41st*'s forward trench and stopped there, in a long line, and began to fire into the fire bays. Behind them came Hoplon carriers. Every cohort front of the regiment had them, rolling forward out of the smoke with their great ramps raised in front of them. And behind the Hoplons came the thralls, thousands of them, with bayonets and picks and the long hooked bars of the casting lines.

And in front of the thralls, running, came the Laistrygons.

Halde had read about them. Everyone had. The Third's intelligence section had circulated a paper on them in Rotation One, after the first deserters from the casting lines reached the Third's line and told what they had seen, and the paper had been read and filed and forgotten. Forty thousand giant-blooded labourers bred by the Hephaestean orders for the heavy work of the casting lines. Three metres tall. Kept by the Lictors on combat stimulants and chained in their barracks between battles.

He hadn't imagined what they would look like coming at a trench.

They came out of the smoke at the speed of a galloping horse, great grey stooped figures, three metres tall, bare-chested and bare-armed, with chains still hanging from the iron collars around their necks and their eyes wide and blank and their mouths open, and they carried nothing at all, and came across four hundred metres of ash toward the *41st*'s forward trench in less than a minute, and the rifles of the *41st* fired into them the whole way, and Halde watched through the periscope as the rounds went into them and they kept coming.

They reached the trench. They went into the fire bays.

He saw it through the periscope, in the gaps in the smoke. He saw a Laistrygon come down into a fire bay of the *3rd* Cohort with four rounds in its chest, and take a trooper of the *41st* by the head with one hand and tear it off, and throw the body at the next trooper along, and go on down the bay. He saw them go through the bays in packs, three or four at a time, tearing, crushing, throwing, and he saw the thralls come into the bays behind them to finish what the Laistrygons had missed, and he saw the Arsenal's tanks drive right up to the parapet of the forward trench and traverse their guns to fire along it.

One after another, the *41st*'s forward positions went off the relay.

"Reserve cohorts," Halde said. His voice was perfectly steady. He was proud of it. "*7th*, *8th*, *9th*. Forward, now, up the communication trenches to the support line. Hold the support line."

They went forward. He watched them go on the overlay, three small green blocks moving up the communication trenches from the reserve line toward the support line, and he watched the Archflamen's guns, which had lifted off the forward trench, find them on the way. Half of them reached the support line. The other half lay in the communication trenches behind them.

He keyed the relay to the *5th* Line Phalanx.

"Forty-First," he said. "Massive attack along my entire front. Tanks, Hoplons, infantry, Laistrygons. Forward trench is lost. I've committed my reserve. I need reinforcements. Anything you've got."

There was a pause.

"*Forty-First, the Phalanx-General regrets that no reinforcements are available,*" the duty officer said. His voice had changed. "*The Fifth's reserve regiments are engaged against attacks of their own on Leaves 61 and 62. You are to hold with what you have.*"

"Hold with what I have," Halde said.

"*Yes, sir. I'm sorry, sir.*"

Halde put the handset down.

**THE SUPPORT LINE OF THE 44TH TRENCH REGIMENT, LEAF 59-30. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 211, FOURTH BELL**

Ilsa Marrow heard the *41st* die from three kilometres away, and the worst of it was that she had nothing to shoot at.

The *44th* was the right flank of the *2nd* Line Phalanx, which put it next to the *5th*, which put the *6th* Cohort of the *44th* on the left end of the *2nd*'s line, three kilometres to the right of the *41st*'s right flank, looking south at the second belt. She'd been standing on the fire-step of Bay 1 since the first bell with Brandt beside her, watching the smoke roll over Leaf 58-31 off to her left and listening to the relay. She could hear the *41st*'s regimental net. Everybody on that stretch of the line could. The centurions of the *41st* were shouting so loudly into their handsets that half the *2nd* Line Phalanx was getting it on the bleed.

She heard the forward trench of the *41st* go. She heard the reserve go forward and get cut up on the way. She heard the colonel ask for reinforcements and be told there weren't any.

"Gods below," Brandt said beside her. He had come back from the vats after K-19 with a new body and the same face and the same gloom. "Gods below, Centurion. They're getting fucked over there."

"I know."

"Can't we do something?"

"We're facing the wrong way, Saul." She looked down the line of her own fire bays, which ran east and west along the northern face of the second belt, every rifle in them pointing south. The *41st* was dying to the west of her, to her right as she faced the enemy, and every gun of the *44th* was pointing at the wrong piece of ash. "We're facing south. They're coming from the west. If they get through the *41st*..."

She didn't finish, and Brandt had already seen it.

If they got through the *41st*, they'd be behind the whole of the *5th* Line Phalanx. They would come down the inside of the *5th*'s line from the left and roll it up, one regiment after another, from west to east, exactly as the *4th* Assault Phalanx had rolled up the outer belt from K-19. And the *44th* would be next.

The relay chimed.

"*Forty-Fourth, this is Second Phalanx,*" said a voice she knew, deep and tired. It was Callix himself. "*All cohorts. The Forty-First is breaking. The Fifth is falling back to the east. You are to swing your left back and dig in facing west to hold the hinge of the line. I say again, swing your left back and face west. Sixth Cohort, Forty-Fourth, you're the hinge. Marrow, are you there?*"

She keyed her handset.

"Here, sir."

"*Dig me a hinge, Centurion. A kilometre. Facing west. From your left flank north, to cover the Fifth as it falls back. Can you do it?*"

"How long have I got?"

"*Until they get through the Forty-First,*" Callix said. "*Which will be some time today.*"

Marrow looked out over the parapet to the west, at the smoke over Leaf 58-31, and the long flat stretch of open ash between the *6th* Cohort's left flank and the *41st*'s right.

"*6th* Cohort," she said, turning round. "Picks and shovels. Everybody. Now."

---

They dug a kilometre of hinge in a cycle and a half under fire, and Marrow would remember it afterward as the hardest work the *6th* Cohort ever did.

There was no manual for it. The manual said you dug rifle pits and joined them into section trenches, and section trenches into maniple trenches, and you did it at night, under cover of your own guns, with a covering party out in front. The *6th* Cohort had none of that. It had the open ash of Leaf 59-30, running north from its left flank toward the Mora Cut, and the Archflamen's guns ranged on every metre of it, and the smoke drifting over from Leaf 58-31 on the wind, and the *41st* dying three kilometres away. So she put the cohort out on the ash in a long line facing west, every soldier with a pick or a shovel, and they started to dig, all of them at once, a single long continuous trench from the left flank of the *44th* north across the ash, and they kept digging while the shells came down.

The Arsenal's gunners saw them at once. They couldn't have missed them. Six hundred soldiers digging in the open on flat ash in the middle of the cycle, and the shells came down on them in their long careful patterns, walking up and down the line of the new trench, and every time a shell came down somebody went up. Marrow lost forty soldiers in the first hour. She lost sixty in the second. The ash was dry, for once, which was the only luck they had that cycle; it came out of the trench clean and stayed where it was thrown, and by the end of the second hour the trench was a metre deep, and by the end of the fourth, waist deep, and the soldiers in it could dig lying down.

At the end of the sixth hour, she went down the line of it with Brandt behind her, as she always did, and found that it was a kilometre long and a metre and a half deep and ran from the left flank of the *44th* north across Leaf 59-30 to the edge of a slag gully, and that the gully covered its northern end.

"It's not a trench," Brandt said, looking at it. "It's a ditch, Centurion."

"It's a ditch with six hundred soldiers in it facing the right way," Marrow said. "Fire bays. Every eight metres. Traverses. And somebody find me the heavy weapons."

The *44th*'s heavy weapons came up at the eighth hour, the regiment's own mortars and quad-launchers and the heavy rifles, swung round from the southern front and dragged across the ash on sledges by their crews. Marrow sited them herself. She put them at the back of the ditch, at intervals along it, every one of them laid to fire along the front of the new wire, flat and low across the ash at waist height, so that anything coming at the hinge from the west would come into a crossing fire. It was what the thralls had done to the *37th* on the Drum. She'd studied the Répertoire's report on the Drum until she could recite it, and she had decided that if the Archflamen's army could do that to the Third, the Third could do it right back.

At the tenth hour, the *41st*'s support line went off the relay.

At the twelfth hour, the first of the Arsenal's tanks came out of the smoke to the west and turned north, toward the hinge.

---

**THE COMMAND DUGOUT OF THE 41ST. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 212**

By nightfall there was no longer a front line on Leaf 58-31, and Brannoc Halde knew he wasn't going to see the morning.

The *41st* had fought through the whole of the cycle. It had fought in its forward trench until the forward trench was gone, and in its support line until the support line was gone, and now it was fighting in pockets, in fragments of trench scattered across three square kilometres of ash, without coordination, without orders, each pocket holding on with whatever it had. Both armies' guns were firing on the support line now. The Third's soldiers and the Arsenal's forward cohorts were so intermingled in it that the Archflamen's gunners and the *5th*'s gunners were killing their own as fast as the enemy's, and neither of them stopped.

The Arsenal had overrun nineteen of the *41st*'s heavy mortar and quad-launcher pits in the afternoon. Halde had watched it happen on the overlay, one green pip after another going red. In most of them the thralls had turned the guns round and fired them into the *41st*'s own trenches until the ready ammunition was spent. He'd lost more soldiers to his own mortars that afternoon than to the Laistrygons.

And between the pockets, in the dark, the Arsenal's tanks moved, and both sides probed, and the shooting never quite stopped.

The command dugout held. It was cut six metres under the support line, with a steel roof and a basalt overburden and a single stair up to the trench, and an observation slit at ground level facing west across the ash, and it had eighty-three soldiers in it now, crammed in shoulder to shoulder along the walls among the panes and the map table and the relay sets. They were the regimental staff, and the signallers, and the headquarters guard, and the walking wounded who had come in from the support line in the afternoon. Halde had held it against two assaults in the dark watch. The thralls had come down the trench toward the stair twice, with the Laistrygons in front of them, and twice the headquarters guard had stopped them at the head of the stair with rifles and grenades and breachers' carbines, and twice they had gone back.

The attack was renewed at the first bell of the second cycle. It reached the support line in its second hour. The trenches on either side of the command dugout went first, and then the dugout was alone.

Senna Oake was killed at the third bell. She was at the observation slit, with a periscope, calling the fall of the regiment's last mortars onto the trench outside, and an Arsenal Thureos came up out of the dark to within sixty metres of the dugout and fired through the slit. Halde was at the map table, three metres away. He didn't see it happen. He heard the shot, and felt the blast of it come through the dugout, and when he turned round, Oake was gone, and the slit was a hole, and the two signallers who had been standing beside her were gone too.

She'd be back. He told himself that, standing at the map table with her blood on his face. She'd be back in a fortnight. She'd come down the Stair and find out what had happened to the *41st*, and she'd be furious with him, and she'd tell him exactly what he should have done differently, in that sharp quick voice of hers, at length.

He picked up the relay handset. It was dead. The line to the rear had been cut some time in the night, and the wireless sets had been jammed since the barrage began.

He looked around the dugout. Eighty soldiers. A third of them wounded. The stair to the trench at one end, held by the last of the headquarters guard. The hole where the observation slit had been at the other. And outside, in the dark, the Arsenal.

"Listen to me," he said.

They listened. He had a voice that carried down a trench from one end to the other, and in the dugout it filled the room.

"Nobody in the *5th* knows what's happened here," he said. "They know the *41st* is in trouble. They don't know the *41st* is gone. They don't know the Arsenal's broken clean through us, and they don't know it's coming round behind them. If they don't find out, they'll be rolled up from the left before the next cycle's out, one regiment after the other, all the way to the *44th*." He paused. "Somebody's got to tell them."

Nobody said anything.

"I'm going to divide you," he said. "Half of you, the fit ones, are going to break out. You'll go out the back of this dugout, through the hole, and east across the ash, and you'll keep going until you reach the *5th*'s line, and you'll tell them what I just told you. The wounded are going with you, as many as can walk." He looked at the map table. "The rest of us are going to stay here and keep the Arsenal's attention on this dugout for as long as we can."

"Sir," said a young tribune of the staff, a boy named Aulus Tolle. "Sir, you should go with the breakout. They'll need an officer..."

"They'll have you, Tribune. You're leading it." Halde smiled at him. "I'm sixty, and I'm fat, and I can't run across eleven kilometres of ash. And somebody has to stay with the dugout." He looked at the faces around him in the lamplight. "I'm not going to ask for volunteers. I'll pick. Anybody who's been killed more than twenty times, stays. Anybody who's been killed less, goes. You'll all be back in a fortnight anyway."

---

The breakout group went out through the hole at the fourth bell. There were fifty-one of them, and the walking wounded, and Tolle at their head, and they went out into the dark and east across the ash at a run, and Halde never knew whether they got through.

They did. He would have been glad to know it.

He held the dugout with twenty-nine soldiers through four more assaults. The thralls came down the trench at the stair, and the Laistrygons came at the hole, and twice they got inside, and twice the twenty-nine threw them out again with carbines and grenades and entrenching tools and their bare hands. By the fourth assault there were eleven of them left. By the end of it, there were seven.

Then the engines came.

He heard them first, out in the dark beyond the hole, a deep grinding sound with a hiss under it, coming closer. He knew the sound of a Thureos, and this was something else, something heavier and slower, with a long rushing roar under it that he had heard before, somewhere, and couldn't place.

He went to the edge of the hole, carefully, and looked out.

It was a Hoplon chassis. He could see that much in the light of the burning ash around the dugout. The great ramp had been taken off the front of it, and in its place, on a heavy mount where the ramp's hinges had been, the Arsenal's armourers had fixed a long, black, fat barrel with a thick armoured hose running back from it into the body of the carrier. And under the hose, in the body of the carrier, where fifty soldiers would have sat, there was a glow. It came out through every seam and vent and vision slit of the carrier, a deep, dull, angry orange light, pulsing, and the air above the carrier shimmered with heat.

Now he knew where he had heard the sound before. He had heard it in the casting lines of the Arsenal, on a tour of inspection forty years before, when he was a young tribune. It was the sound of a heat-well.

"Pourer," one of the seven said behind him, in a whisper. "That's a pourer, sir. The thralls call them pourers. I heard about them from a deserter. They throw slag..."

The pourer stopped forty metres from the hole, and the long black barrel on its front came round, slowly, and lowered, and pointed at the dugout.

Halde stepped back from the hole. He looked round at the seven, in the lamplight, among the panes and the dead relay sets and the map table with the overlay of Leaf 58-31 still glowing on it.

"Twenty-two," he said. "That's mine. Twenty-two times. I'll be back in a fortnight. So will you."

He put his hand on the map table. He found that he wanted to say his own number aloud. Some soldiers did, at the end; it was supposed to help the implant fix the pattern, though the technical officers said it made no difference. He'd never done it before. He did it now, because there was nothing else to do.

"Halde," he said. "Brannoc. Colonel. Forty-First Trench Regiment. Nine-one-one, seven-four, three-three, alpha..."

The pourer fired.

It threw molten slag at a thousand degrees across forty metres in a long, rushing, glowing stream, white at the centre and orange at the edges, straight through the hole where the observation slit had been, and it filled the command dugout of the *41st* Trench Regiment from wall to wall in less than four seconds.

---

The relay logs of the *5th* Line Phalanx's signals section show that Colonel Brannoc Halde's implant fired at the seventy-first minute of the dark watch of the two hundred and twelfth cycle, and that the upload was handed to the noosphere. It never reached Watchpoint Hades. Nothing of Halde was ever recovered. The slag set in the dugout like stone.

**THE GUEST QUARTERS OF THE TABULARIUM. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 212, DARK WATCH**

Mirren was asleep when the bird went over, and it woke her up.

She'd been sleeping badly for two days. Everybody had. The strays had been moved out of the pens under the Tabularium in the second rotation, into the old guest quarters of the Enforcement keep, a row of whitewashed rooms with real beds and real blankets and windows that looked out across the gorge at the Acropolis, and the Archflamen sent them food from his own table every night, and the Flamens came every morning to say the rite over them. It was the best place Mirren had ever lived. But for two days the birds had been going up off the northern Flats in thousands, so many that she couldn't count them, and so loud that she couldn't sleep through them, and she had lain awake in the dark with her hands over her ears.

She had been asleep for an hour when the bird went over.

It was louder than any of the others. It was louder than the big bright bird near the bridge, louder than the two hundred and six. It came up off the Flats somewhere far to the north and rose toward the ceiling in a long rising cry, and it was calling out.

It was calling out a number. Over and over. Nine-one-one, seven-four, three-three, alpha...

She sat up in bed. She'd never heard a bird say anything before. They sang, and they cried, and sometimes they screamed, but they'd never said anything she could understand, and this one was saying a number, as clearly as if it were standing beside her bed.

And then something caught it, low over the Flats, and held it, and it went quiet.

She was out of bed and across the room and out into the corridor before she knew where she was going, barefoot, in her nightdress, and down the stairs to the room at the bottom where the Haruspex slept when he stayed in the Tabularium, which was most nights now. She hammered on the door. She'd never done that before either.

Dace opened it at once, as if he'd been awake. He was fully dressed, in his grey coat, with a lamp in his hand.

"Mirren," he said.

"There was a bird," she said. She was out of breath. "There was a bird, and it was calling a number. It said a number, Ilan. Over and over. And then it stopped."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he stepped back from the door.

"Come in," he said. "Sit down. And tell me the number."

---

**THE TABULARIUM RECORDS ROOM. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 213, FIRST BELL**

Ilan Dace had been a clerk for twenty-two years before he became a Haruspex, and he still thought of himself as one, and in the early hours of the two hundred and thirteenth cycle he went down to the records room of the Tabularium and did what clerks did best.

The records room was a long low chamber at the bottom of the keep, below the old office where Lictor-Superior Rusk had sat with her back to the Echo Mast and written reports nobody read. It had survived the storming. The Sworn had been told to leave it alone, and they had, and it was exactly as the Enforcement detachment had left it, row after row of iron filing cases holding the Tabularium's files on every soul who had ever passed through the Arsenal, and every soldier who had ever been posted near it, and every unit of the Third that had ever been stationed within sight of it. It also held, in a case at the far end that Dace had found on the night of the storming and kept the key to ever since, a complete copy of the personnel codes of the officers of the *3rd* Siege Army, sent down from the Quarterdeck four years before for an inspection that had never happened.

He took the lamp down the long room to the case at the far end and unlocked it. Mirren stood at the door behind him in her nightdress, with a blanket around her shoulders, and watched.

"Nine-one-one," Dace said to himself, running his finger down the index. "Seven-four. Three-three. Alpha." He took out a drawer. He went through the cards in it, slowly, by the light of the lamp, one at a time, as he had gone through cards all his life. "Alpha. Alpha..."

He stopped.

"*Halde, Brannoc,*" he read aloud. "*Colonel. Forty-First Trench Regiment, Fifth Line Phalanx. Nine-one-one, seven-four, three-three, alpha, two, six.*" He looked up. "A colonel, Mirren. A colonel of the Third. The Forty-First is the regiment Prefect Ostorian attacked on the northern Flats yesterday. The Prefect's runners say it was destroyed."

Mirren didn't say anything. She was looking at the card in his hand.

"You heard him go up," Dace said softly. "And you heard him say his number. And you heard him stop."

"Yes."

"Where is he now?"

She closed her eyes and listened. The birds were still going over, far fewer now than in the last two days, the long thin endless traffic of the noosphere passing high overhead. And under it, low down, over the northern Flats, among the other quiet ones that had been caught there over the months and hadn't gone anywhere, she could feel him. He was bigger than the others. He wasn't calling his number anymore. He was just there, hanging in the air over the Flats, quiet, the way they all were.

"He's still there," she said. "With the others. He's not singing."

Dace put the card down on top of the case. He was quiet for a long time.

"Mirren," he said finally. "Do you think you could call him?"

She opened her eyes.

"Call him?"

"Down. Here. To you. When you hear a bird like that, a loud one, saying its number, do you think you could call it down to you, before it goes quiet, before whatever catches them catches it?"

She stared at him.

"I don't know how," she said.

"No. I don't suppose you do." He came back down the long room toward her, with the lamp. "Neither do I. But I think we might be able to learn it together. And if we can, Mirren, then you and I will know everything that colonel knew. Every order he was given. Every map he saw. Every plan the Third had for the northern Flats."

He crouched down in front of her, as he always did, so that his face was level with hers.

"Will you try?" he said. "For me? For the Archflamen?"

Mirren looked at him, at his kind clerk's face in the lamplight, and the grey vial sticking out of the pocket of his coat, and she thought about the big bird calling its number over and over and then going quiet. She didn't want to. She didn't know why she didn't want to, but she was sure of it, the way she was sure of the birds.

"Yes," she said.

**THE HINGE, LEAF 59-30. ROTATION THREE, CYCLES 212 TO 216**

The Arsenal's tanks came at the hinge at the twelfth hour of the first cycle, and again at the fourth hour of the second, and again at dawn on the third, and the *6th* Cohort held.

Marrow held it with the heavy weapons. She had sited them to fire along the front of the new wire, flat and low across the ash at waist height, and when the Arsenal's tanks came out of the smoke to the west and turned north toward the hinge, with the Hoplons behind them and the thralls behind the Hoplons and the Laistrygons in front of the thralls, they came into a crossing fire exactly like the one the Third had walked into on the Drum.

The Laistrygons came first, as they always did. They came across the ash toward the ditch at the speed of a galloping horse, three metres tall, chains swinging from their collars, and the rifles of the *6th* Cohort fired into them and didn't stop them. The quad-launchers did. Marrow had told the quad-launcher crews to hold their fire until the Laistrygons were a hundred metres out, and they held it, and at a hundred metres they fired, and the quad-launchers of the *44th* fired four heavy rounds a second each, along the front of the wire, at waist height, and nothing three metres tall could cross that.

The tanks were harder. The Arsenal's Thureos tanks stopped at four hundred metres from the ditch, as they had on Leaf 58-31, and began to fire into it, and the *6th* Cohort had nothing that could reach them at four hundred metres except the regiment's mortars, which couldn't hit a moving tank. She lost a hundred soldiers to the tanks in the first assault. Then the *20th* Armoured Regiment came.

It came from the east, behind the hinge, along the inside of the *5th*'s line as the *5th* fell back. The *5th*'s Phalanx-General had gathered it up from the *4th* Assault Phalanx, which had lent it to the *5th* for the northern advance, and thrown it into the enemy's path, together with the *41st*'s surviving outrider squadrons. It had forty-eight tanks on the line, Thureos tanks and a squadron of old Ajax heavies, against more than a hundred and twenty of the Arsenal's by the outriders' reports. It had no hope of stopping the Arsenal, so it came up behind the hinge and went into hull-down positions behind the slag gully at the northern end of the ditch, and fired on the Arsenal's tanks from there as they sat four hundred metres out firing into the ditch, and the Arsenal's tanks turned to face it.

That was all the hinge needed.

The *20th* fought a series of delaying actions back across the Flats over the next three cycles, from one cinder rise to the next, and every cycle the Arsenal took less ground. Marrow watched it from the hinge. She watched the Ajax heavies go back across the ash in bounds, two at a time, one firing while the other moved, and she watched the Arsenal's Thureos come after them and burn. On the second cycle she watched an Ajax called *Sorrow of Callisto* hold a cinder rise alone for an hour against eleven of the Arsenal's tanks, and kill six of them, and then burn on the crest of the rise for the rest of the cycle with its crew still inside.

On the sixth cycle of the counter-attack, the Arsenal's armour was spent. The Laistrygons had used up their stimulants. The attack came to a halt.

It had driven a wedge eleven kilometres deep through the *5th*'s line and forced it to give up most of the ground it had taken since the fall of the outer belt. The *41st* Trench Regiment had been destroyed in two cycles, with seventy-six thousand of its soldiers transferred or captured, its colonel among them. The *20th* Armoured counted ninety-one enemy hulls on the Flats when the fighting was over, and the Répertoire put the Archflamen's losses at forty-one thousand, and judged the exchange favourable.

The hinge had held. The *6th* Cohort had lost three hundred and four soldiers holding it.

---

Marrow stood on the fire-step of the ditch at the end of the sixth cycle, with Brandt beside her and the ash in front of them heaped with the Arsenal's dead, and looked west across Leaf 59-30 at the burning hulls of the Arsenal's tanks and the great grey shapes of the dead Laistrygons lying among them.

"Ninety-one tanks," Brandt said. "That's a good number."

"It's a good number."

"Forty-one thousand of theirs." He paused. "That's a good number too."

"Saul."

"Centurion?"

"How many of the *41st*'s transfers were null?"

He didn't answer for a while. He'd heard it on the regimental net, the same as she had, an hour ago. Everybody in the *2nd* Line Phalanx had.

"Four thousand two hundred," he said. "And the colonel."

"And the colonel." Marrow looked out across the ash to the west, toward Leaf 58-31, where the command dugout of the *41st* Trench Regiment was a low black mound of solid slag in the middle of the Flats. "First officer above centurion to go null in the whole siege. Callix told me. Sixty years old. Twenty-two times up the ladder." She shook her head. "They say his implant fired. They say the upload went into the noosphere. It just never got to the Watchpoint."

"Jammers, they're saying."

"They're always saying jammers." She stepped down off the fire-step. "Come on. We've got a ditch to turn into a trench before the Arsenal decides to try again."

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 230**

The Command's inquiry into the loss of the *41st* Trench Regiment reported within the rotation. Cael Varros read it in his office at the stern of the Quarterdeck, with Lector Pell reading it over his shoulder.

It was a good report. It was fair, and careful, and correct. It found that the *5th* Line Phalanx had advanced on the second belt faster than its guns could follow, and that its regiments had halted to dig on ground the enemy had ranged in advance and could reach with every gun in the northern half of his line. It found that the sound of engines through the night had been reported to the *5th*'s headquarters and treated as a local matter. It found that the Third had no reliable estimate of the armour in the Arsenal's vaults and had assumed that the garrison would not risk it outside the belts. It was careful to add that every one of those decisions had been reasonable, given what was known at the time.

Its recommendations were adopted. The Third's advance would halt each cycle only within the cover of its own guns. Every regiment on the line would keep armour within a cycle's drive. The Third's spotters were ordered to treat any movement of vehicles behind the second belt as the preparation of an attack. And every regiment was issued additional anti-armour weapons to kill the pourers at range, and breachers' carbines for the forward cohorts to stop the Laistrygons.

The inquiry did not consider Colonel Halde's null. It noted it as a consequence of the slag that had destroyed his dugout, and listed it with the other four thousand two hundred nulls of the counter-attack as an effect of the Archflamen's jammers.

Varros read that paragraph twice. Then he took down the file marked *RUSK* from the shelf behind his desk, which was much thicker than it had been, and opened it, and put the inquiry's paragraph inside, behind Phalanx-General Callix's letter and the five books of nulls that Callix had sent him since.

"Sir," Pell said quietly. "The slag. The inquiry says the slag destroyed the pattern."

"I read it."

"Could it? I mean, physically? Can slag do that?"

"No," Varros said. "The implant fired. The relay logs of the *5th* show it. The pattern left his body at the seventy-first minute and went into the noosphere intact, and once it's in the noosphere, there's nothing in that dugout that can touch it. Slag or anything else." He closed the file. "Something touched it after it left."

"Then why does the inquiry say slag?"

"Because the inquiry was asked why the *41st* was lost," Varros said, "and the *41st* was lost for the three reasons it gives. Nobody asked it about Halde." He put the file back on the shelf. "That's the trouble with asking good questions, Lector. You get very good answers to exactly what you asked."

He sat for a while looking at the shelf.

"Draft a query," he said at last. "To the Répertoire's technical office. Ask them for the noosphere routing logs for the northern Flats, cycles two hundred and eleven to two hundred and thirteen. All bands. Including the ones the Arsenal uses."

"The Arsenal's bands are dark, sir. The Mast went silent in Rotation Minus One."

"The Mast stopped talking to us in Rotation Minus One," Varros said. "The *3rd*'s signallers have been noting for three rotations that it's still drawing power, and still transmitting, on bands we don't use. I'd like to know what it's been saying."
