## Chapter 21: Behemoth-Shock

*"We were told the engines would come. We had been told a great many things would come. Then the ground started shaking at the first bell, and somebody on the fire-step said, oh, and nobody in the bay could think of anything better to say."*

*Trooper Jory Cade,* 2nd *Cohort,* 29th *Trench Regiment*

---

**THE COCYTUS SHELF, FORLORN COHORT NINE. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 338**

The order reached Forlorn Cohort Nine on the ice at Depth 52 in the middle of the dark watch, and the centurion who read it out stood on an ammunition crate in the cold with a hand lamp held up to the slate, and read it twice over, and then looked out across the shelf at the soldiers in front of her with an expression nobody in the cohort could place.

"Former soldiers of the *29th* Trench Regiment," she said, "will fall out and form on the cable head. Bring everything. You're not coming back."

Jory Cade had been on the ice for a hundred and ninety cycles. He had come out of the vats at Watchpoint Hades two cycles after the colours burned, the fourth time in his life he had come out of a vat, and a depot clerk at Kappa had looked at the regiment on his tags and drawn a line through it with a stylus and written FORLORN 9 beside it, and he had been on the cables to the Cocytus before the end of the watch. He'd spent the hundred and ninety cycles since then chipping out firing positions in ice that refroze behind the pick, and standing sentry over a stretch of black water where nothing ever came up, and burying three of the cohort's soldiers who had gone null in their sleep from the cold and the long dark, which the medicus said was impossible and wrote down anyway.

He didn't move at first. Nobody did. The cohort stood in its long ragged rows on the ice and the wind came off the frozen river and went through all of them, and the centurion lowered her lamp.

"That's you, Cade," she said, quite gently. "And you, Abernethy. And the four of you at the back, I know who you are. Go on."

They went. There were nine of them from Forlorn Nine, and when they reached the cable head at the edge of the shelf there were forty more coming in along the ice from Forlorn Six and Forlorn Eleven, in ones and twos, with their packs on their backs and their rifles slung, and more coming down the long stair from the upper shelf, and nobody said much. Cade knew some of the faces and didn't know most of them. A big man from the *4th* Cohort with frost in his beard nodded to him. A woman from the heavy maniples whose name he'd never known stood a little apart with her arms folded and stared down at the cables, and when he looked at her she looked back and said, "Well, it can't be a firing squad. They'd have done it up here and saved the fuel," and three or four of them laughed, and that was as near as anyone came to talking about it on the way down.

The cable car took eleven hours to reach Kappa. They spent most of it asleep.

---

**KAPPA, THE PARADE GROUND. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 340**

There were eleven thousand of them on the parade ground at Kappa, in the end.

Cade hadn't known there would be so many. On the hundred and seventh cycle of Rotation Eight there had been nineteen hundred of them standing in a hollow square in this same place, watching the colours burn, and he had assumed somehow that the nineteen hundred were the *29th*, and the rest were dead. They weren't. They were the ones who had come back from Leaf 50-27 on their own feet. The others had come back through the vats, in the cycles after, and gone straight to the Forlorn Cohorts without ever being mustered with the regiment at all, and now the Forlorn Cohorts had given them up again and here they were, eleven thousand soldiers of a regiment that didn't exist, drawn up in their ice-burned greatcoats in long uneven ranks on the ash of the parade ground, while the depot staff walked up and down between them with slates and tried to sort them back into cohorts.

It took most of the cycle. The depot staff had the old rolls of the *29th* to work from, and the rolls were wrong in a hundred places, and the soldiers kept correcting them, and the depot staff kept being offended by it. Cade found himself standing in the *2nd* Cohort's ranks among men and women he'd last seen in the smoke on Leaf 50-27, and some of them he'd last seen dying, and they looked at each other and didn't quite know what to say.

Optio Merrin Holt was three files down from him in the *1st* Maniple.

He saw her at the third hour, when the depot clerk called the maniple's roll, and she answered to her name in the same flat hard voice he remembered from the fire-step, and he looked along the rank and found her, small and fierce and grey-haired, with a fresh pink scar from the ice along one cheekbone. She was looking straight ahead. He looked straight ahead too.

At the end of the roll she glanced along the rank at him, once, and he met her eyes and gave her the smallest nod he could manage, and she gave him one back, and that was all. He never spoke to her about Provost-Optio Vey in his life. He thought about it sometimes afterward, though, and he thought it was probably the most important conversation he ever had.

At the sixth hour a column of staff cars came up the road from the cable head, and a short heavy grey-haired man in a Marshal's greatcoat got out of the second one and walked out onto the parade ground without waiting for anyone to call the regiment to attention.

Somebody did anyway. Eleven thousand soldiers came to attention with a long ragged crash of boots on ash, and the Marshal stopped in front of them with his hands behind his back and looked at them for a long time.

Cade had heard of him. Everybody on the ice had heard of him by then. Marshal Theron Kastellan, seventy-nine, who'd held the Ophion Gate for nine rotations with a single Line Phalanx when the Command had given it up for lost, and who had come down the cables to the Arsenal on the three hundredth cycle and sacked half the Lord-General's staff within the week. The ice said he was a hard old bastard. The ice said he had walked the whole Fifty-Fourth's sector on the Lethe Cut on his second cycle in command, on foot, alone except for his adjutant, and come back and gone into his office and not come out for a day.

"My name is Kastellan," he said. He didn't raise his voice. The depot had rigged a speaker horn on a pole behind him, and it carried his voice out across the parade ground flat and quiet and close, and Cade had the odd sense that the old man was talking to him alone. "I command the Third now. You'll have heard that. You'll have heard what I'm doing here, too, and some of you will have heard it wrong, so I'll tell you myself."

He walked a few paces along the front rank, looking at them.

"The *29th* Trench Regiment was disbanded on the hundred and sixth cycle of Rotation Eight by order of the Lord-General, for cowardice in the face of the enemy. Its colours were burned on this ground. Seven of its officers were made null by order." He stopped. "I've read the papers. I've read the Antilochus orders and the Répertoire's figures for Leaf 50-27 and the provost reports and the inquiry, and I've read the medicus's register for the night of the raid on Lance Seven. I don't intend to say what I think of any of it. It's done. I can't undo it."

He looked along the ranks.

"What I can do is this. As of this hour the *29th* Trench Regiment is raised again, on my authority, in the *7th* Line Phalanx. You'll have a new colonel and new colours. You'll be filled from the drafts. You'll train for forty cycles and then you will go back into the line." He paused. "I'll tell you where. You've a debt on Leaf 50-27. I expect you to pay it."

Eleven thousand soldiers stood without a sound on the ash. Cade stood at attention in the second rank of the *2nd* Cohort and felt something come up in his chest so hard and so suddenly that for a moment he couldn't breathe, and he didn't know whether it was grief or rage or something else altogether, and he stood there with it and stared straight ahead at nothing.

Kastellan stood a moment longer. Then he turned and walked back to the staff cars, and the depot centurion shouted the regiment to stand easy, and nobody stood easy at all.

---

The new colours came three cycles later, crimson and gold, with nothing on the hoist.

The new colonel came with them. Her name was Fabia Dunmore, and she was forty-six and had come up through the *11th* Armoured and then two Trench Regiments on the Ophion front, and she had a bad leg from a Kingsbane round at the Ophion Gate that made her walk with a slight roll, and a gravel-chute voice. She called the officers together in a dugout at the depot on her first cycle, and the centurions of the *2nd* told it afterward to the whole regiment, until everyone could quote it.

"I've read the file," she said. "Somebody shot two provost-optios in your trenches during Antilochus. I don't know who. I'm not going to ask. I'm going to tell you what I'll do if anybody in this regiment ever has to shoot one of mine, and that is I'll find out why, and if the reason's good I'll hang the provost-optio's body off the nearest lance silo myself and write the Lord Marshal an apology for the waste of rope." She looked around the dugout. "There won't be any more provost-optios in the *29th*. There won't be a firing squad. There'll be me. Any questions?"

There were none.

The drafts came in over the next twenty cycles, thousands of them, raw and pale and frightened and loud, straight off the cables from the ceiling foundries and the ration offices of the Lethe Hold and the dock-wards of the upper Cocytus, and every cohort of the *29th* took its share. That was Kastellan's doing as well, the depot said. Every cohort on the Flats was getting its drafts mixed in among the veterans now, a handful to a section, so that every raw recruit who had never seen the Flats would go over the top for the first time with somebody beside them who had.

Cade got four of them in his section. One of them was a seventeen-year-old ration clerk from the Lethe Hold named Oriel Tansy, thin and blond and painfully earnest, who asked him on the first cycle whether it was true that the *29th* had been the regiment that broke on Leaf 50-27.

"Yes," Cade said.

"And you were there?"

"Yes."

Tansy thought about this.

"My cousin was in the *54th*," Tansy said. "On the Lethe Cut. They've listed him absent." He looked at Cade. "Do you know what that means? Absent? Nobody will tell my aunt."

Cade looked at him for a while.

"No," he said, at last. "I don't. Sit down, Tansy, and I'll show you how to strip that rifle before you lose a finger in it."

**THE CRADLE-YARD BELOW THE DRUM. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 190**

The Hephaestean servicing companies had built the cradle-yard for the Forge Cohort in the ruins of the old outer belt below the Drum, and it had taken them forty cycles, and by the time it was done it was the largest structure the Third had raised on the Cinder Flats since the siege began.

It had to be. A Brontes-pattern Behemoth stood eighty metres from its feet to the top of its carapace, and weighed more than the whole of the Drum, and when it came back from a fight it needed to be held upright in a gantry while the servicing crews climbed over it and cut the damaged plate away and welded new plate on and drew the spent reactor cores and fed new ones in, and the gantries had to be anchored deep in the basalt to take the load. Twenty-two of them stood in the cradle-yard now, in two long rows of bronze-black scaffolding a kilometre long, with the engines standing in them dark and still and enormous, and cranes moving among them, and the blue light of the cutting torches flickering over everything day and night.

Doros Varne spent most of the cycles before the offensive out of his cradle, which was rare for him.

He walked the yard. He went up the gantries on the long cage lifts and stood on the walkways at the height of the engines' shoulders and watched the crews work, and he talked to the cradle-masters of the other engines one by one, at length, as the old cradle-lords had always done before a war. He was two hundred and eleven years old, and out of his cradle he moved slowly, a tall stooped man with a long white beard and the old augmetic sockets at his temples and the base of his skull standing out bronze and bright against his skin, and the servicing crews stopped work and stood when he came past, and he waved them back down irritably every time.

"They do it at the Forge," he told Hesione Calder, the cradle-master of the *Steadfast*, on the hundred and ninetieth cycle. "I've been telling them to stop for a hundred years. Nobody listens to an old man."

"They listen," Calder said. "They just don't do what he says."

She was the youngest of his cradle-masters, at sixty-four, a broad square woman with a shaved head and a burn scar down one side of her neck from a reactor breach in the deep cradles when she was an apprentice, and she was the only one of them who ever argued with him. He valued that more than he had ever told her. They were standing on the walkway at the *Steadfast*'s shoulder, sixty metres up, looking out across the yard at the Flats beyond.

The bombardment had been going for nine cycles. From the walkway they could see the whole of it, the long arc of the gun lines curving away north and south across the ash for a hundred kilometres and more, every gun firing, the flashes running along the arc in a continuous flickering line, and beyond it, on the far side of no-man's land, the inner belt under the shells, a long low ridge of smoke and fire with the dark hump of the Cinder Wall rising beyond it and the Acropolis beyond that, and above the Acropolis, faint in the ash, the thin dark needle of the Mast. The sound reached them up here as a single unbroken roar, felt more than heard, a pressure in the chest and in the steel of the walkway under their feet.

"The ordnance crews say the barrels are glowing at night now," Calder said. "They're wearing out the breech blocks faster than the Quiet Ones can send new ones down. The recoil pistons on the Phaethons are going too. One blew yesterday on the *4th* Ordnance Phalanx's line and took the whole crew."

"I heard."

"They won't hold out another ten cycles of this."

"They won't need to."

Calder looked at him sideways. "The Marshal's set the date, then?"

"The two hundred and fortieth. He told me this morning." Varne put his hands on the rail. "He told me something else as well. He can't order us. He knows it. The lines of command don't run from the Third to the Forge, and they never have. So he came to the yard himself, at the first bell, and stood where you're standing, and asked me where I meant to put the engines."

"And?"

"I told him I'd put them on the front of the *7th* Line Phalanx," Varne said. "All of them. Leaf 54-45 to Leaf 50-27. Four regiments. The *22nd*, the *37th*, the *49th* and the *29th*."

"The *29th*." Calder frowned. "That's the one that broke."

"It's the one that broke," Varne said. "He's raised it again and put it back in the line opposite the place it broke. He says it has a debt there. He asked me to walk in front of it."

Calder was quiet for a while.

"He's a hard old bastard, isn't he," she said.

"He's a soldier," Varne said. "The Third's been short of them."

He looked at the *Steadfast* beside them, at the vast curved bronze-black flank of it falling away beneath the walkway, with the eagle of the Empire and the bronze hammer of Talos painted on the shoulder plate in front of them taller than either of them, and the honour banners hanging from the carapace, three hundred years of them, and the long barrels of its turbine-cannon folded down along its side for servicing.

"How is she?" he asked.

Calder knew what he meant. Every cradle-master knew. He wasn't asking about the plate or the reactors or the guns.

"Restless," she said, at last. "Since the ichor. Since before it, honestly, Lord, since we came below Depth 30 in the forge-lighter. She keeps turning toward the river. I lie in the cradle at night and I can feel her doing it, turning and turning, toward something down there, and when I try to turn her back she won't come." She hesitated. "Is the *Anvil* the same?"

"Yes."

"Do you know what it is?"

Varne looked out across the Flats toward the river, toward the long red glow in the ash on the far horizon, past the Arsenal, where the Phlegethon ran.

"I've an idea," he said. "I'd rather not say it aloud until I'm sure."

**LEAF 54-45, THE *22nd* TRENCH REGIMENT. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 239**

Colonel Imre Saldane had commanded the *22nd* Trench Regiment for six years, and had been in it for thirty-one, and on the night before the Kastellan Offensive he went round every fire bay on Leaf 54-45 in the dark, the whole two kilometres of the regiment's front, and spoke to every section he found awake.

It took him most of the watch. His adjutant, a tired young man named Corso Penn, followed him with a hand lamp and a slate of the cohort returns and tried to stop him going into the bays nearest the front, and failed. The bombardment was still going on overhead, a continuous roar of shells going over to the inner belt eight hundred metres away, and the night was lit red and orange and white by the bursts along the ridge, and there was enough light in the bays to see the faces of the soldiers in them.

Saldane was sixty, short and bald and broad-shouldered, with a big bent nose that had been broken on the Cinder Stair in Rotation Zero and never set properly, and a habit of talking to his soldiers with his hands in his pockets, which the Phalanx-General had twice told him was unbecoming in a regimental commander. He had kept his hands in his pockets both times.

"Who's this?" he said, in the fifth bay of the *3rd* Cohort's forward trench, stopping in front of a pale young trooper sitting on the fire-step with her rifle across her knees.

"Draft, sir," said her centurion. "Trooper Ismay Fell. Came in with the convoy."

"Fell." Saldane looked at her. "Where from, Fell?"

"The dock-wards, sir," the trooper said. Her voice cracked. "Upper Cocytus. I was a crane-hand."

"Ever been on the Flats before?"

"No, sir."

"Scared?"

The trooper looked at her centurion, and back at Saldane, and seemed to decide something.

"Yes, sir," she said. "Shitting myself, sir."

"Good," Saldane said. "So am I. The ones who aren't are idiots, and they die first, and they take the people round them with them. You stick close to your centurion tomorrow, Fell. She's been doing this nine rotations and she's still here, and that's not luck, whatever she tells you." He took one hand out of his pocket, briefly, and laid it on the trooper's shoulder. "And when you see the engines, try not to stand there with your mouth open. Everybody's going to want to. Somebody's got to keep shooting."

He went on to the next bay. Penn followed him with the lamp.

"Sir," Penn said, in the communication trench, "the Phalanx-General's staff want to know where you'll be during the assault. For the command net."

"With the standard."

"Sir, the orders say regimental commanders will direct the assault from the regimental command post."

"I know what the orders say."

"Sir..."

"Corso," Saldane said, and stopped in the trench and turned round, and looked at his adjutant in the red light of the shells. "That standard came down the Cinder Stair in Rotation Zero. I carried it for the last two hundred metres, after Signifer Arlo went down. The *22nd* was the first regiment of the Third to reach the Flats and it was that standard that went first. Tomorrow the *22nd* is going to go into the inner belt behind twenty-two Behemoths of the Forge of Hephaestus, which nobody in the Third has seen in a fight for six hundred years, and the soldiers of this regiment are going to look to their left and to their right to see whether their colonel's there." He put his hand back in his pocket. "Tell the Phalanx-General's staff I'll be on the command net from the standard. They can reach me on my band. If they need to shout at me, they're welcome to."

Penn sighed.

"Yes, sir," he said.

They went on. In the *1st* Cohort's lines they found the regiment's signifer, Rosamund Tey, sitting in the regimental command dugout with the standard in its case across her knees, oiling the staff by lamplight. She was a tall, rangy, weathered woman of forty, with eleven transfers, and she had carried the standard for four rotations, and she looked up when Saldane came in and didn't stand, which was a privilege of signifers that most colonels found irritating and Saldane didn't.

"Sir," she said.

"Signifer." Saldane sat down on an ammunition box across from her. "All set?"

"All set, sir." She looked at the case. "I've been thinking about whether to take it out of the case before we go up the ladder or after. If I take it out before, it catches on the revetment. If I take it out after, I've got to stand on the parapet and unwrap it with the whole inner belt shooting at me."

"What did Arlo do?"

"Signifer Arlo carried it uncased from the bottom of the Stair, sir. The whole way."

"And he went down two hundred metres from the Flats."

"Yes, sir."

Saldane thought about it.

"Take it out before," he said. "Hold it low going up the ladder. Raise it when you're on the parapet. I'll be on your left. If anyone in this regiment wants to look for the standard tomorrow, I want them to be able to find it."

"Yes, sir."

He sat a while longer in the dugout with her, saying nothing, listening to the shells go over. Then he got up and went out, and Penn followed him with the lamp, and they went on along the line toward the *2nd* Cohort, where there was a section still awake round a brazier in the sixth bay, playing cards for ration biscuits.

At the first bell, the guns stopped.

**THE CRADLE-YARD. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 240, FIRST BELL**

Varne lay in his cradle aboard the *Anvil of Talos* and felt the silence come down over the Flats.

He felt it through the *Anvil*'s augurs before he heard it, a sudden great absence in the sound-fields, the roar of a hundred thousand guns cut off all at once, and then the engine's own deep hearing filling up the space where the guns had been with everything else: the shouts in the trenches, the whistles, the clatter of ladders, the long grind of the Thureos and the Colossi starting their engines in the assembly trenches behind the line, and beyond it all, out across the ash, a deep low rumble from the inner belt that was the Archflamen's garrison coming up its shafts to its guns.

He had been joined since the dark watch. He lay in the cradle with the links seated at his temples and the base of his skull, in the warm dark of the command deck seventy metres above the ash, with the cradle-crew at their stations round him, and the *Anvil*'s mind lay round his own, vast and slow, and turned and stirred, and waited.

"All engines," he said into the relay, quietly. "Walk."

The *Anvil of Talos* came out of its gantry.

He felt every step of it. He felt the great bronze feet lift and come down on the ash and the ash give way beneath them, and the gyroscopes in the engine's heart holding the whole vast weight of it upright, and the reactors under the carapace rising to full output with a deep low thrumming that went through his bones. He felt the turbine-cannon unfold from along the engine's sides and lock into place and the shells feed up into their breeches from the magazines, and he felt the Aegis-wards come up round the engine one after another, three of them, layered, a pale shimmering in the augur-fields that hung about the carapace.

Behind him and to either side, the other twenty-one came out of the yard.

He felt them all. That was the gift of the cradle-lord, and its burden: he felt every engine of the Forge Cohort Talos through the relay, faintly, at the edges of his own joining, every engine's mind and every engine's step, so that when the whole cohort walked out of the yard together he walked with twenty-two bodies. The ten Brontes in their long line, the *Anvil* at their centre and the *Steadfast* on its right. The twelve Steropes on the wings, lighter and quicker, already pulling ahead to screen. He felt Calder in the *Steadfast*'s cradle, her mind bright and steady and hard. He felt the *Steadfast* itself under her, restless, turning toward the river, and Calder turning it back.

They walked out of the cradle-yard and down through the old outer belt toward the Third's forward trenches, and the Flats shook under them.

---

**LEAF 54-45. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 240, FIRST BELL**

Signifer Tey went up the ladder with the standard held low in front of her, and stepped up onto the parapet, and raised it.

Saldane was on her left. He'd gone up the ladder first, against every regulation in the Third, and he stood on the parapet in the grey half-light of the first bell with his hands in his pockets and the inner belt eight hundred metres in front of him under a long low pall of smoke, and he watched the standard go up beside him, crimson and gold and silver, the eagle and the long column of honours down the hoist, *The Cinder Stair* at the top in faded thread, and felt the whole *22nd* come up the ladders all along the regiment's front and see it.

Then the inner belt opened fire.

The machine guns came first, as they always did, a long ripping rattle from the forward trenches of the belt, low along the ground, and then the mortars, and then the field guns in their pits further back, and the ash in front of the *22nd*'s parapet went up in fountains. The first wave of the *3rd* Cohort was already out across the ash, moving at a fast walk in open order with their rifles at the port, and Saldane saw a dozen of them go down in the first few seconds, and heard their implants tick through the command band, one after another.

"Smoke," he said into the band. "Mortars. Now. On the forward trench. Penn, where are the Thureos?"

"Coming up behind the *1st* Cohort, sir. Three minutes."

"Tell them to hurry the fuck up."

He walked forward off the parapet into the ash, and Tey came with him with the standard, and behind them the *1st* Cohort came up out of the trench in its long lines and followed. The regiment's mortar batteries opened up from the support trench behind them, and the smoke rounds came down on the inner belt's forward trench in a long white bank, and the machine-gun fire from the belt slackened and grew ragged. The Thureos came grinding up through the gaps in the *22nd*'s own wire behind them, a long line of them, low and blunt and heavy, their hull-guns already firing into the smoke.

They were three hundred metres out when the ground began to shake.

Saldane felt it first through the soles of his boots. A long, slow, deep tremor, rhythmic, coming up through the ash, and then the sound that came with it, a sound he had never heard before in thirty-one years in the *22nd*, a vast slow booming tread that came from behind the regiment and went right through his body and on out across no-man's land toward the inner belt. Around him, all along the line, the soldiers of the *22nd* had stopped. They were turning round. They were looking back.

He turned and looked back too, and he forgot entirely about keeping shooting.

The Behemoths of the Forge Cohort Talos came out of the smoke behind the *22nd*'s trench in a long line, and they were the largest things he had ever seen move. They walked. He knew they would walk, he'd read the briefings, he'd seen them pass his trench on the three hundred and fourth cycle at a distance; he hadn't stood under one. They came striding out of the ash on legs as thick as the Drum's towers, eighty metres high, bronze-black, with the eagle and the hammer on their shoulders and their honour banners streaming from the carapaces in the hot wind off the gun lines, and the pale shimmer of their wards hanging round them, and the ash rolling away from their feet in great slow waves. The nearest one came over the *22nd*'s forward trench in a single stride, the whole trench passing under it, and its foot came down twenty metres to Saldane's left with a shock that threw half the *1st* Cohort off their feet, and it walked on past them into no-man's land with its turbine-cannon swinging round toward the inner belt, and its shadow went over him.

Somebody near him was laughing. He looked round. It was Tey. She was standing in the ash with the standard held high and her face turned up toward the engine as it went by, laughing aloud, tears running down through the ash on her cheeks.

"Signifer," he said.

"Sir."

"Keep walking."

"Yes, sir."

They kept walking. The *22nd* went forward in the shadows of the Behemoths across the last of no-man's land, and in front of them the Forge Cohort opened fire on the inner belt.

---

Varne felt the first salvo go out of the *Anvil*'s turbine-cannon, and the whole engine rock back on its heels with the recoil, and the gyroscopes catch it.

The shells went into the inner belt's forward trench three hundred metres ahead, and the forward trench disappeared. He watched it through the augurs. He saw a section of the belt two hundred metres long go up in one great rolling column of fire and black basalt, the trench and the bunkers and the machine-gun nests and the wire all together, and come down again in a rain of stone, and where it had been there was a crater twenty metres deep. All along the line of the *7th* Line Phalanx's front, the other engines were firing too, and he felt them do it through the relay, each one a deep slow shock in the back of his own mind, and the inner belt in front of them went up in fire along four leaves at once.

The belt fired back.

He'd known it would. He'd read Corvis's papers on the inner belt, and Castorre's, and the Répertoire's, and he knew what was in it, and he felt it come at the *Anvil* now from every direction at once. Machine-gun rounds rattling off the carapace and the legs. Field-gun shells bursting against the outermost ward with sharp white flashes. A divine mortar, somewhere in the belt, laying a keyed round on the ward that lit it up gold and set it ringing in Varne's head with a high thin note that went on and on. And then, from the silos behind the forward trench, the lances.

Three of them fired flat across the Flats at once, the long white beams lancing out through the smoke at the *Anvil*, and struck the wards, and the wards held.

He felt it as heat. The outermost ward flared white and burned and went down, all at once, and the second took the beams and burned and held, and through the cradle links he felt the *Anvil*'s reactors surge to feed it, a great rising heat in the engine's heart. And then the lances went out, their charge spent, and the second ward steadied, and the outermost came back up round the engine, flickering and pale, and the *Anvil* walked on.

"Lance silos," he said into the relay. "Bearing two hundred and eighty, two hundred and ninety-five, three hundred and ten. All Brontes. Fire."

The ten Brontes of the Forge Cohort Talos turned their turbine-cannon on the lance silos of the inner belt and fired together, and the silos went.

He had read that the silos had been built to withstand anything that came down out of the ceiling, and that the Third had nothing on the ground as heavy. That had been true. It was no longer true. He watched the first of the three silos take four salvoes from four engines, and crack across its great basalt dome, and split, and the lance inside it came up out of the ruin with its housing torn open and its focusing coils hanging loose and fell over sideways into the belt with a crash he felt through the ash a kilometre away. The second silo took six salvoes and went the same way. The third took eleven, and when it went it went all at once, the charge in its capacitors going with it in a long white flash that lit the whole inner belt from end to end, and when it cleared there was a hole in the belt half a kilometre across.

The relay was full of voices. He heard the regimental commanders of the *7th* Line Phalanx shouting over each other on the command band, the *22nd* and the *37th*, the *49th*, the *29th*, and through it all, faint and far off, someone laughing.

He walked on.

---

The counter-attack came at the third hour.

Saldane saw it coming out of the smoke from the second trench of the inner belt, where the *22nd* had gone to ground in the ruins of the first with the Thureos round it. Tanks first, a long column of them, Arsenal-pattern heavies in the Archflamen's black and red, grinding up out of a communication trench behind the belt with their turrets traversing, and infantry behind them in their hundreds, thralls in grey and Sworn in black, and behind them all, coming at a loping run through the smoke with their blades glowing, a dozen Cinderborn.

"Fuck," said Penn, beside him, with great feeling.

"Thureos," Saldane said into the band. "Engage the tanks. *1st* and *3rd* Cohorts, hold this trench. Mortars on the infantry. Penn, get me the engines."

"Sir, I don't know if I can..."

"Get me the engines."

Penn got him the engines. It took ninety seconds, and in the ninety seconds the Arsenal tanks reached the first trench and two of the Thureos were burning, and the thralls were in among the ruins of the bunkers on the *22nd*'s left, and Saldane was standing in the bottom of a shell crater with his pistol out of his pocket for once, shooting at grey figures coming over the lip of it, with Tey beside him holding the standard up in the smoke with one hand and firing her own pistol with the other.

"Sir," Penn said, crouching beside him with the handset. "Cradle-Lord Varne."

Saldane took the handset.

"Lord," he said. "This is Saldane, *22nd*. I've a tank column and infantry and Cinderborn in my first trench on Leaf 54-45. Can you help?"

There was a pause on the line, full of static and something under the static, a deep low sound that he realised was the engine breathing.

"Keep your heads down, Colonel," said Varne's voice, old and dry and calm. "Thirty seconds."

Saldane looked up out of the crater.

The *Anvil of Talos* was four hundred metres away to his right, standing in the ruins of the inner belt's forward trench with its turbine-cannon trained on something further off, toward the silos. As he watched, it turned. The whole vast engine turned round on its legs, slowly, the carapace swinging round over the ash, and its cannon came down and round until they were pointing straight along the line of the *22nd*'s first trench, straight at him, it seemed, and he had time to think *oh, shit*, and to shout to Tey and Penn to get down, and to get down himself.

The *Anvil* fired.

He never saw the shells. He felt them go over, a long tearing shriek in the air above the crater, and then the Arsenal tank column in the first trench two hundred metres away went up in a single continuous explosion, one tank after another along the line, eleven of them, the turrets coming off and going up into the smoke on columns of fire, and the infantry round the tanks were gone, and the Cinderborn were gone, and the trench where they had been was a long smoking furrow in the basalt full of burning wreckage. It took less than a minute. He'd counted. He'd been lying in the bottom of the crater with his face in the ash and his hands over his head counting, and he'd got to fifty-one before the shooting stopped.

He got up slowly. Tey was getting up beside him. The standard was still in her hand.

"Lord," he said into the handset. "Thank you."

"Push on, Colonel," Varne said, and the line went dead.

**LEAF 50-27, THE *29th* TRENCH REGIMENT. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 240, SECOND HOUR**

The *29th* went over the top at the first bell beside the *49th*, from the same trench on Leaf 50-27 it had gone over from on the first cycle of Antilochus, and Jory Cade went up the same ladder.

He hadn't meant to. The *2nd* Cohort had been given the old *2nd* Cohort bays without anyone thinking about it, and Cade had come into the trench in the dark watch and gone down the line to his section's bay and found himself standing in front of the same fire-step, the same splintered revetment, the same ladder with the same broken third rung that had been broken on the first cycle of Antilochus and that nobody in two rotations had replaced. He had stood and looked at it for a long time. Then he'd sat down on the fire-step with his section round him and checked their rifles and their ammunition one by one, and Tansy's twice, and waited for the guns to stop.

Lance Seven was gone.

That was the first thing he saw when he came up over the parapet. The dark hump of the silo on the second belt three hundred metres in front of the trench, which had stood there every morning of his life for two rotations, which he'd looked at every morning past the thirty-one, wasn't there. The Third had taken the second belt in Rotation Eight, the cycle after Antilochus, from another direction, and lost it again after Gallows Rise, and the Archflamen's garrison had come back into it, and nobody had ever repaired Lance Seven. The Phaethons had smashed it in the eleven cycles of Antilochus. There was a long low mound of shattered basalt where the dome had been, and a crater in front of it, and rubble.

The wire was still there, though. The second strand ran in front of the rubble as it had always done, rusted and sagging and shot through in a hundred places, and the thirty-one were still on it.

He'd known they would be. He saw them anyway.

They hung on the stakes in their long grey row in front of the rubble, three hundred metres away, in the half-light of the first bell. Nothing much of them remained. Two rotations of ash and seep-storms and shellfire had been at them, and little was left that anyone could have called a soldier. But the stakes were still standing and the cords were still on them, and the shapes on the cords were still there, thirty-one shapes, a few metres apart, and the ash had banked against the feet of them in long grey drifts. He looked for the fourteenth from the left. He found her.

"Cade," said his centurion, beside him. "Move."

He moved. The *2nd* Cohort went forward across the ash at a fast walk in open order toward the wire, and the old second belt opened fire on them as it had always done. The machine guns, the mortars, the long rattling chatter out of the rubble of Lance Seven, and the ash going up in fountains, and soldiers beginning to fall. Cade heard Tansy cry out behind him and turned and saw the boy flat on his face in the ash with his hands over his head, unhurt, and went back and hauled him up by his webbing and shoved him forward.

"Walk," he said. "Walk, Tansy. Keep walking. Don't look at them."

"At what?"

"Keep walking."

They were a hundred metres out when the Behemoths came.

He heard them before he saw them, the long slow booming tread through the ash, and felt it in his feet, and then the shadow came over him, and he looked up. One of the Steropes was going past on the *29th*'s left, sixty metres tall, bronze-black, moving fast, at a long loping stride that ate up the ground, with its cannon traversing toward the rubble of Lance Seven. Behind it came another. And then, further off to the right, on the front of the *49th*, one of the great Brontes, walking slowly through the ash with its honour banners streaming and its wards shimmering round it, and as he watched it fired.

The rubble of Lance Seven went up.

The whole mound of it, the shattered dome and the machine-gun nests in it and the bunker underneath and everything, went up in a single enormous blast that he felt in his teeth three hundred metres off, and came down in a rain of black stone over the ash and the wire, and when the smoke cleared the second belt in front of the *29th* was silent. The Steropes were already past it, walking on toward the inner belt beyond. The *2nd* Cohort stood in the ash and stared.

Then somebody started running.

Cade never found out who it was. He thought, later, that it might have been Holt. Somebody in the *1st* Maniple started running toward the wire, and somebody else followed, and then the whole *2nd* Cohort of the *29th* Trench Regiment was running across the last two hundred metres of no-man's land on Leaf 50-27, shouting, with their rifles in their hands, toward the thirty-one, and he was running with them.

He reached the wire. He reached the fourteenth from the left.

He stood in front of her. The ash was banked against her feet up to the knee. There was nothing much left of her face. The dressing at the base of the skull was still there, he saw, what was left of it, a grey rag under the cords, with a black stain in it that hadn't faded in two rotations of weather.

He took out his knife and cut the cords.

All along the wire round him, the *2nd* Cohort was doing the same. Soldiers with their knives out, cutting, and other soldiers catching what came down off the stakes, and laying them gently in the ash, one by one. Nobody spoke. The Behemoths had walked on toward the inner belt and the sound of them was further off now, and the guns of the inner belt were firing at them, and over it all the *2nd* Cohort stood at the wire in front of Lance Seven and cut down the thirty-one.

Tansy came up behind him.

"Who are they?" he asked. His voice had gone small.

"The *29th*," Cade said. He had Tamm in his arms. She weighed almost nothing. "From the raid on Lance Seven. In Rotation Six. They were taken and they were tied up here and they died here." He looked down at her. "This is Iska Tamm. Breacher. Heavy maniples. I was her loader."

Tansy looked at her for a long time.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Yeah," Cade said.

They carried them back across no-man's land themselves.

The centurion of the *2nd* Cohort said afterward that he'd tried to stop it, that the orders were to push on to the inner belt and leave the dead for the burial details, and that he'd stood in the ash with the order in his mouth and looked at his soldiers and found that he couldn't say it. So he didn't. He sent the *3rd* Maniple on behind the Steropes to hold the second belt, and the rest of the *2nd* Cohort came back across the three hundred metres of no-man's land to the *29th*'s trench in the middle of the Kastellan Offensive, carrying the thirty-one, one soldier to each, slowly, through the ash.

Medicus Valcourt was waiting at the parapet.

Cade didn't know how she'd known. She'd been posted to the *49th*'s aid station since the *29th* was disbanded, a kilometre away down the line, and nobody had sent for her. But she was there, standing on the fire-step of the *2nd* Cohort's forward bay in her stained grey medicus's coat, with her face set, and when Cade came up to the parapet with Tamm in his arms she reached up and took her from him, carefully, and laid her down in the bottom of the bay.

"Fourteenth from the left," Valcourt said.

"Yes, Medicus."

Valcourt knelt beside her in the sump and looked at her for a while, and put her hand on what was left of Tamm's shoulder.

"I told you," she said to Cade, without looking up, "on the sixth cycle of Antilochus. I told you she asked me for a grenade, and I didn't have one."

"Yes, Medicus."

"I've carried one since." Valcourt touched the cord at her neck. "Every watch. For two rotations. For her."

She sat back on her heels. Down the length of the trench, the soldiers of the *2nd* Cohort were coming over the parapet one after another with the thirty-one in their arms and laying them down along the bottom of the bays, in a long row, with their heads toward the wall.

"They'll go to Kappa," Valcourt said. "I'll see to it. They'll be burned with the honours of the regiment, every one of them, if I have to go to Quarterdeck Nineteen and stand in front of the Marshal myself." She stood up. "Go back, Cade. Your cohort's out there."

Cade looked down at Tamm once more. Then he went back up the ladder with the broken third rung, and over the parapet, and out across the ash toward the inner belt after the *29th*.

By the end of the first cycle, the *29th* Trench Regiment had retaken every metre of Leaf 50-27 it had lost since Gallows Rise, and the old second belt in front of it, and four hundred metres of the inner belt beyond, and it held all of it that night against two counter-attacks. The *7th* Line Phalanx's report for the cycle listed it as one of four regiments to have reached all of their objectives. Beneath that, in a separate line, somebody on the Phalanx staff had written: *Remains of thirty-one breachers of the 29th recovered from the wire before Ceiling-Lance Seven, Leaf 50-27, and carried back by the regiment.*

Kastellan read it on Quarterdeck Nineteen at the end of the cycle, and read it again, and then sat for a long time with the slate in his hand.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 240, NINTH HOUR**

The request from the *50th* Trench Regiment reached Quarterdeck Nineteen at the ninth hour of the first cycle, and Iason Dray brought it into the Marshal's office himself and put it on the desk without a word.

Kastellan read it standing up.

*50th Trench Regiment, Leaf 58-51, to Marshal Commanding. Regiment struck at the fourth hour by Grey Seventh on a front of two kilometres, delivered by the Arsenal's heavy mortars from the edge of the Grey. All transfers since the fourth hour null. Count to the eighth hour: four thousand one hundred. Regiment cannot advance and cannot hold in the vapour. Request permission to reply in kind from the stocks held at Ore Head. Thrale, Colonel.*

He read it twice. Then he put it down and looked at Dray.

"Ore Head," he said. "What's at Ore Head?"

Dray had the file. Dray always had the file.

"A sealed magazine behind the old Ore Head depot, Marshal. The Third took the Arsenal's outer stores in Rotation Three and found three thousand rounds of Grey Seventh in them, mortar and field-gun calibres, in the original Arsenal casings. The Lord-General had them sealed in place. The order is in the file. They've been under guard ever since." He paused. "The Lord-General's order says they were sealed pending a ruling from Olympia Sacra on their disposal. No ruling was ever made."

"What does Olympia Sacra say about Grey Seventh?"

"That it's proscribed, Marshal."

"Why?"

Dray hesitated. It was the first time Kastellan had seen him hesitate.

"The Arsenal's old inventory lists it as proscribed by order of the Third's own Flamen-Inquisitors in the Arsenal's first century, Marshal. It gives the order's number and its date. It doesn't give a reason." He looked at the slate. "I've asked the College for the order. They can't find it. I've asked the Répertoire what it knows about the distillate. It says it knows what it does to transfers, which is that it makes them null, and nothing about what it is or why. Nobody in the Third knows what it is, Marshal. I've asked."

Kastellan was quiet for a moment.

"Who's against?" he said.

"Most of the staff, Marshal. The Flamen-Inquisitor will protest it within the cycle. Enforcement will join him." Dray's voice was even. "I'm against."

"Why?"

"Because we don't know what it is," Dray said, "and we've just been told that the Arsenal's own priests banned it six hundred years ago and wouldn't write down why."

Kastellan looked at him for a long time. Then he looked down again at the request on the desk, at the number. *Four thousand one hundred.*

"Thrale's been losing every soldier he sends forward since the fourth hour," he said, "and he'll lose every one he sends forward tomorrow. They're going null on the edge of the Grey whether we fire it or not, Dray. The vapour's on the field already. The Archflamen put it there." He picked up a stylus. "I'll grant it. On my authority, for the *50th*'s front, on the edge of the Grey only. Note your objection and the staff's. I want it in the record."

"Yes, Marshal."

Kastellan signed the slate and handed it back.

"And find me someone who knows what that stuff is," he said. "There must be someone. Ask the Hephaesteans. Ask the Vestals. Ask the fucking Quiet Ones if you have to."

"Yes, Marshal."

Dray went out. Kastellan stood at the desk a while longer and looked at the map of the nulls on the wall, the clean ring round the Acropolis at the Mast's reach and the pale wash in the south, along the edge of the Lethe Cut, where the Grey was.

He never found anyone who knew. He asked for the rest of his command.

---

The *50th*'s guns opened fire on the edge of the Grey at the second hour of the second cycle, and by the end of the cycle the Archflamen's lines in front of the regiment were soaked in the same pale vapour that had been soaking the *50th*'s.

The *50th*'s soldiers watched it from their trenches. Colonel Silvan Thrale watched it from the regimental observation post on the parapet of Leaf 58-51, through a periscope, with his long face set, and his officers told it afterward to anyone who'd listen. The vapour came down on the Archflamen's forward trench in long slow grey drifts and lay in the bays, and for a while nothing happened. Then the thralls in the trench began to come up out of it.

They came up empty. That was the only word anyone in the *50th* ever found for it. They climbed up out of the trench onto the parapet in ones and twos and stood there in the open, in full view of the *50th*'s guns, and looked about them, slowly, with their faces quite blank, and then began to wander. Some of them walked out into no-man's land toward the *50th*'s wire and stood at it, looking at it. Some of them sat down in the ash. One of them walked straight along the parapet of the Archflamen's trench for half a kilometre, upright, in the open, with the *50th*'s machine guns firing all round him and missing, until he came to the end of the trench and stepped off it and walked away south into the Grey, and was gone.

The *50th*'s soldiers cheered. Thrale didn't.

He stood at the periscope and watched them wander, and when his adjutant asked him what he thought of it he didn't answer for a long time.

"I've seen that before," he said at last.

"Sir?"

"On our own side. In the *54th*'s sector, on the Lethe Cut, after the Grey Seventh came down." He took his eyes away from the periscope. "We sent patrols out. They found soldiers of the *54th* wandering in the ash, like those. Walking about, looking at things. They didn't know their names. Some of them came back with the patrols and some of them didn't."

His adjutant said nothing.

"Keep firing," Thrale said. "The Marshal's orders. Keep firing until we're told to stop."

They kept firing. And none of them suspected, then or for a long time afterward, that every round they put into the Grey was doing the Lethe's work, putting more of the river into the ground.

**THE NORTHERN FLATS. ROTATION TEN, CYCLES 240 TO 245**

Everywhere else on the arc, the Kastellan Offensive went as the Third's offensives had always gone.

In the north, the *2nd* and *5th* Line Phalanxes attacked the inner belt along the edge of the Mora Cut without the Behemoths, as the plan required, and for five cycles they went forward into the guns as the Third had gone forward into them since Rotation Zero. They gained between four hundred and nine hundred metres on most of their leaves. The Répertoire's figures for the five cycles came to six hundred and seventy thousand transfers. The *9th* Line Phalanx, opposite the Demus Rim, made diversionary attacks as its orders required, so that the Archflamen couldn't move his reserves from the Rim to the *7th*'s front, and lost eighty thousand more. The *8th* Line Phalanx, still being rebuilt behind the lines after the Lethe Cut, took no part.

Wherever the Cinderborn came into the fight, or the Forsworn, or the Unremembered, the Third did badly. The Phalanx reports from the northern Flats over those five cycles were full of the same lines. *Cohort stopped at second trench by Cinderborn counter-attack. Cohort withdrawn.* *Forsworn engaged on Leaf 41-19. Regiment fell back to start line.* *Unremembered reported in front of the 33rd. Contact lost with the 33rd's 4th Cohort.*

On the *7th* Line Phalanx's front, between Leaf 54-45 and Leaf 50-27, there were no such lines.

**LEAF 57-48, THE *52nd* TRENCH REGIMENT. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 241**

The second cycle began with the Wrath wings.

Optio Bram Keddy heard them come over at the first bell. He was in the *52nd*'s forward trench on Leaf 57-48 with the regiment's *1st* Cohort, and he'd been awake since the dark watch, and he heard them before anyone else in the bay did: a high thin scream far up in the ash, coming down from the north, and then the long rising roar of the bombers' engines, and he looked up and saw them come out of the ash cloud in two long waves, low, black, with the Fleet's grey and white on their wings, going over the *52nd*'s trench toward the inner belt so low that he could see the bomb-bays opening under them.

The bombs went into the inner belt on Leaf 57-48 two hundred metres in front of him, and the ground under his feet heaved, and the forward trench of the belt went up in a long rolling wall of fire.

"Holy fuck," said somebody beside him.

The second wave went over. The second wall of fire went up behind the first, on the belt's second trench. Keddy stood on the fire-step with his mouth open and watched it.

Colonel Lowe came along the bay behind him at a run, with her helmet on and her rifle in her hand, and stopped beside him on the fire-step and looked out over the parapet at the fire, and Keddy saw her face in the light of it, and she was smiling.

"Well," she said. "That's new."

"Colonel," Keddy said. "The Fleet's never done that for us."

"The Fleet's never been able to," Lowe said. "The drowned had the low geometry. Now the Wrath have it." She looked along the bay at the *1st* Cohort, at their faces, all of them turned up toward the bombers going away north into the ash. "Get them up, Keddy. Now. While the belt's still got its head down. We go in under the smoke."

They went in under the smoke. And the *52nd* Trench Regiment, which had held Leaves 56 to 58 for three rotations and gained eleven hundred metres in all of them together, gained more ground on Leaf 57-48 on the second cycle of the Kastellan Offensive than it had gained in a whole rotation. Keddy kept the count. He kept the count of everything, it was his job, and at the end of the cycle he went to Lowe in the new command dugout on the belt's second trench with the slate, and she read it and sat for a long time looking at the figures.

"Send it up," she said. "All of it. Send it to Quarterdeck Nineteen with my compliments, and tell the Marshal that if he can get me two more waves of those, I'll get him the next trench as well."

Kastellan read it on Quarterdeck Nineteen that afternoon. That evening, he asked the Fleet for more bombers.

**LEAF 54-45. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 241**

The drowned came on the second cycle, under a seep-storm.

Varne felt them before the augurs did, as he had felt the *Eye of Light* waiting among the wrecks. He was walking the *Anvil of Talos* forward through the ruins of the inner belt on Leaf 54-45 at the head of the Forge Cohort, under a brown hissing rain of seep that ran down the carapace and steamed off the wards, and he'd just finished a column of Arsenal tanks coming up the communication trench from the belt's third line, eleven of them, in a little under a minute. He had counted them going. And as the last of them burned, he felt the *Anvil*'s mind go still.

He knew that stillness now. He'd felt it twice. He lay in the cradle with the engine's vast slow mind gone rigid round his own, and he felt it turn, the whole engine's attention swinging round toward the north and the west, toward the Mora Cut and the Demus Rim, and through the cradle links, at the deepest floor of its mind, he felt it answer something, as it had answered the ichor.

"Augurs," he said into the relay. "Bearing three hundred and forty. Range eleven kilometres. What do you have?"

There was a pause on the relay. Then the augur-master of the *Anvil*, a dry precise man named Emrys Falk who had sat at his station on the command deck for sixty years, said, "Lord. Behemoths. Twelve of them. Coming from the Mora Cut and the Demus Rim together. They'll be on us in forty minutes."

Varne lay still.

"Drowned," he said.

"Drowned, Lord."

He had known. He had known since the ichor, in the deep cradles, when the *Anvil*'s mind had gone still and fixed and something down at the bottom of it had answered. He had known what the *Anvil* had been turning toward, all those cycles, since they came below Depth 30.

The Forge had been sending Behemoths into the Phlegethon for as long as there had been Behemoths. Into the river itself, down the long dark descents below the Arsenal, to fight the things that came up out of it. Most of them came back. Some didn't. And of those that didn't, some had come back walking for something else, out of the river, drowned, with their cradle-crews gone and their old minds filled with river-fire, and the Forge had been fighting them since the first engine went down. The lords of the Forge had a list of them. Every engine the Forge had lost in the river, back to the first. Varne had read it a hundred times, in the great cold hall above the deep cradles. He'd known their names since he was an apprentice.

The *Anvil* hadn't been turning toward the river. It had been turning toward its brothers.

"All engines," he said into the relay. His voice was steady. "Drowned Behemoths, twelve, bearing three hundred and forty, closing. All Brontes, form on the *Anvil*. Steropes, screen the left. Infantry and armour of the *7th* Line Phalanx, keep pushing. We'll handle this."

He felt Calder's voice come on the relay from the *Steadfast*, beside him.

"Lord," she said. "Is this what she's been turning toward?"

"Yes."

Calder was quiet a moment.

"Do you know which ones they are?"

"Not yet," Varne said. "I will."

---

He knew the first of them at four kilometres.

It came out of the seep-storm at the head of the drowned line, walking, and the augurs gave him its shape: a Brontes-pattern engine, eighty metres tall, of an old mark, with the long high carapace and the swept-back shoulder plates of the engines the Forge had built four hundred years ago, before Varne was born. Its plate was black and scaled and running with river-fire, and where the eagle and the hammer had been painted on its shoulder there was nothing now but a long glowing seam where the Phlegethon had eaten the bronze. It carried, on its right arm, where a Brontes of that mark had carried a turbine-cannon, something else. A great long-barrelled hammer-gun, black, of no pattern the Forge had ever built, with river-fire running down the barrel in long bright drips.

Varne knew the hammer-gun. Every cradle-master of the Forge knew it, from the list. The cradle-crews had named it four hundred years ago, from the sound it made. They called it the Smith's Laughter.

And he knew the engine that carried it.

"*Ironsong*," he said aloud, in the cradle.

Falk, at the augurs, turned his head.

"Lord?"

"The lead engine. The one with the Laughter. That's the *Ironsong*." Varne's voice was quiet. "She went into the river at the Ninth Descent four hundred and eleven years ago, with Cradle-Master Odalric Thane and a crew of nine. Her bell was rung at the Forge for a cycle and a night. I've read her name on the list every year of my life."

Nobody on the command deck said anything.

"All Brontes," Varne said into the relay. "The lead engine is the *Ironsong*. Fire on her first."

---

The war of the engines began at the fourth hour of the second cycle, on the ruins of the inner belt between Leaf 54-45 and Leaf 53-41, under the seep-storm, and it went on for the rest of the cycle while the infantry battle went on at the engines' feet.

Saldane watched it from a shell crater in the belt's second trench, where the *22nd* had dug in for the cycle. He watched it for the rest of his life, in a way. He told Penn, years afterward, that he dreamed about it.

They were evenly matched. That was the first thing he understood, watching. Ten Brontes and twelve Steropes of the Forge Cohort Talos against twelve drowned engines, of every mark and every age, the oldest of them so old that the Forge's own records no longer showed their pattern, and every one of them black with river-fire and scaled with the Phlegethon's slag, and every one of them as large as the *Anvil of Talos*. They came at each other across the ruins of the inner belt at a slow walk, firing, and the wards round them flared white and gold and a sickly river-red under the impacts, and the shells that missed went on past and came down on the belt and on the Third's trenches and on the Archflamen's, and the whole of Leaf 54-45 shook under them.

He saw a Steropes of the Forge Cohort go in fast on the drowned left, at a long loping run, firing, and two of the drowned engines turn on it together, and its wards go down in a long white flicker, and the Steropes go on running another forty metres with fire pouring out of its carapace before its legs went and it fell. It fell slowly. It went down on its knees in the ash and then forward onto its face, and the ground jumped under him, two kilometres away.

He saw a drowned engine take a full salvo from three Brontes at once and stagger and stand with fire running out of its carapace in long glowing streams, and then turn, and keep firing, and walk on. He saw it take another salvo and fall.

He saw the *Ironsong*.

---

Varne fought the *Ironsong* for four hours.

She was old. She was older than any engine in the Forge Cohort, and she'd been drowned for four hundred years, and the river-fire in her had made her strong in ways no Brontes of her mark had ever been. Her wards were river-wards, red and thick and slow, that took the *Anvil*'s turbine-cannon fire and drank it, and burned, and held. And the Smith's Laughter, when it fired, didn't fire shells. It fired something heavier and slower, a great glowing slug of molten river-iron the size of a Thureos that came across the ruins of the belt with a long rising shriek, a sound Varne realised at the first shot was the reason for the name, a high crazed peal of laughter in the air, and struck the wards with a shock that went through every engine in the Forge Cohort at once.

The *Anvil*'s outermost ward went down at the first shot. The second held, barely, and burned. Varne felt the *Anvil*'s reactors surge to feed it, and felt the heat come up in the engine's heart, and the cradle-crew round him shouting readings, and Falk at the augurs calling the range, steady and dry. He walked the *Anvil* sideways through the ruins and fired, and fired again, and the *Ironsong*'s wards burned red under the *Anvil*'s shells and held, and the Laughter came round on him again.

He was aware of the *Steadfast* beside him the whole time. He was aware of all of them through the relay, every engine of the Forge Cohort, walking and firing and burning in the seep-storm round him, but the *Steadfast* was nearest, on his right, two hundred metres off, and Calder was firing on the *Ironsong* with him, salvo for salvo. He felt her in the relay, steady and bright and hard. He felt the *Steadfast* under her, no longer restless. The *Steadfast* had found what it had been turning toward, and it was fighting.

At the seventh hour the *Ironsong* turned the Laughter on the *Steadfast*.

Varne felt it happen. He felt the *Ironsong*'s attention swing off the *Anvil* and onto the engine beside it, and he had time to say, "Calder," into the relay, and nothing else.

The first shot took the *Steadfast*'s outermost ward. The second took the second. The third, coming one after another so fast that Varne would never afterward understand how the drowned engine had fed the Laughter, took the last ward, and the *Steadfast* stood unwarded in the seep-storm for a moment with its turbine-cannon still firing, and Calder's voice came on the relay, quite calm.

"Lord. Wards down. Raising the..."

And a Phlegethon-cannon on one of the drowned engines behind the *Ironsong*, which Varne had not seen and Falk had not called, put a long jet of river-fire through the *Steadfast*'s command deck.

He felt them die. He felt it through the relay, every one of them, Calder and her crew of nine, at their stations on the command deck seventy metres above the ash, in the cradle and at the augurs and the reactor boards. He felt them burn. It took less than three seconds. He felt Calder's mind go out of the relay, bright and steady and hard to the last, and then the *Steadfast*'s higher mind, vast and old and slow, go out after her, burned out of the engine all at once.

The *Steadfast* stopped.

It stopped where it stood, on the ruins of the inner belt on Leaf 54-45, with its turbine-cannon raised toward the *Ironsong* and silent, and its honour banners hanging from the carapace in the seep, and the fire from the command deck running down its flank in long slow streams, its gyroscopes holding it upright where it stood. Its legs were locked in the stride it had been taking when its crew died, and it stood there in the ash, eighty metres tall, dark and silent, with the battle going on round its feet.

It stood there for the rest of the war. The Third's soldiers came to know it as a landmark. It was the tallest thing on the Cinder Flats outside the Acropolis, and they used it to give directions by, *two hundred metres east of the Steadfast*, *the Steadfast's shadow*, until long after the siege was over and the Forge came at last to take it home.

Varne lay in the *Anvil*'s cradle and felt the *Steadfast* go silent beside him in the relay, and for a moment, the length of a breath, the *Anvil of Talos* stood still on the ruins of the belt and didn't fire.

Then the *Anvil*'s mind came round his own, vast and slow and hot, hotter than he had ever felt it, and he felt the engine turn of its own accord toward the *Ironsong*, and he didn't try to turn it back.

"All Brontes," he said into the relay. His voice was perfectly steady. "Fire on the *Ironsong*."

It took them the rest of the cycle. They never brought her down. At the end of the second cycle, with three of her drowned brothers fallen in the ash round her and the Smith's Laughter glowing white along its whole length, the *Ironsong* turned and walked away north into the seep-storm with the rest of the drowned line, slowly, scarred, and the Forge Cohort, with nothing left to stop her, let her go.

At the Forge of Hephaestus, eleven cycles' travel above the Flats, they rang a bell for the *Steadfast*.

It was rung in the great cold hall above the deep cradles, at the first bell of the next cycle, by the eldest of the lords of the Forge, on the bronze bell that had been rung for every engine the Forge had lost since the first. He rang it for a cycle and a night, as the custom was. And he rang it again before the siege was over, for each of the *Steadfast*'s brothers that fell at the Arsenal, and the Forge's lords said afterward that the bell hadn't been rung so often in a single war since the Ninth Descent.

---

**LEAF 54-45. ROTATION TEN, CYCLES 242 TO 243**

Without the engines, the infantry advance slowed.

The Forge Cohort spent the third cycle and the fourth fighting the drowned line across the ruins of the inner belt, a slow brutal grinding duel of engines that went back and forth across four leaves and left the ground between Leaf 54-45 and Leaf 51-30 a wilderness of craters, shattered basalt and burning wreckage, and the regiments of the *7th* Line Phalanx fought the Archflamen's cohorts at the engines' feet for every trench. Little ground was taken on either cycle. The Répertoire's figures rose.

Saldane's *22nd* took the belt's third trench on Leaf 54-45 on the third cycle, and lost it, and took it again on the fourth. On the fourth cycle a Cinderborn champion with a blade as long as a man came over the parapet of the third trench into the *1st* Cohort's bays at the head of forty Cinderborn, and the *1st* Cohort held the trench against them with grenades and bayonets and flame lances for an hour, and Saldane was in the bays with them for the whole of it, with his pistol, and Tey was beside him with the standard. A Cinderborn's blade took the standard's staff a hand's breadth above Tey's grip and cut it clean through.

She caught the head of it as it fell. She stood in the bay with the broken staff in one hand and the standard bunched in the other and her pistol in her teeth, and then she took the pistol out of her teeth and shot the Cinderborn in the face, and it fell back off the parapet, burning.

"Sir," she said to Saldane, afterward, holding the two halves of the staff. "I'm sorry, sir."

"For what?" Saldane said. "It's a stick, Signifer. Get a new stick. The Quiet Ones have thousands of them."

She had the standard on a new staff by the end of the watch. It was a length of Thureos aerial-mast, steel, half a metre longer than the old one, and she said afterward that it was heavier but that it didn't break.

---

**LEAF 54-45. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 244**

By the fifth cycle both sides had lost heavily, and of all the regiments in the Kastellan Offensive, the *22nd* had gone furthest.

It had driven a salient into the inner belt on Leaf 54-45, a kilometre and a half deep and nine hundred metres wide at its base, a long narrow tongue of shattered trenches pushed into the heart of the belt with the Archflamen's lines on three sides of it, and it had held it for two cycles against everything the Archflamen sent at it. The Phalanx-General wanted to pull it back to straighten the line. Saldane had refused, politely, three times. And on the night of the fourth cycle, Varne came down the line from the cradle-yard to see him.

He came on foot, out of his cradle, in the dark, with an escort of four Hephaestean servicing hands and an old bronze lantern, and he walked the whole length of the salient to the *22nd*'s command dugout in the belt's third trench. Saldane came out of the dugout to meet him and stood in the trench in the red light of the shells and looked at him, the tall stooped old man with the long white beard and the bronze sockets at his temples, and didn't know what to say.

"Colonel," Varne said. "You're the man with the standard."

"I am, Lord."

"I saw it on the first cycle. Through the *Anvil*'s augurs. I saw it go up on your parapet before the engines came over your trench." Varne looked about him at the salient. "I've been looking at your ground. You've gone further than anyone. If the belt's going to break anywhere, it'll break here."

"That's what I keep telling the Phalanx-General, Lord."

"I'm going to throw the engines at it," Varne said. "Everything I have left. At first light. Either we break the belt here, or we draw the drowned engines onto ours and let somebody else break it somewhere else. I'm told the *37th*'s close on Leaf 53-41." He paused. "I wanted you to know. You'll be walking behind us again. It'll be bad."

Saldane looked at him for a while.

"Lord," he said. "I'm sorry about the *Steadfast*."

Varne was quiet.

"Thank you, Colonel," he said at last. "Her cradle-master was named Hesione Calder. She was sixty-four. She was the only one of them who ever argued with me." He looked out over the parapet at the dark, toward the place two kilometres away where the *Steadfast* stood silent in the ruins with the shells bursting round its feet. "I'd like someone in the Third to know her name."

"I'll know it, Lord," Saldane said.

---

They went at first light on the fifth cycle.

The Hephaestean crews had worked through the night in the cradle-yard, rearming and patching the engines, and Varne walked the Forge Cohort out of the yard at the first bell with what was left of it: eight Brontes and seven Steropes, scarred and blackened, their honour banners burned to rags, their plate patched with fresh bronze over the old in long bright seams. They came down through the old outer belt and across the Third's lines and into the *22nd*'s salient, and the *22nd* came up out of its trenches and walked behind them, with Tey and the standard on its new steel staff at the head of the regiment, and Saldane on her left with his hands in his pockets.

The Archflamen's barrages came down on them at once. Varne walked the engines through them, the wards flaring under the shells, and the Thureos and the escorting cohorts came on behind. And as the Forge Cohort came up out of the salient onto the open ground at the heart of the inner belt, he felt, through the *Anvil*'s augurs and through the relay, how thin the Archflamen's line was.

After five cycles of fighting there was almost nothing left of it. The Archflamen's cohorts on Leaf 54-45 had been thrown at the *22nd*'s salient for two cycles and broken on it. The drowned engines had held the line together alone, and the drowned engines had lost engines too, five of them now, fallen in the ash between Leaf 54-45 and Leaf 51-30. The rest of them were standing a kilometre and a half away at the northern edge of the belt, the *Ironsong* among them with the Smith's Laughter dark along its barrel, and as the Forge Cohort came on out of the salient toward them, Varne felt them turn.

He felt them weigh it. Seven drowned engines, scarred and outnumbered, against fifteen. And he felt them fall back.

They went slowly, walking backward into the smoke with their guns still trained on the Forge Cohort, toward the Cinder Wall, and the *Ironsong* went last, and as she went Varne felt her attention touch the *Anvil*'s once, briefly, through the river-fire, an old cold vast regard, and turn away.

The Archflamen's cohorts couldn't stand against the Forge Cohort alone.

They tried. Varne gave them that. A divine mortar battery dug in at the foot of the Cinder Wall brought one of the Steropes down with concentrated fire, a dozen keyed rounds on its wards at once, until the wards burned through and the Steropes went down in a long white flash with its crew still firing. It was the Archflamen's one success of the cycle. Some of the thralls in the belt fought on in their trenches with a dreadful blank determination, firing at the engines with rifles until the engines walked over them. But most of them broke.

The Third's soldiers had a name for it afterward. They called it Behemoth-shock.

Saldane saw it from the middle of the open ground, walking behind the *Anvil* with Tey at his side. He saw the Archflamen's thralls in the trenches ahead look up and see the engines coming, eight Brontes and six Steropes in a long line across the heart of the inner belt with their wards blazing and their turbine-cannon firing and the ground shaking under them in great slow waves, and he saw them break. They came up out of their trenches in their hundreds and ran. They threw down their rifles and their grenades and their packs and ran back across the belt toward the Cinder Wall, stumbling and falling in the craters, and the Thureos' hull-guns caught them in the open, and the *22nd*'s riflemen, and still they ran. A cohort of Sworn in their black tried to stop them at the belt's fourth trench, and Saldane saw the Sworn shooting thralls in the back as they fled, and the thralls kept running over them.

"Fucking hell," said Penn, quietly, beside him.

"Keep walking," Saldane said.

The *22nd* walked on, behind the engines. The tanks and outriders of the *4th* Assault Phalanx came up into the salient behind them and through it and joined the pursuit, Colossi and Thureos and the light fast Dromos tanks of the outrider squadrons, grinding up out of the salient in long columns across the heart of the belt, firing as they came. And at the end of the sixth cycle of the Kastellan Offensive, on Leaf 54-45, the inner belt broke.

Saldane was there. He stood on the parapet of the belt's last trench, the sixth, the one the Third's engineers had dug six hundred years ago to protect the spur, with the Cinder Wall rising dark in front of him three kilometres away across the ash, and he watched the fresh tank columns of the *4th* Assault Phalanx come pouring through the gap past him into the open ground beyond. Tey stood beside him with the standard. The *22nd* came up the trench round them and stood on the parapet too, along the whole of the breach, and watched.

Nobody cheered. They were too tired.

"Penn," Saldane said, at last.

"Sir."

"Make a note. In the regimental diary. Today's date. *The 22nd Trench Regiment reached the last trench of the inner belt on Leaf 54-45 behind the Forge Cohort Talos.*" He thought about it. "And put down that the *Steadfast*'s cradle-master was Hesione Calder. Sixty-four. Put it in the regimental diary of the *22nd*. Somebody asked me to."

Penn wrote it down.

**BEFORE THE CINDER WALL. ROTATION TEN, CYCLES 246 TO 260**

This time the Archflamen's army didn't abandon its works when the belt broke.

It had done so at the outer belt and at the second, both times. Both times it had fallen back to the next belt and dug in and waited for the Third to come at it again. But there was no next belt now. Over the rotations of the siege, the Archflamen's engineers had covered the ground between the inner belt and the Cinder Wall with trenches and pillboxes and gun pits, without the deep shelters or the careful siting of the inner belt, but more than enough to contest every metre of it. And the Archflamen's commanders knew that close to the spur they couldn't afford to fall back to the Wall and dig in again. There would be nowhere left to go.

So there was no clean breakthrough, and no rapid advance. The tank columns of the *4th* Assault Phalanx came pouring through the gap on Leaf 54-45 and ran straight into the pillboxes and the gun pits on the open ground beyond, and lost a third of their strength in the first cycle, and went to ground. After that the battle became a slow crawl forward against counter-attacks every cycle, the Third's cohorts taking one line of pits at a time, the Archflamen's cohorts coming back at them in the night.

On Leaf 54-45 the Archflamen's strength was spent, and the *22nd* made large gains there over the next ten cycles before it too had to halt, to avoid outrunning its supplies and its guns. When it stopped it was eleven hundred metres from the foot of the Cinder Wall. Saldane went up to the forward pits on the night it halted and looked at the Wall through a periscope for a long time, the great dark rampart of it rising out of the ash, with the Acropolis behind it and the Mast faint above, and came back down and said nothing to anyone about what he'd seen.

The Forge Cohort walked behind the crawl for those ten cycles, breaking pillboxes for the infantry, and it paid for every one of them. Two more Brontes went down to the gun pits under the Wall and a Steropes with them, and in return the engines caught a drowned Brontes of an old mark that had been left behind to cover the retreat, alone among the pits, and killed it. The Behemoths were withdrawn into reserve at the cradle-yard for repair on the two hundred and fifty-sixth cycle.

They'd made the difference. Everyone on the Flats knew it. They had cost the Forge four Brontes and seven Steropes, which would have to be replaced from the Forge, and the Third's staff credited them with six drowned Behemoths destroyed. The *Ironsong* wasn't among the six. Varne walked the *Anvil of Talos* back into its gantry in the cradle-yard on the two hundred and fifty-sixth cycle with its plate scorched black from the carapace to the knees and its turbine-cannon worn out, and lay in the cradle for a long time afterward without unjoining, and the cradle-crew left him alone.

He was thinking about the list. The Forge's list of every engine lost in the river, back to the first. He'd read it a hundred times. He was thinking about how many names were on it.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 262**

The battle for the ground before the Cinder Wall was far from over. Kastellan was already planning the next phase. But a long step had been taken, and on Quarterdeck Nineteen the staff knew it.

The Archflamen had lost heavily. His cohorts on the inner belt had been broken. His drowned engines had been mauled, six of them fallen, and the rest had gone back behind the Cinder Wall. And while the Third's replacements kept coming down the cables at Kappa by the thousand, the Archflamen's couldn't. Kastellan sat at the desk that had been Castorre's with the returns of the offensive in front of him and Dray standing at his shoulder, and read them through, and understood something that he'd known for forty years and never yet been able to use on this front: that a siege of attrition always favoured a besieger who was properly supplied.

For the first time in four rotations, the Third could say it was winning the reduction of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal.

The Répertoire's figures for the offensive bore it out. Transfers had been lower than in any offensive of the siege. So had nulls, on almost every front, at two and three percent where they had run at six and seven through Antilochus.

Almost every front.

"The *50th*," Kastellan said.

"Forty percent, Marshal," Dray said. "Nulls on the *50th*'s front ran at forty percent of transfers for the whole of the offensive. Before and after the regiment began firing."

Kastellan looked at the figure for a long time.

"Show me the map," he said.

Dray brought it. It was the Répertoire's map of the nulls on the Flats, the same map that hung on the office wall, brought up to the end of the offensive, and Kastellan spread it on the desk and leaned over it with his hands flat on either side.

The ring round the Acropolis was still there, clean and sharp, at the Mast's reach. And in the south, along the edge of the Lethe Cut, where the pale wash of the Grey had lain since the *54th* went into it, the wash had spread. It had spread north. It ran up from the Lethe Cut now along the *50th*'s whole front and past it, a long pale tongue over Leaf 58 and Leaf 59 and into the edge of the *52nd*'s sector, eleven kilometres further north than it had been on the two hundred and fortieth cycle.

"Since the *50th* began firing," Dray said.

Kastellan said nothing.

"Every round they've put into the Grey, Marshal," Dray said, carefully, "the Grey's got bigger. The Répertoire doesn't know why. Nobody does. But the ground the *50th*'s shells have fallen on is reading the same as the ground on the Lethe Cut, and the transfers from it are going null at the same rate, and the patrols say..." He stopped.

"Say it."

"The patrols say soldiers of the *50th* have started wandering, Marshal. A few. In the last five cycles. Walking out of their trenches into the Grey and not coming back."

Kastellan straightened up slowly from the map.

"Stop the firing," he said. "Now. Today. Seal what's left at Ore Head and put a double guard on it. Nobody fires another round of that stuff on my front for any reason. Put that in writing over my name."

"Yes, Marshal."

"And Dray." Kastellan looked at him. "The person who knows what it is. Did you find them?"

"No, Marshal. The Hephaesteans don't know. The Vestals wouldn't say. The Quiet Ones," Dray hesitated, "didn't answer."

"Keep looking."

---

The College's Flamen-Inquisitor had already protested the decision to Olympia Sacra, within the cycle, as the use of a proscribed weapon by an army of the Imperator. Enforcement's Lictor-Superior had joined the protest. Neither of them had proposed any other remedy for the *50th*.

Kastellan answered them both in a single slate, which Dray sent up the cables to the Lord Marshal on the two hundred and sixty-third cycle, and which was afterward much quoted.

*The 50th Trench Regiment was losing every soldier it sent forward without return,* he wrote. *The vapour was on the field already. The Archflamen put it there. I granted the regiment the means to reply on my own authority and against the advice of my staff, whose objection is recorded. I have now withdrawn it on the evidence of the Répertoire, whose figures are attached. If Olympia Sacra knows what Grey Seventh is and why it was proscribed, I would be glad to be told, since nobody on the Cinder Flats does. Kastellan.*

The Lord Marshal accepted the defence. On the evidence Kastellan had, it was reasonable. Olympia Sacra never answered the question.

And the ground the *50th*'s shells had soured was the Lethe's for the rest of the siege.

**KAPPA. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 270**

They burned the thirty-one on the parade ground at Kappa on the two hundred and seventieth cycle, with the honours of the regiment, as Valcourt had said they would.

The *29th* was in the line. It couldn't come. But it sent a guard of honour from the *2nd* Cohort, forty soldiers, and Cade was one of them, and Holt, and Tansy, who had asked to come and whom nobody had had the heart to refuse. They stood in two ranks on the ash in their cleaned field uniforms with their rifles, on the same ground where the colours had burned, and the thirty-one lay on a long pyre in front of them under the regiment's new colours, crimson and gold, and Medicus Valcourt stood at the head of the pyre with the regiment's chaplain and read the names.

She read them in the order they had hung on the wire, from left to right. *Iska Tamm* was fourteenth. Cade stood at attention and listened to her read it.

Colonel Dunmore came down from the line for it, on her bad leg, and stood with them. And at the end, when the names were read and the chaplain had said what chaplains say, and the pyre was lit, she stepped forward to the front of the guard with a slate in her hand and read out a line from it in her gravel-chute voice.

"By order of the Marshal Commanding," she read, "the battle honour *Leaf 50-27* is awarded to the *29th* Trench Regiment, for the recovery of the ground and of the thirty-one." She lowered the slate. "It'll go on the hoist. First on the new colours. The Quiet Ones are sewing it now."

The guard stood silent while the pyre burned in front of them on the ash where the old colours had burned, and Cade stood in the front rank of the guard of honour of the *2nd* Cohort with his rifle at the shoulder and watched it, and beside him Tansy was crying quietly, and on his other side Holt stood perfectly still with her eyes on the fire.

When it was over and the guard was dismissed, Holt turned to go, and then stopped, and looked at Cade.

"Trooper," she said.

"Optio."

She held his eyes for a moment.

"Good work on the wire," she said, and walked away across the parade ground toward the cable head, small and fierce and grey, with her rifle slung, and Cade watched her go, as he had once before, and this time he found he didn't mind at all.
