## Chapter 3: Eleven Weeks

*"Quod cadit, surgit."*

*Motto of the 3rd Siege Army, cut over the door of every depot*

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 318**

The meeting to decide whose fault it was took nine hours, and Leda Corvis spent most of them counting the chairs.

There were fourteen. The conference hall of Quarterdeck Nineteen had been built for the staff of the *3rd* Siege Army in the days when the Third had a staff worth seating, a long iron room behind the observation gallery with a table of black basalt down the middle and a crystal floor at one end through which, if anybody had cared to look, the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal could be seen burning on its spur nine hundred metres below. Nobody looked. Eleven of the chairs were occupied by people in the flesh. The other three held projections from the noosphere, slightly translucent, flickering whenever the relay hiccupped, and every time one of them flickered Corvis watched the people in the flesh lean away from it, as if a hiccup in the noosphere might be catching.

At the head of the table was a projection of Legate-Superior Tiberius Hask of the Pyrrhus Command, who had been awake for thirty hours and looked it. On his right was a projection of the Flamen-Prior of the College of Pontiffs for the Tartarean theatre, a soft heavy man in crimson named Sulpicius Mago, who had not stopped talking since the second hour. On Hask's left was the third projection, which was nobody. It was a chair with an amber line of text hanging above it in the air.

*Répertoire du Savoir Athénien. Present.*

The Répertoire attended conferences like this one much as weather attended a picnic. It didn't speak unless spoken to, and when it did speak, the conversation was generally over.

Corvis sat at the foot of the table beside her Lord-General. She was a tall, spare woman of sixty with grey hair cut short, an Athenian of the Academy on Callistus, which meant she had spent eleven years being taught the Tactica Olympica by people who believed that every war could be won on paper if the paper was big enough. She had served as chief of staff of the Third for nineteen years. In that time the Third had fought eleven reformation peaks out of the Phlegethon and won all of them, and she had learned to sit through a meeting of this kind without letting anything she thought reach her face. Beside her, Lord-General Aurelian Castorre sat with his hands folded on the table and listened to the Flamen-Prior with every appearance of interest. He had been doing that for five hours. Corvis had served him for nineteen years and still couldn't tell whether he was actually listening.

"...an Archflamen," Mago was saying, "of the Lord Imperator, consecrated before the full College, in the middle of a vat-rite. In the middle of a rite! I have the Flamens of eleven holds asking me whether it's true, and I don't know what to tell them, because nobody told me. The College was not consulted. The College was not informed. The College found out from a Lethe Hold commander's sector log, and only because she was careful enough to send us a copy."

"The College was informed of the Archflamen's conduct," Varros said, from halfway down the table. He was the only person at the table older than the Flamen-Prior and the only one who didn't seem to mind it. "By the Flamens of the holds on his route. For three rotations. I have the reports here, if the Flamen-Prior would like to read them."

"Those were reports of a revival."

"Yes. They were. They were also reports of desertions from every hold on his route, rising every rotation, and of soldiers who stopped answering the rite. The Répertoire sent the College a summary of them in Rotation Minus Three. Ref. COLL-T-1144." Varros recited it without looking at his pane. "The College asked for more preachers."

Mago turned a darker shade of crimson.

"The method," he said. "Leave aside whether we were informed. The method. A single round, during the rite, before ten thousand witnesses, and a false flag. A false flag! Do you know what the Haruspex is saying down there? Do you know what the Archflamen's people are writing on the walls of the casting lines? They're saying the ceiling sent a killer to the altar dressed as the deep. They're saying the ceiling would rather see him dead than hear him. And they're right! They're right, that's exactly what happened, and it failed, and now he's a martyr who didn't have the decency to die."

"The method was chosen," the amber line said, without warning, "to spare the College the trial of one of its own."

The hall went quiet.

Mago looked at the chair with the line of text over it for a long time.

"I see," he said finally. "Thank you."

Castorre stirred beside her. Corvis felt it more than saw it, a slight shift of weight, the first sign in five hours that he was in the room. He'd been watching the Enforcement director, a lean, furious woman named Pallas Quiro who had been sitting at the far end of the table with her arms folded since the first bell. Enforcement had lost the Tabularium. It had lost its Lictor-Superior to a null, the first null of the Arsenal, and a marksman it couldn't find, and the director had made it plain in the first hour that she blamed the College for the Archflamen and the Répertoire for the shot and everybody else at the table for existing.

"I have a proposal," Quiro said now. "Lictor action. A strike team, thirty Lictors, down the old Hephaestean service ways under the cone. They take the Acropolis by night and bring the Archflamen out in a sack."

"The Tabularium's fall," the amber line said, "has shown what a Lictor action at the Arsenal can achieve."

Quiro's mouth went thin. She didn't say anything else for the rest of the meeting.

---

Mago's proposal took up the sixth and seventh hours. He wanted a delegation. He wanted twelve Flamens of rank to go down the Pilgrim Stair under the College's banner and a flag of truce, and to offer the Archflamen a hearing of his doctrine before the full College in exchange for the return of the casting lines, and he wanted it before any soldier of the Empire set foot on the Flats.

"He's a priest," Mago said. "Whatever else he is. He'll want to be heard. Give him that, and he'll give us the Arsenal."

Corvis glanced at her Lord-General. Castorre was looking at his hands.

"Répertoire," Hask said, from the head of the table. He sounded very tired. "Price it."

*Delegation. Probability of return of the casting lines: under 0.01. Probability of the delegation being retained as hostages: over 0.6. Probability of the delegation's conversion to the Archflamen's doctrine: 0.14.*

"Fourteen percent," Mago said. "Fourteen percent of my Flamens would go over to him. That's what it thinks of us."

"It thinks the same of everyone," Varros said, not unkindly. "It priced my staff at nine."

Hask refused the delegation. He did it gently, with thanks to the College for its courage, and Mago accepted it with the air of a man who had expected nothing else and wanted it on the record that he had tried. Then, at the start of the eighth hour, the Legate-Superior turned his projection toward the foot of the table, and Corvis understood why her Lord-General had spent five hours looking at his hands.

"Lord-General Castorre," Hask said. "The Third."

"The Third, Legate-Superior."

"You'll have read the Répertoire's assessment."

"I have." Castorre unfolded his hands. "It's a sound assessment. Every other front in the theatre is short of shells already and will be short of soldiers within three rotations. The Third is the only army in Tartarus that can be committed without moving another. We're on the Flats already. We know the ground. We built the Arsenal's inner belt six hundred years ago and still hold the plans of it in our archive. And our line north of the bend can be thinned to a screen for the length of a siege without exposing anything the Command considers vital." He paused. "The Command considers very little north of the bend vital. It's been telling us so for six centuries."

There was a ripple of something near laughter down the table. Corvis didn't laugh.

"Then you accept it."

"It wasn't phrased as a request, Legate-Superior."

"No," Hask said. "It wasn't."

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN, THE LORD-GENERAL'S QUARTERS. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 318, NINTH WATCH**

The Répertoire's plan arrived at the end of the ninth watch. It came down the noosphere into the pane on the Lord-General's desk as an index of eleven thousand documents, and Corvis, who had expected it, had already sent the staff to bed, and poured two glasses of wine, and sat down in the chair across the desk from Castorre to wait for it.

He opened it. She watched his face while he read.

Aurelian Castorre was sixty-eight. He came of an old family of the Pyrrhus Command, one of those houses that had sent sons and daughters to the Academy on Callistus for three hundred years and expected them to come back generals, and he had come back a general, and spent forty years in Tartarus being careful. He was a small, neat, grey man with a soft voice and an excellent memory, who knew the names of every Phalanx-General and most of the regimental colonels of the Third and could recite the casualty figures of every battle the army had fought since he joined it. He didn't waste soldiers he couldn't replace. Since the Third could replace almost all of them, the soldiers he had saved were mainly gunners, keyers and engineers, and the Third's gunners would have walked into the Phlegethon for him.

He read the first document of the plan, which was the summary, and then he sat back.

"Four rotations," he said.

"Four rotations of front time." Corvis slid a pane across the desk. "Recovery of the casting lines intact if possible. No ceiling on transfers."

"No ceiling on transfers." He said it softly. "It's never done that before."

"It's set one on nulls instead. Three percent."

He looked at that number for a while. Three percent of the transfers would come back to the vats empty, a blank body grown around nothing, and the Répertoire had decided that it would tolerate that and no more. Corvis knew he was doing the arithmetic. Corvis had done it herself an hour earlier, in her own quarters, with the door shut. If the Third took the casualties a siege of three belts would cost against six million defenders, three percent of them would be a figure that no Lord-General of the Third had ever signed for.

"What's our null rate now?" he asked. "On the line north of the bend."

"Point four of a percent. Rising to point nine in the Lethe sectors, in the fog."

"And at the Arsenal?"

"One. Lictor-Superior Rusk." She hesitated. "Of sixty transfers. The Répertoire attributes it to relay interference from the Phlegethon."

"Does it." He didn't make it a question. He drank a little of his wine, and set the glass down exactly where it had been, on the ring it had left. "What does Varros think?"

"Varros has opened a paper file."

That got her a look.

"Has he," Castorre said. "Well. Varros is eight hundred years old and has been wrong about something perhaps four times in my hearing. I'd like to see the file, when he's ready to show it." He turned back to the plan, and turned its pages one by one, and for a long while didn't say anything else.

Corvis knew the plan already. She had read it in her quarters, all eleven thousand documents of it, which had taken her two hours at the speed of the Academy, and she had found it, as she found most of the Répertoire's plans, admirable. It was plain and methodical. It gave great attention to the supply of the army and little to its price. It assumed that the Third would advance by sapping and shelling, belt by belt, as it always had, a few hundred metres at a time under a weight of ordnance no defender could stand, until the three belts were broken and the Wall was breached and the Acropolis stood alone on its hill and could be starved or stormed at leisure. It priced every metre of the Flats in transfers. It priced every belt, and every assault, and the descent of the Cinder Stair, and the destruction of the sixty ceiling-lances, and the storming of the Wall, and at the bottom of the last page it gave a total, which was a large number, and a duration, which was four rotations, and a probability of success, which was ninety-four percent.

It was exactly the plan she and Castorre would have written themselves, if they'd had eleven years and the Répertoire's bandwidth. It suited them both perfectly. That was what worried her.

"There's no Fleet support," Castorre said, toward the end.

"The ceiling-lances. Sixty of them. Phlegethon-fed, eight thousand years old, sited to clear the geometry above the Flats of anything that flies." She called up the envelope. It was a red dome that covered the whole spur, the inner and second belts and most of the outer one, and it ended, as if somebody had drawn it with a compass, about four kilometres short of the line of the Third. "The Navarch can lend us a squadron off the *Pallas Ascendant* to escort the convoys down the cables. She'll bring heavy guns down into any corridor we open. Until we open one, nothing that flies can live over the Flats."

"So we take the lances first."

"The plan has us taking the lances in the first rotation, with the *10th*'s siege guns. Counter-battery from the outer belt." She paused. "It prices it at two hundred thousand transfers."

"And what do you price it at?"

She had known he would ask. She'd been an Athenian for forty years, and she had learned before she was twenty that when the Répertoire put a number on a thing, it was nearly always right, and that when it was wrong, it was wrong in only one direction.

"More," she said. "I don't know how much more. Nobody's tried to kill a ceiling-lance in eight thousand years. We don't know what they're made of."

"Then let's find out," Castorre said, and closed the plan, and sat for a while looking at the black window behind her, where the Arsenal burned below them in the dark. "The shells first. How long do we have?"

"For the Lethe holds? On the ration?" She'd done this one too. "Eleven weeks. After that the divine batteries in the Lethe sectors are firing their last keyed rounds, and the fog comes up the river and there's nothing to answer it but rifles and plasma packages. The holds will fall back again. And again. The Répertoire gives the Lethe line a ninety percent chance of holding through the twelfth week and a sixty percent chance through the twentieth."

"And after the twentieth?"

"It doesn't give a figure."

Castorre nodded slowly, as if that was the answer he'd expected.

"Then we have eleven weeks to get the Third down the Stair," he said, "and to start taking the Arsenal back from a man who has more shells than we do."

**THE ARSENAL. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 314 TO ROTATION ZERO, CYCLE 50**

Prefect Galba Ostorian had been passed over for promotion three times, and on the night after the storming of the Tabularium, while the bodies of the Lictors were still being thrown off the bridge into the gorge, he discovered why.

He was standing in the inner ward with the eastern terrace burning behind him and the Haruspex Dace in front of him, and Dace was telling him, in his pleasant, reasonable clerk's voice, that the Garrison Cohorts were to be divided.

"Divided how?" Ostorian asked.

"Into those who'll follow you," Dace said, "and those who won't."

Ostorian was fifty-four, which was young for a garrison prefect of the Hephaestean orders and old for anything else. He was a big square man with a broken nose and the forge-hammer of the orders tattooed on the back of each hand, and he had commanded the Pyriphlegethon Garrison Cohorts for eleven years, and served in them for thirty before that, and he knew every centurion of the sixty-eight cohorts by name. He'd been passed over three times for promotion to the holds. The first time, he had been told that a garrison officer lacked line experience. The second time, he'd been told the same thing in different words. The third time, nobody had told him anything, and he had found out from a ration-board notice that a man eleven years his junior, from a Pyrrhus Command family, had been given the post. He'd taken it well. He took most things well. In the eleven months since the Archflamen came down the Stair, a seat at the Archflamen's table had come to mean more to him than he liked to admit. It was the first time in thirty years that anyone above him had asked his opinion and then listened to the answer.

"Most of them will follow me," he said.

"I'm sure. Most is a very good number. But the Haruspex of the Imperator's Hold isn't concerned with most." Dace smiled at him. "We'll need to know which ones by morning."

Ostorian found out by morning. It wasn't hard. The garrison had watched the shot on the altar and the Enforcement flash on the assassin's sleeve and the fall of the Tabularium, and by the end of the night the cohorts had sorted themselves out into those who believed what the Haruspex had told them and those who didn't. Forty-one cohorts came over to the Imperator's Hold without a question. Nine came over with questions, which Ostorian answered as well as he could. Eighteen didn't come over at all.

The eighteen were mostly old cohorts. They were the cohorts of the north wall and the ore lifts, posted at the Arsenal for decades and staffed by retired line legacies and old centurions who had fought in the holds and come to the Arsenal to finish their service in a quiet place, and who had looked at what the Archflamen was and decided that whatever it was, they hadn't sworn to it. Their senior centurion was a woman named Varia Tull, whom Ostorian had known for thirty years. She'd taught him to strip a Thureos engine when he was nineteen. She came to see him in the inner ward on the first morning with her helmet under her arm and asked him, plainly, what he thought he was doing.

"Holding the Arsenal," he said. "For the Imperator."

"For the priest."

"For the Imperator, Varia. You saw the shot."

"I saw a shot." She looked at him for a long time. "Galba, the ceiling will come down the Stair. You know it will. The Third's nineteen kilometres from the north wall, and it built the inner belt, and it'll come down the Stair with forty regiments, and when it gets here every single one of us on this hill will be a traitor, and the ceiling won't give a shit which of us believed the priest. It'll take us back to the vats a thousand times over and put us in the ground every time." She put her helmet on. "My people and I are done with it. We're going up the ore lifts tonight, and we're going to tell the ceiling what's down here, and if you're any kind of officer, you'll let us go."

He thought about it. He would remember afterward that he had honestly thought about it, for perhaps a minute, standing in the inner ward with the Tabularium smoking across the gorge and the eastern terrace still burning, and that if Dace had not come back across the bridge at that moment with twelve of the Sworn behind him, he might have said yes.

Dace came back across the bridge.

"Centurion Tull," he said pleasantly. "We were just talking about you."

---

The eighteen cohorts went into the Number Four heat-well over two nights.

They didn't go easily. Tull's cohorts held the north wall and the ore-lift platforms against the Sworn for most of the first cycle, and they had the best guns on the north side of the spur, and it took Ostorian himself, in the end, with four of his own cohorts and a pair of Thureos tanks from the vaults, to break them. He did it well. He was a good soldier, and he did it as he'd been trained, in good order and by bounds, and when it was done he walked along the north wall in the smoke and counted his own dead and theirs, and found that he couldn't tell them apart. They were wearing the same armour. He'd known most of them for thirty years.

The survivors were marched to the Number Four well. The Lictors of the Flame did that part. Ostorian went along because Dace asked him to, and because he thought somebody who knew their names should be there, and he stood on the catwalk over the shaft where the Steward had died eleven months before and watched the Lictors walk his soldiers off the edge in their armour, two at a time, into the orange glare of the Phlegethon fire at the bottom.

Tull went last. She looked at him once, as they walked her to the edge, and he waited for her to say something and found that he didn't want to hear it. She didn't say anything. She stepped off the catwalk on her own before the Lictors could push her.

Afterwards, Dace stood with him on the catwalk and looked down the shaft.

"Thank you, Prefect," he said. "That can't have been easy."

"They were good soldiers."

"I'm sure they were. That's why it had to be done." Dace took out the small grey vial and tipped three drops onto the back of his hand. The smell of winter river came up off his skin, faint and cold in the heat of the well. "A bad soldier who doubts you will run away. A good one will wait until the ceiling comes down the Stair and then open the gate."

He licked his hand clean and put the vial away.

"You'll have the eighteen cohorts' armour and weapons by morning," he said. "Give them to the thralls."

---

After the garrison, it was the thralls' turn, and then the pilgrims', and for four months the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal ate itself.

Ostorian saw most of it. He didn't do much of it himself. He wasn't asked to, and he was careful not to volunteer, but he was Prefect of the Imperator's legion now, and that meant he was expected to be everywhere, and so he was. He saw the thrall gangs of the casting lines settle their scores with the thrall-masters of the Hephaestean orders in the first week, with bars of iron and ladles of slag and the long hooks they used to shift ingots in the quench-tanks. He saw some thrall-masters who had sworn on the Styx protected by the Lictors and some who had sworn on the Styx not protected at all. He saw the gangs turn on each other in the second week, when the thrall-masters ran out, and the thralls of the Number Two casting line fight the thralls of the Number Six for the old thrall-masters' quarters on the north slope with a viciousness that left four hundred dead on the ore road between them. He saw the pilgrims who had followed the Archflamen's crawlers down from the holds try to leave by the ore lifts when they realised what they'd followed, and saw the Sworn shoot them on the platforms, and saw the bodies stacked on the lift cages and sent up to the ceiling as a message.

He saw the Lictors of the Flame grow from six hundred to six thousand. They were drawn from the garrison and the thralls alike, from anybody who had proved himself in the first weeks, and they were given the flame-orange sashes and the iron rods and the right to kill without appeal any soldier of the Imperator's legion who wavered, ran or questioned an order. He saw the Sworn of Orrhae grow from forty to forty thousand, and then keep growing, and he saw the ash-mark on their foreheads, and he saw Dace choose their officers, every one, from a list that he kept in a small black book.

He saw the Laistrygonians brought up out of the deep barracks under the Number Eight line, forty thousand of them, three metres tall and stooped from the low ceilings of the barracks they'd been bred in, giant-blooded labourers made by the Hephaestean orders for the heavy work of the casting lines. The Lictors chained them in their barracks between shifts and fed them on combat stimulants, and when he asked Dace what they were for, the Haruspex said that they were for the walls.

He saw the keyers. There were about four hundred of them left on the spur, demigods of the Hephaestean orders posted to the Arsenal for the casting lines, and they were the only souls on the spur who could key a divine shell at the breech. The revolt had left them alone, more or less. Some of them had sworn on the Styx and some hadn't, and it didn't matter, because without them the eighty-one percent of the theatre's divine shells sitting in the Arsenal's vaults were just the largest pile of rocks in the theatre. Dace had them moved into the Archflamen's palace. The Lictors guarded them day and night, more closely than they guarded the Archflamen.

And in the fifth week, he went to the Archflamen's table, and sat in the seat that had been promised to him, and found that he was sitting below the salt.

---

The Archflamen ate at a long table on the terrace of the palace, under the Echo Mast, with the Vestals of the Hearth standing at his back and the burning eastern terrace below him. He ate little, and listened to his commanders as they reported, as he listened to everyone, with his kind tired face turned toward whoever was speaking and his hands open on the table, and when they had finished, he thanked them and said a blessing over them and did not give them an order. Ostorian had sat through eleven dinners at the table before the revolt, and he had loved them. He'd sat at the Archflamen's right hand, and talked about the garrison and the Flats and the Third, and the Archflamen had asked him questions and listened to the answers.

Now he sat at the far end of the table, with the thrall-captains and the Lictor-captains, and the Archflamen's right hand was Dace's.

He didn't complain. He was a good soldier, and he understood that the order of seating at a table was a matter of the Archflamen's pleasure. But he noticed that the Sworn officers in his sectors now held authority over his own centurions, and that when he asked Dace about it, the Haruspex smiled and said it was a practice of the Imperator's legion, and that when he asked the Archflamen about it, the Archflamen said that he was sure the Haruspex knew best.

He noticed, too, that there were things he was not told. He wasn't told where the Haruspex went at night when he left the palace by the north stair. He wasn't told why the Haruspex had begun to send Sworn down the Styx road to Depth 44 in parties of a dozen, and why the parties came back with river-water on their boots and fewer men than they'd left with. He wasn't told about the river at all. When he asked about the cold-smelling things the thralls had begun to see moving on the Phlegethon at night, below the Number Four docks, Dace told him not to concern himself with the river, since the river was the Haruspex's business, and the Flats were his.

So he concerned himself with the Flats. He knew them as well as any centurion of the Third, and he'd spent twenty years watching the Third fight on them, and he knew exactly how the Third would come. It would come down the Cinder Stair, and across the Flats from the north and west, and it would come slowly, by sapping and shelling, belt by belt, as it always had. He knew the Third's guns and its phalanxes and its Tactica. He'd read the manuals. He knew what the Répertoire would price.

He went down onto the Flats at the start of the second month, with four hundred of the garrison's old centurions behind him and a hundred thousand thralls behind them, and he began to dig.

---

There had been an outer belt once. It was begun a century before against the Phlegethon, when a reformation peak came up out of the river at the bend and the Hephaestean orders took fright, and it ran in an arc of a hundred and ninety kilometres from the Mora Cut in the north to the edge of the Demus Gorge in the south-west, and it had never been finished. Most of it was ditch. Some of it was ditch and parapet. A few kilometres of it, near the Mora Cut, had been finished with bunkers and wire, before the reformation peak burned itself out and the Hephaesteans forgot about it.

Ostorian finished it. He did it in eleven weeks, with a million thralls and the casting-line tools of the Hephaestean orders and the vaults of the Arsenal behind him, which held enough razor-wire to wrap the Flats twice and enough mines to sow it three times. He sent the thralls out onto the ash in their hundreds of thousands, in shifts, day and night, under the eyes of the Lictors, and they dug.

He was good at it. He'd read the Third's manuals. He sited the bunkers to cover each other, and he laid the wire in belts that funnelled an attacker into the killing grounds of the bunkers, and he sowed the mines in the funnels. He ranged the Arsenal's divine mortars on marker stakes across the Flats until every metre of ash between the outer belt and the Third's line was known. He dug saps out from the belt toward the Third, and then filled them in again, so that the Third's surveyors would map them and waste their shells on them. He dug a forward trench, and a support trench, and a reserve trench, and communication trenches between them, and then he went back to the forward trench and dug it deeper.

He trained the thralls with rifles from the vaults. It took a week to teach a thrall to fire a rifle and a month to teach him to hold a trench, and Ostorian had perhaps three months before the Third came down the Stair, and four million thralls, and four hundred old centurions to teach them. He did it anyway. He did it with the thralls' own militia companies, which the Arsenal's charter required, and which Dace had shown the Archflamen eleven months before; one thrall in four had drilled with them already, badly. He did it with the garrison's drill-masters and the Third's own manuals. He did it with the Lictors behind every company, and he tried not to watch what the Lictors did to the thralls who couldn't learn.

At the end of the third month he went up the Stair to the palace terrace and stood before the Archflamen at his table, in the place he had stood eleven months before when he still sat at the Archflamen's right hand, and reported that the outer belt was finished.

The Archflamen listened, as he always did. When Ostorian was done, he thanked him, and said a blessing over him, and asked him whether it would hold.

"Against the Third?" Ostorian considered. "For a while, Holiness. Not forever. It's a hundred and ninety kilometres long, and I have the soldiers to hold perhaps a third of it properly. The Third will come at it where it's weakest, which is the middle, and it'll break it in a rotation or two. Then we fall back to the second belt and start again."

"How long, for all of them?"

"The second belt is better. The inner belt the Third dug itself, and it's eight kilometres deep in places, and they'll be digging through their own grandfathers' work." He hesitated. "Four rotations, Holiness, if they come the way they always come. Six, perhaps, if we're lucky."

"And then?"

"And then they'll be at the Wall."

The Archflamen looked at him for a long moment with his kind, tired eyes.

"Thank you, Prefect," he said. "That's a very honest answer. I'm grateful for it."

At his right hand, Dace said nothing. He was smiling a little, indulgently, as if Ostorian had given a fine answer to a question nobody had asked, and the smile went down the Stair to the Flats with Ostorian that night and stayed with him in the trenches for a long while afterward.

**THE PENS, BENEATH THE TABULARIUM. ROTATION ZERO, CYCLE 11**

Mirren was eleven, and she had been in the pens under the Tabularium for six weeks before the fighting, and nine weeks since, and she knew exactly how many birds had gone over in that time, because she'd counted them.

She'd told nobody about the birds until the Haruspex came. The pens weren't a place for telling people things. They were a long low cellar under the Tabularium, cut into the black rock of the inner ward, with iron grilles at both ends and a row of iron cages down each side, and three hundred and eleven children and young people in the cages who had been taken out of the deep holds because the witch-blood was showing in them. They were waiting for Tithe Day, when the Black Ships came down to the ceiling to take them away. Nobody knew where the Black Ships went. Some of the older ones said that the Black Ships took you up to the stars, and you learned magic there and came back as a magister with a staff and a fine coat. Some of the older ones said they put you to sleep and used you to talk to each other through the dark. Mirren hadn't decided which she believed. She'd been born in Hold Kestrel, at the bottom of the Lethe road, and nobody she knew had ever seen a star.

The witch-blood had come out in her when she was nine. It started with dreams, and then with knowing things she shouldn't, and then, one night in the barracks at Kestrel, with the lamps all going out at once while she screamed in her sleep, and after that the hold's Flamen had come with an Enforcement officer and a registry form, and they had sent her down the road to the Arsenal in a crawler with eleven other children to wait for Tithe Day. She had cried most of the way, and hadn't cried since.

The birds were the noosphere. She knew that now, though she hadn't at first. When she was small, in the barracks at Kestrel, she had thought everyone could hear them, a great flight of birds going over high overhead, so many and so fast that they were always passing and never gone, and she had thought that the sound of them was just the sound that the world made. Then she'd found out that nobody else could hear them, and she'd stopped talking about it. When the Enforcement officer asked her, filling in the registry form, whether she ever heard anything, she said no. She'd been eight, and already knew better.

At the Arsenal, the birds were loud. They came and went up the Echo Mast all the time, thousands and thousands of them, going up out of the Arsenal to somewhere far above and coming down again, and they sang as they went past, all of them at once, a thin high singing she could hear through the rock of the inner ward if she put her ear to the wall of the cage. She'd thought, the first few weeks, that the singing was the loveliest thing she had ever heard.

Then the fighting happened.

She'd been asleep when it started. She woke up to shooting overhead, and the other children screaming, and the soldiers of the Tabularium running past the grilles at the end of the pen, and she put her hands over her ears and her face against the wall and listened to the birds instead, as she always did when it was bad, and that was when she'd felt them start going up.

They went up from the bridge, one at a time, all through the night. They were different from the other birds. They were bigger, and louder, and they went straight up the Mast in a long rising cry that she could feel in her teeth, and every time one of them went up she knew without knowing how that it was one of the soldiers she'd heard running past the grilles. She counted them. Twenty-two in the first two hours, fast, when the shooting was loudest. Then a few more, and a few more, and then nothing for a long time, and then a great many all at once, near the end, when the soldiers came up through the cellar past the cages and the other children screamed and screamed and the Lictors in the pen fought them and died. Two of those went up right beside her, through the bars of her cage, so loud that she was sick on the floor afterward.

Fifty-eight birds went up the Mast that night, and the fifty-ninth never made it.

She'd felt it start. It was right at the end, near the ninth hour, after the shooting had stopped, and it was louder than any of the others, a big bright bird that started to rise up off the bridge toward the Mast as all the others had, and then it stopped. Something caught it. She didn't know what. She felt it stop in the air above the bridge as if something had closed a hand around it, and she felt it beat its wings against whatever was holding it, for a long time, longer than she could stand, and then she felt it go quiet. It didn't go up. It didn't go anywhere. It just went quiet and stayed where it was.

She'd lain awake for a long time listening for it, after that. She could still feel it, sometimes, if she put her ear to the wall. It was up there somewhere, near the bridge, near the Mast. It wasn't singing.

The day after that, the Mast stopped too.

She'd been listening to the birds go up and down it for six weeks, and then one night in the middle of a sentence they stopped, all at once, and the Mast went quiet. For a whole day there was nothing. She thought she'd gone deaf. Then, faintly, the Mast started up again, changed. It had stopped sending birds up and was making a different sound, low and slow and steady, like nothing she had a word for, and the sound went down into the rock instead of up into the sky.

She didn't like it. It made her think of the river.

---

The Haruspex came to the pens on the ninth night after the fighting. He came alone, with a lamp, and he went down the row of cages slowly, looking into each one, and at the end of the row he unlocked the grille of the last cage and sat down on the floor inside it, cross-legged, with the lamp beside him. It was Mirren's cage.

"Hello," he said. "My name's Ilan. What's yours?"

She didn't answer. She was in the corner of the cage, as far from him as she could get, with her knees drawn up to her chin.

"That's all right," he said. "I'll come back tomorrow."

He came back the next night, and the next. He brought food from the Archflamen's table, real food, bread and dried fish and once a whole orange, which none of them had ever seen before and Mirren didn't know how to eat until he showed her. He brought blankets, and lamp-oil, and a Flamen of the Archflamen's household who washed the children's faces and said the rite over them. On the fourth night he brought the Archflamen himself, who walked down the row of cages barefoot in his white vestments with his kind tired face and told the children that he was sorry they had been frightened, and that nobody would ever take them up to the Black Ships, and that from now on they would eat at his own table.

They did. Every night after that, the three hundred and eleven of them were taken up the inner stairs of the Tabularium and across the bridge, where somebody had scrubbed the stones clean, and up the Pilgrim Stair to the palace, and sat at the long table on the terrace under the Echo Mast, below the salt, with the thrall-captains and the Lictor-captains and a big square man with a broken nose who ate without speaking and never looked at them. The Archflamen called them his children. He said a blessing over them every night.

After dinner, Dace sat with them.

He always sat with Mirren. He'd learned her name from the registry form the second night, and he used it softly, as if it were something he'd been given to look after. He asked her about Hold Kestrel, and about the lamps going out, and about her mother, who had died in the fog when Mirren was six and whom she barely remembered. He never asked about the birds. He waited.

On the eleventh night, she told him.

She didn't mean to. She'd decided a long time ago never to tell anybody about the birds, and she'd kept to it for three years. But he had given her an orange, and he'd sat with her every night, and he listened to her with his whole face, as nobody ever had. And in the end she told him about the birds going up and down the Mast, and the birds going up from the bridge, fifty-eight of them, and the one that didn't.

He listened without moving. When she was done, he was quiet for a long time.

"The one that didn't go up," he said at last, gently. "Can you still hear it?"

She nodded.

"Where is it?"

"Near the bridge." She hesitated. "It's not singing. It's just there. It's like it's waiting for something."

"And the Mast? You said the Mast changed."

"It sings down now," she said. "Into the rock. I don't like it."

"No," Dace said. "I don't suppose you do."

He took her hand in his. His hand was cool and dry and smelled faintly of cold river.

"Mirren," he said. "I'd like you to do something for me. Every night, when we sit together, I'd like you to tell me what you hear. The birds going over, and the ones going up, and the ones that don't. And anything else you hear. Anything at all." He smiled at her. It was a kind smile, nearly as kind as the Archflamen's. "Will you do that for me?"

She thought about it. She thought about the bird that wasn't singing, waiting up there near the bridge for something, and the low slow sound the Mast made now, going down into the rock.

"Why?" she asked.

"Because there's a great deal down here that nobody can hear but you," Dace said, "and I want to know what it's saying."

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION ZERO, CYCLE 41**

The Archflamen's letter reached Quarterdeck Nineteen on the forty-first cycle of Rotation Zero, in a sack of loose papers that had been tied to the cage of the Number Three ore lift and sent up to the ceiling with a corpse.

The corpse was a pilgrim. The papers were copies of the letter, three hundred and nine of them, all in the same neat scribe's hand, and over the next month, as they turned up in the holds along the Archflamen's old route and in the trenches of the Third's line north of the bend, the staff on the Quarterdeck counted a little under a hundred thousand more.

Corvis read the first one standing at the window of the conference hall, with the crystal floor under her feet and the Arsenal below. It was short. It said that the Archflamen held the Arsenal for the Imperator and for the soldiers of the theatre, that the College had abandoned the deep and the Répertoire had priced it, and that any soldier who laid down his rifle rather than fire on the Imperator's Hold would be received there as a brother. It was signed by the Archflamen in his own hand, and under the signature somebody had drawn the gold lamp of the Hearth.

"Desertion?" Castorre asked. He was sitting at the table behind her, reading one of his own.

"Point zero eight of a percent, across the Third, since the letters started arriving." She put the letter down. "Most of them were taken by the river trying to reach the spur. The Répertoire logged two hundred and eleven unaccounted for. It thinks most of those are in the Phlegethon."

"That's lower than I expected."

"The Third's soldiers don't care much about priests, sir. They care about the vats. The Archflamen can't offer them a vat." She hesitated. "The holds are another matter. Attendance at the vat-rites in the holds on his old route is down by half since the letters started. The Flamens are reporting that soldiers have stopped answering the rite. They won't give his response and they won't give ours, so they stand there in silence."

Castorre put his own letter down.

"The College?"

"The Flamen-Prior has asked the Command for a hearing to explain why the College didn't see what the revival was. The Command has declined to give him one, on the grounds that it's busy." She almost smiled. "The College is not happy, sir."

"No. I don't suppose it is." He looked at the letter for a while longer. Then he folded it carefully along its creases and put it in the inside pocket of his coat. "Leda."

"Sir."

"The Lethe line. Where are we?"

She knew this one by heart. She'd been checking it every cycle since the verdict, with the same cold feeling in her stomach.

"Forty-one cycles in. Sector 74B and its neighbours are holding on their second positions, firing four keyed rounds a cycle. Thaleia's reported six fog-crossings in the last rotation and turned all six with rifles and plasma, at four hundred transfers. The divine batteries in the Lethe sectors will run dry about the seventy-seventh cycle." She paused. "Thirty-six cycles left. A little over five weeks."

"And the Third?"

"The *22nd* Trench Regiment is at the top of the Cinder Stair and ready to start the descent on the seventy-fourth. Its first cohorts reach the foot of the Stair two cycles after that. The *22nd* is followed by the *2nd* Line Phalanx, then the *5th*, then the Assault Phalanxes and the *10th*. The relay pylons are going down the shelf wall behind them. The rail-line across the Flats to the line of departure is a third finished." She slid the movement table across the desk to him. It was eleven pages long. She'd written all of it herself, and she was proud of it. "On the Répertoire's schedule, the Third will be in position to open the outer belt by the thirtieth cycle of Rotation One."

"Too slow," Castorre said.

"It's the largest movement the Third has ever made, sir. Down a single road. Under the guns of an enemy who knows the road."

"I know." He was quiet for a moment. "I know, Leda. I'm not blaming you. I'm thinking about Thaleia, at four rounds a cycle, in the fog."

Corvis said nothing. There wasn't anything to say. She had spent forty years being taught that a staff officer's job was to make the numbers add up, and these numbers didn't, and no Tactica in the Academy's library had a chapter on what to do when the numbers didn't add up and the army had to march anyway.

Castorre picked up a stylus.

"Then we'll do it the way the Répertoire says," he said. "It's a sound plan. It's the plan we would have written. Belt by belt, by sapping and shelling. We'll come down the Stair and across the Flats and we'll take the lances first, and the outer belt, and the second, and the inner, and then the Wall." He signed the movement table at the bottom of the eleventh page, in his small neat hand, and dated it. "Rotation Zero, cycle seventy. I'll sign the first order of the siege on the seventieth, and the *22nd* starts down on the seventy-fourth. That's eleven weeks from the verdict, near enough."

"Yes, sir."

"Leda."

"Sir?"

"Send a copy of the movement table to the commander of Sector 74B," Castorre said, "with my compliments. She'll want to know when we're coming."

**LETHE HOLD, SECTOR 74B. ROTATION ZERO, CYCLE 70**

The copy reached Thaleia at the second bell of the seventieth cycle, in the middle of a fog-crossing, and Ilon read it to her while she was on the field-net trying to raise the gunners of her last divine battery.

"Compliments of the Lord-General," he said, over the sound of plasma packages bursting on the river below the hold. "The *22nd* starts down the Stair on the seventy-fourth. Third's in position to open the outer belt on cycle thirty of Rotation One. And there's a note at the bottom, ma'am. In his own hand. It says, 'Hold on. We're coming.'"

Thaleia put her hand over the field-net.

"Cycle thirty of Rotation One," she said. "Ilon, that's a hundred and thirty cycles from now."

"Yes, ma'am."

"We've got seven cycles of keyed rounds."

"Yes, ma'am."

She looked down through the overlay at the river, at the fog coming up off it in long grey banners and the shapes moving in the fog, and at the green line of her second positions, where the plasma packages were bursting in long white sheets and the troopers of Sector 74B were firing their rifles into the grey with nothing to aim at but the movement.

"Log it," she said. "Received with thanks."

Then she took her hand off the field-net and went back to trying to raise her gunners.
