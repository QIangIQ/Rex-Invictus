## Chapter 2: Judicium Peto

*"Method: single round, public, during rite."*

*Répertoire du Savoir Athénien, verdict ref. TAB-PA-0300*

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN, THE CEILING ABOVE THE CINDER FLATS. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 300**

Archmagister Cael Varros had been alive for eight hundred and twelve years, and he had spent roughly six hundred of them waiting for the Répertoire to finish a sentence.

Quarterdeck Nineteen hung from the roof of Tartarus on four basalt chains, each link the size of a Hoplon carrier, a long iron hull bolted into the rock nine hundred metres above the Cinder Flats. From the observation gallery a man could look straight down through a floor of smoked crystal at the Phlegethon bend, a crooked orange vein in the dark, and at the black spur of rock that rose out of the bend with the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal squatting on top of it. Varros rarely looked down. Heights didn't bother him, though the view did a little, much as a long ledger did when he knew there was a mistake on page four hundred and hadn't found it yet.

His office was at the stern, behind the relay vault, and it was small enough that the cognition chair took up a third of it. The other two thirds held a desk, a cot he had not used in eleven years, a shelf of paper files that nobody else on the Quarterdeck was allowed to touch, and Lector Iunia Pell, who was twenty-six and currently sitting on the floor with four panes open on her knees.

"It's moved it up the queue again," Pell said.

"I can see that, Lector."

"Third time this cycle. It was nine thousand and something at the start of the watch. Now it's eleven." She tilted a pane toward him, as though he couldn't read it from the chair. "Eleven, sir. Out of the whole theatre."

The pane showed the Répertoire's verdict queue for Tartarus, a column of amber reference numbers that scrolled upward in a slow, endless crawl. Every report written by every officer, clerk, Flamen, Lictor and logistics daemon below the ceiling ended up in it sooner or later, and most of them ended up at the bottom, where they stayed until the matter resolved itself or the officer died. Reports from the Tabularium detachment at the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal had lived at the bottom for most of a year. Varros had watched them pile up there, two hundred and nine of them, each one cross-indexed, each one pending.

Now one of them was eleventh.

"It's reading the oath-wells," Varros said.

"Sir?"

"The Styx oath-well records at Depth 44. It pulled them at the start of the watch. I watched it go." He tapped the arm of the chair, which woke a pane of his own, and flicked it across to her. "Then the desertion rolls for every hold on the Archflamen's route, then the ration draw, then the Flamens' attendance reports, then Rusk's last six. It's been building a case since the watch began. It does that, when it's decided something already and wants to know how much it'll cost."

Pell read the access log, which was long, and dull, and to Varros the most frightening document he'd seen in a decade.

"It's going to rule," she said.

"It's going to rule."

"On an Archflamen."

"On the Archflamen of the theatre," Varros said, "with twenty-seven million soldiers who take his rite at every vat. Yes. I'd be grateful if you'd get off the floor, Lector, because when it does, I'm going to need you to look like an officer."

She got off the floor.

The queue went on crawling. On the far side of the relay vault, the Quarterdeck's night watch was running the cycle's traffic down to the *3rd* Siege Army's line, routine and endless, troop rotations and shell counts and a long acrimonious exchange between two depot quartermasters over a missing consignment of boot-soles that had now gone on for four rotations and was, as far as Varros could tell, the only thing in the theatre anyone was enjoying.

At eleven minutes past the third bell, the queue stopped scrolling.

It didn't stop often. Varros had seen it happen nine times in eight centuries, and on each occasion something on the far side of a CODE 31 had stopped being alive. He sat up in the chair, which creaked, and put his hands flat on its arms.

The verdict came up in the middle of the pane in the same unhurried amber as everything else the Répertoire said, with no warning tone and no change in colour, as if it were a ration allocation or a missing shipment of boot-soles.

*CODE 31. JUDICIUM PETO. SUBMITTED FROM: TABULARIUM DETACHMENT, PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL.*

*VERDICT: ERASURE. SUBJECT: ORRHAE, SABINUS, ARCHFLAMEN. METHOD: SINGLE ROUND, PUBLIC, DURING RITE. FALSE FLAG: PHLEGETHON INCURSION. AVE IMPERATOR.*

Pell made a small noise.

Varros read it twice, which took him about four seconds, and then he read the annex, which took longer. The annex was where the Répertoire showed its working, for the benefit of any human officer who wanted to argue, and it ran to nine hundred and six pages. He went through the first forty at the speed of long practice.

The arithmetic was simple, once it was laid out. The Arsenal made eighty-one percent of the lineage-keyed divine shells fired in the theatre. Its output was holding at ninety-eight percent of quota, and the Répertoire projected, from the pattern of every apostate cult it had ever indexed, that the figure would fall to zero within two rotations of the Archflamen declaring himself, which he would do within six. If he were tried, he would have to be arrested, and the Tabularium's sixty soldiers could not arrest a man surrounded by eleven thousand followers and forty Vestals without a battle on the eastern terrace that the Arsenal would watch from every window. If he were recalled, he would refuse, and the refusal would be the declaration. If he died quietly, in his bed or down a heat-well, Dace would make a martyr of him within the cycle, and no one in the Arsenal would believe it was anything but murder.

If he died in public, in the middle of a vat-rite, struck down by something from the deep, his following would have watched the deep take him. It would have no ceiling to blame. The College would bury him with honours and send a new Archflamen to take up his charge, and the faith of twenty-seven million soldiers would be shaken by a tragedy rather than broken by a scandal. The Répertoire had priced the alternative, a College trial of an Archflamen of the Lord Imperator, at a fourteen percent fall in rite attendance across the theatre and an unquantified rise in desertion. It had priced this at a fraction of a percent.

"It wants the Tabularium to shoot him," Pell said, "during the rite, and make it look like the river did it."

"Yes."

"Can it do that? I mean, can it order that? He's an Archflamen. The College..."

"The College will be informed of the verdict once the verdict is executed," Varros said, pointing at line four of the annex, where the Répertoire had said exactly that. "The Command was informed eleven seconds ago. Enforcement owns the Tabularium. The Tabularium asked for a CODE 31. Lictor-Superior Rusk submitted for judgement on the Archflamen's household in report two hundred and four, and the Répertoire has now judged." He leaned back. "She asked for this. She just hasn't seen what she asked for yet."

He sat for a moment with the verdict on the pane in front of him.

It wasn't his verdict. Quarterdeck Nineteen didn't render verdicts, and neither did he. The Quarterdeck was the relay between the *3rd* Siege Army's line, the holds of the Flats and the Watchpoint nine hundred kilometres above them, and Varros was its Archmagister, which meant that he was responsible for making sure the things the Répertoire said reached the people it said them to, intact and in the correct order. He had done that job well for six hundred years, and in all that time he had sent on every verdict that crossed his desk without changing a word, and he had stopped noticing, some time in his third century, that he was allowed to read them first.

He was noticing now.

"Sir," Pell said, carefully, "are you going to send it?"

"I'm going to query it."

He opened a query pane, which in six hundred years he had done perhaps two hundred times, and typed into it with two fingers. He wasn't a quick typist. The Répertoire had never once complained.

*CODE 23 – Inquisitio. Ref. TAB-PA-0300. Query: confidence of execution. Detachment strength 60. No precision marksman on Arsenal roll in last census. Archflamen escorted by forty Vestals of the House of the Hearth at all public rites. Query: probability Vestal escort carries ward or aegis unknown to Tabularium. C. Varros, Archmagister, QD-19.*

He sent it. Pell watched the pane as though it might bite.

The answer came back in four seconds, which for the Répertoire was a long, slow, considered breath.

*Query received. Marksman: Vell, Tertia, Enforcement, Tabularium roll since R-6, qualification Sagittaria Prima. Vestal ward probability: 0.04. Confidence of execution: 0.91. Verdict stands. Transmit.*

"Ninety-one," Pell said.

"I heard."

"That's good, isn't it? For a CODE 31?"

"It's very good for a CODE 31." He looked at the four percent, which was a small number, and at the nine percent left over, which was not so small when it was a man's life on one side of it and the faith of a theatre on the other. "It's not very good for an Archflamen. The last time it ruled on a Flamen of rank, it gave itself ninety-eight and was still wrong."

"What happened?"

"He had a twin."

Pell opened her mouth, and then shut it again.

Varros put his finger on the transmit glyph and held it there for a second longer than it needed. Then he pressed it, and the verdict went down the Echo Mast to the Tabularium on the inner ward of the Acropolis, nine hundred metres below his floor and nineteen kilometres west, in eleven seconds, intact and in the correct order.

"Log it," he said. "Code 31 transmitted, third bell plus eleven, my authority. And put a flag on the Tabularium's relay. I want to see every packet that comes back up that Mast until this is over."

"Yes, sir. What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to read the other eight hundred and sixty-six pages," Varros said, "and then I'm going to have a drink."

**THE TABULARIUM, THE INNER WARD. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 300**

Aemilia Rusk had wanted a verdict for two hundred and nine reports, and now that she had one, she sat at her desk with it on the relay pane and found that she badly wanted to give it back.

She read it four times. The amber didn't change.

The office at the top of the Tabularium was quiet in the third watch. Through the window behind her the Echo Mast climbed into the dark, a spire of black iron hung with relay vanes, and below it the gorge between the inner ward and the outer dropped away into nothing, crossed by a single bridge of fitted stone. Across the gorge, the eastern terrace was lit from end to end with tin lamps. They burned every night now. The thralls kept them burning in shifts, and the preachers walked among them, and at the far end of the terrace, beneath the steps of the Archflamen's palace, the Lictors of the Flame had put up a whipping post of black iron, and nobody had taken it down.

"Single round," she said aloud. "Public. During rite."

There was nobody to hear it. She had sent the duty clerk down to the records room twenty minutes ago, before the verdict arrived, and she was glad of it now.

She had imagined, over two hundred and nine reports, a great many things the Répertoire might do. An inspection had been her favourite: a Command staff officer coming down the ore lift with a writ in one hand and a cohort behind him, and the Archflamen's household broken up and sent home and Dace shipped off to some College tribunal on a ceiling world where they could argue about his theology at length. She had imagined a recall. She had imagined, in the bad nights, the Répertoire ordering her to arrest the man herself, and had worked out exactly how many of her sixty would die trying, which was all of them. She had never once imagined that the Répertoire would read her reports and decide that the cheapest answer was to murder an Archflamen of the Lord Imperator in front of ten thousand people and blame the river.

She found, with some surprise, that she didn't think it was wrong. That bothered her more than the verdict.

She keyed the internal net.

"Vell," she said. "My office."

---

Tertia Vell came up the stairs four minutes later with her hair still wet from the wash-house and her rifle over her shoulder, which was a habit Rusk had spent six rotations failing to break. She was a narrow, pale woman with a long jaw and a long neck and the stillness of somebody who had learned to sit in one place for a living. She had been a marksman in the Lethe holds before Enforcement poached her, and before that she had been a gunner's daughter in a ceiling depot, and she had once told Rusk, in the only personal conversation they had ever had, that she joined Enforcement because Lictors got better boots.

"Ma'am."

"Shut the door."

Vell shut the door. Rusk turned the relay pane around on the desk, so that it faced her, and watched her read it.

Vell was not a reader of faces, and nobody had ever got much out of hers. She read the verdict once, slowly, and then she read the annex reference at the bottom, and then she looked up.

"Sagittaria Prima," she said. "That's me."

"It named you in the query response. Archmagister on Nineteen asked it about confidence of execution, and it came back with your name."

"Huh." Vell considered that. "I didn't think it knew I existed."

"It knows everyone exists. It just doesn't care until it needs them." Rusk sat back. "I'm not going to order you to do this."

"It's a CODE 31, ma'am. You don't have to order me."

"I know what it is." Rusk heard her own voice go hard and made it soften again. "I'm telling you that if you want to go to the vats instead, I'll put you on the relief roll tonight and send you up the ore lift and nobody will think the worse of you, because I'll put it in your file that I sent you."

Vell looked at her for a long moment.

"Who goes instead?"

"Nobody goes instead. There's nobody else on the roll who can make the shot."

"Then I'll go," Vell said. "But I want to know where the shot is, before you tell me how the river did it."

Rusk got up and went to the map case.

---

The Pilgrim Stair climbed the outer face of the cinder cone from the eastern terrace to the summit, nine terraces of cut stone, broad as a road and steep as a ladder in places, worn into shallow bowls by eight thousand years of feet. At the top, under the palace steps, was the high altar of the Arsenal's vat-rite, a slab of red marble quarried from the Katabasion itself, and below it, on the first and second terraces, the thralls gathered in their thousands for the Archflamen's sermons.

The cone was honeycombed. The Hephaestean orders had cut cooling flues through it for eight thousand years, to bleed the heat of the casting lines up and out of the rock before it could crack the walls of the Katabasion below. Most of the flues were still in use and would kill a woman in a minute. A few had been closed off when the heat-wells moved, and left to cool, and forgotten.

Rusk unrolled the old Hephaestean survey across the desk and put her finger on one of them.

"Third terrace, east face," she said. "Flue 3-E-11. It was shut off when they rebuilt Number Seven well, three hundred years ago. It runs up through the cone at an angle and comes out in a grille under the lip of the third terrace, about sixty metres below the altar and forty to the left of it. It's never been opened since. The grille's rusted over. The Lictors keep the third terrace clear for the Archflamen's procession, so nobody walks it during a rite."

"Sixty metres." Vell studied the angle. "Upward shot, through a grille."

"You'll take the grille out first."

"And the river?"

Rusk turned over the next sheet. It was an Enforcement requisition in her own hand, made out that afternoon, before the verdict came, which she had written with the uneasy sense, after two hundred and nine reports, that she knew where this was going.

"Salamander round," she said. "Phlegethon pattern. The armoury has a case of them from the last time a fire-thing came up out of the river at the docks, nine rotations back. It's a cast round with a core of river-fire bound into it. When it hits, the core burns out through the wound and leaves the kind of char a salamander's tongue leaves. A Phlegethon thing that comes up through the vents and strikes at a crowd would leave exactly that."

Vell nodded slowly. She knew the pattern. Everyone who had served at Depth 40 knew the pattern; the Phlegethon threw up something with teeth every rotation or so, and the vents of the cone were the obvious way up.

"The flue opens on the river side of the cone," Rusk said. "It vented heat to the river for five thousand years. When you fire from inside it, the shot will come up out of the cone from the vent of a river flue, in a char-pattern that matches a river strike, in the middle of a rite. Anyone looking for the source will find a rusted grille over a shaft that runs down toward the Phlegethon. And by the time anyone thinks to climb down it, you'll be out the bottom end."

"Where's the bottom end?"

"Number Seven well's old service tunnel. It comes out under the third terrace, on the river side. It's been bricked. I'll have it unbricked tonight." She hesitated. "The tunnel runs back into the cone and joins the inner service ways. There's a way up from there to the inner ward that the Hephaesteans used before they built the bridge. You come home through the rock, and you're back in this office before the rite is over."

"And if they find the tunnel?"

"Then you shoot whoever finds it."

Vell rolled the survey back up herself, neatly, and held it out.

"When?"

"The verdict says during rite. The Archflamen preaches at a full vat-rite every twelfth cycle. The next one is on the three hundred and twelfth." Rusk took the survey. "I want you in the flue tonight. If we wait, we'll be seen moving, and Dace sees everything."

"Eleven days in a flue."

"Eleven days in a flue."

"Fuck me," Vell said, with feeling, and then, because Rusk was looking at her, "Ma'am. Sorry."

"No. That's about right." Rusk almost smiled. "Get your kit. I'll sign out the rounds myself."

**FLUE 3-E-11, THE CINDER CONE. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLES 301 TO 312**

The first thing Tertia Vell learned about Flue 3-E-11 was that the word "disused" meant something different to a Hephaestean.

It had been closed for three hundred years and was still hot enough that she could feel it through her boots when she climbed in, at the bottom of the old Number Seven service tunnel, a little after the first bell of the three hundred and first cycle. The air inside was thick and dead and smelled of iron and old slag, and the walls of the shaft were glazed black where the heat of the casting lines had passed through them for five thousand years before that, as smooth as the inside of a bottle. It ran upward through the cone at about forty degrees, a little over a metre across, too steep to walk and too shallow to climb, and she went up it on her elbows and knees with the rifle on her back and the case of salamander rounds strapped to her thigh, for three hundred metres, in total darkness, while the heat came up through the rock and cooked her slowly from the stomach outward.

It took her four hours. At the top, under the grille, there was a ledge where the flue had once turned up into a vent-throat, about the width of a bunk and two-thirds of its length. She lay on it, breathing, and looked up through the grille at the lamps of the third terrace, and thought that she was going to be here for eleven days and that it was the worst place she had ever been in her life, including the Lethe holds, including the time at Hold Kestrel when the fog had come in and she'd spent a night in a drainage pipe with a dead man.

Then she got to work.

The grille was set into the lip of the terrace, an iron lattice crusted with three centuries of rust, overgrown along its upper edge with a pale fungus that lived on the heat of the cone. She took it apart one rivet at a time over two nights, working only during the second and third watches, when the terrace above was empty, and filing the heads off with a diamond rasp wrapped in cloth so it made no sound. She left the lattice in place, held up by the fungus and a few strands of wire, so that it looked from outside exactly as it always had. When the time came, a push would drop it into the flue.

She cut her firing port into the fungus with a knife no longer than her thumb. It gave her a slot about two fingers wide and a hand long, and through it, if she lay on her side with her cheek against the hot stone and the rifle laid along her body, she could see the whole of the high altar sixty metres above her, red marble against black, and the palace steps behind it, and a slice of the sky of the cone where the Echo Mast stood up on the inner ward with its relay vanes turning slowly in the heat.

After that, there was nothing to do but wait.

---

She learned the terrace in the first three days.

The Lictors of the Flame swept it twice a cycle, at the start of the first watch and at the change of the third, in pairs, with orange sashes over their armour and short iron rods on their belts. They were thorough in the way new men were thorough, looking into every crevice and every lamp-niche, and the first time a pair of them stopped over her grille, Vell lay in the dark with her heart slamming against the stone while one of them prodded the fungus with his rod and said to the other that it was a disgrace, the state of these old vents, and someone should do something about it. The other said that he'd mention it to the Prefect. Neither of them did. They came past every cycle after that, and every cycle they looked at the grille, and every cycle it stayed exactly as ugly as they remembered it.

The thralls came up the Stair every cycle too, though never to the third terrace. They gathered below, on the first and second, in the long hours between shifts, to sing and to listen to the preachers and to look up at the palace. They were gaunt and filthy, burned along the hands and forearms, with the debt-brand of the Hephaestean orders on the backs of their necks, and many of them had the slow, careful movements of people who had lost fingers to the casting lines and learned to work around the gaps. Vell watched them through her slot. They sang well. She had heard the vat-rite sung a hundred times in the holds, and it had never sounded like this, three thousand voices in the dark on the side of a burning hill, singing for a man who had told them that the Imperator knew their names.

On the fourth cycle, the Archflamen came out onto the palace steps.

She had seen him twice before, at a distance, on the eastern terrace, and both times he had been surrounded. This time there were only two Vestals with him, and Dace a step behind in his grey coat, and a scribe. Orrhae was tall and grey-haired, barefoot as always, and he had the long, kind, tired face of a man who had spent a lifetime being patient with people. He stood at the top of the steps and looked out over the terraces at the singing thralls, and he raised one hand to them, and the singing broke up into a roar that Vell felt through the rock under her belly.

She put the reticle on his chest. Her finger was outside the guard.

Sixty metres, upward, through a slot in a fungus, at a man standing still in good light. She'd made shots at four times the range through worse. She lay and watched him through the scope for a long time, as she would have watched anything she'd been told to kill, learning where he put his weight when he turned, and she found that it was harder than it should have been, and she didn't know why, and she didn't like not knowing.

Dace said something to him. The Archflamen laughed, and the laugh was warm and unguarded and carried all the way down to her. Then the two of them went back inside, and the Vestals with them, and Vell took her eye off the scope and lay on her back in the dark, sweating, and drank a mouthful of water that tasted of the bladder, and thought about nothing for a while.

---

The relay buzzed against her collarbone twice a cycle, on a burst so short it wouldn't show on the Arsenal's own net. The first time, at the change of each watch, it was a single amber line from the Tabularium.

*Status.*

She thumbed back a single pulse for green.

The second time, at the end of the third watch, it was Rusk, by voice, in a whisper so low it was mostly breath.

"Vell."

"Ma'am."

"How is it?"

"Hot."

"Water?"

"Half."

"Food?"

"Fine." It wasn't. The ration paste had gone to something with the texture of grease in the heat, and she could only make herself eat a mouthful at a time. "Ma'am, I think the Archflamen's people know there's something wrong."

A pause.

"Why?"

"The Lictors doubled the sweep this cycle, and they brought dogs." She had lain rigid while the hounds went past, big rangy grey things with burned muzzles, and one of them had stopped at the grille and sniffed, and then sneezed violently at the fungus and gone on. "They're not looking for me. I don't think they're looking for anybody in particular. But they're looking."

"They've been looking for a month," Rusk said. "Dace has been seeing assassins under the bed since the Steward went down the well. That's not you." The breath of her voice went quieter still. "Six more."

"Six more."

The relay went dead.

---

Dace came out onto the third terrace on the seventh night.

She heard him before she saw him. The Lictors had swept the terrace an hour before, and it was supposed to be empty, and she was lying on her side with her eyes closed and her mind somewhere at the bottom of a ceiling depot, eight years old, watching her mother load a mortar. The footsteps brought her back fast. They were light and unhurried, and they came down the Stair from the palace and stopped, and then came along the terrace, toward her, one at a time.

She rolled very slowly onto her front and put her eye to the slot.

It was the Haruspex, alone, in his grey coat, with a small lamp in one hand. He walked along the lip of the third terrace in the dark, looking down at the thrall camps on the slopes below and at the black ribbon of the Phlegethon beyond the Wall, and he was humming something under his breath, and when he reached the grille he stopped, and turned, and crouched down on his heels, and looked at it.

Vell stopped breathing. The rifle was against her body. Her hand was on the grip. At three metres, through the fungus, she could have shot him in the face before he knew she was there, and the whole of Rusk's plan would have died with him in a burst of salamander fire on the third terrace.

Dace looked at the grille for a long time. Then he took a small grey glass vial out of his coat and uncorked it, and Vell smelled, faint and cold through the heat of the flue, something like a winter river.

He tipped three drops onto the back of his hand. He put his hand to his mouth.

"Do you know," he said quietly, to the grille, or to the cone, or to nobody, "they used to throw children down these, before the Hephaesteans came. When the ground shook. To make it stop." He corked the vial. "I've been reading the old surveys. There are three hundred and nine flues in this hill, and twenty-one of them are cold, and I've been to look at all of them. I was a clerk once. I like lists."

He stood up, brushing ash off his knees.

"Good night," he said, and went back up the Stair.

Vell lay in the flue until the sound of his footsteps had gone and the lamp was out of sight, and then for a long while after. When the relay buzzed at the end of the watch, she thumbed back green, and when Rusk's whisper came and asked her how it was, she said, "Hot," and nothing else, and could not have explained to anyone why.

---

**THE PILGRIM STAIR. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 312**

On the three hundred and twelfth cycle the whole of the Arsenal came up the Stair.

The thralls came first, as they always did, in long ragged columns from the casting lines and the barracks of the Hephaestean orders, still in their work-leathers, with the heat of the wells still coming off them. They filled the first terrace, and then the second, and then they spilled out along the lower slopes of the cone and onto the eastern terrace below it until Vell could see nothing from her slot but faces and lamps, ten thousand of them at least, more coming. The garrison came in their ranks behind them, the Arsenal's grey armour with the forge-hammer of the Hephaestean orders on the pauldron, and the pilgrims in their robes, and the Sworn with ash on their foreheads. The Lictors of the Flame lined the edges of the terraces in their orange sashes, a pace apart, facing outward.

The third terrace stayed empty. The Lictors had swept it twice that cycle and posted a pair at each end of it, and in the middle, about forty metres to the right of her grille, the Archflamen's procession would come down from the palace to the altar and go back up again. For the rest of its length it was bare stone, and lamps, and the rusty, fungus-grown grille that everybody had been looking at for eleven days and nobody had seen.

She had been awake for most of the last two cycles. She drank the last of her water at the second bell, and then she lay on the ledge with the rifle along her body and a salamander round in the chamber and four more in the case at her thigh, and waited.

The singing started at the third bell. It went on for an hour, rising and falling, and Vell had heard enough of it over eleven days that she knew every word without trying, and the hair stood up on the back of her neck anyway. At the end of the hour the great doors of the palace opened, and the Vestals came out.

There were forty of them, in white, with the Hestian lamps on their short spears burning the soft, steady gold that never went out, and they came down the palace steps in two files and took their places on either side of the altar. Behind them came the choir, and behind the choir came the Archflamen.

He was barefoot. He wore the white and gold vestments of his rank, and over them the crimson stole of a College Archflamen, and on his chest, outside the vestments, the lamp-medallion of the Hearth that Vestals wore, which she'd seen on him before. He came down the steps slowly, with his hands open at his sides, and the thralls on the terraces below fell silent one terrace at a time as he passed, until the only sound left on the whole cone was the low roar of the Phlegethon at the foot of the spur, and the footsteps of the procession, and the slow turning of the vanes of the Echo Mast.

He reached the altar. He put his hands on the red marble.

"You came," he said.

His voice carried down the terraces without effort. Vell had never understood how he did that.

"Every one of you came," he said. "I can see you. I know you. You came up out of the wells with your hands burned, and you came out of the barracks and the pens, and you came all the way from the holds, some of you, on foot, down a road that kills people." He looked out over the crowd. "You came because somebody told you that the Lord Imperator knows your names. I'm here to tell you that it's true."

She put the reticle on his chest, a hand's width below the medallion, over the heart.

"He went down," the Archflamen said. "Before any of us. He went into the dark alone, and the dark did not keep him, and when he comes back up the road you're walking..."

Vell let out half a breath and held it, and pushed the grille out of the fungus with the muzzle of the rifle. It dropped into the flue below her with a soft scrape that nobody heard over his voice.

"...the deep will be emptied, and the vats will close, and there will be no..."

She fired.

The salamander round left the muzzle as a streak of orange fire, up out of the dark mouth of the old river flue, sixty metres through the hot air of the cone to the altar, so fast that by the time the crack of it reached her own ears it had already arrived. She saw the Archflamen jerk. She saw the fire hit him square in the chest, a hand's width below the medallion.

And she saw it stop.

It stopped in the air a hand's breadth from his vestments, splashing into a flat, bright disc of white-gold light that sprang out of nowhere around his body. The salamander core burst against it and spread, orange fire racing across the white light in a sheet, and the light held it, and burned it, and swallowed it, and went out.

The Archflamen stood at the altar with his hands still on the marble. His vestments were smoking. He had not fallen.

*Aegis*, Vell thought, very clearly. She had heard of them and never seen one, a Hestian ward cast by a Virgo Maxima into a pendant of the Hearth, which would take the first killing thing that came at its wearer and spend itself to stop it. They weren't given lightly. They were never given to anyone outside the House. The Répertoire had put the probability at four percent.

The pendant was the lamp-medallion. It was the thing she'd been aiming under.

Her hands had chambered the second round before she'd finished thinking it. On the altar, the Archflamen was looking down at his chest, and the Vestals were moving, spears coming down, and the crowd on the terraces hadn't yet understood what it had seen. She put the reticle on the medallion itself and fired again.

The second round struck the pendant dead centre. This time the white light only flickered, a thin, guttering flash, and she saw the medallion crack across its face, the gold splitting and the little glass lamp inside it shattering, and she saw the salamander fire spill off it and across his shoulder and out into the air behind him in a long orange streamer that hit the palace steps and splashed. He staggered, and went down on one knee, with his hand pressed to his chest, and then he got up again.

He was alive. The aegis had spent itself on the first round and, cracked, had still turned the second.

The scream went up from the terraces in a single sound, ten thousand voices together, and under it Vell heard the Archflamen's voice, not shouting, saying something to the Vestals that she couldn't make out, and saw Dace come out of the choir at a run and put himself in front of him. She had three rounds left. She put the reticle on Dace, and in the instant before she fired, forty Vestals raised their spears and their lamps, and a curtain of gold light went up across the top of the Stair from one end of the altar to the other, and her shot burst against it and fell away in sparks.

Then the Vestals turned, all of them together, and looked down the slope of the cone, straight at the flue.

She'd known she had to go. Some animal part of her had known since the first round stopped in the air. She pushed off the ledge feet first, and slid.

---

The flue had taken four hours to climb. She went down it in less than a minute, on her back, with the rifle clutched against her chest and the glazed stone burning through her suit, faster and faster, ricocheting off the walls, her boots bouncing off the turns. Above her she heard shouting, a great deal of it, and then something came into the flue after her, a light, gold and steady, and she understood that a Vestal had thrust a lamp down the throat of the vent and was following its light with her eyes.

She hit the bottom of the flue at a speed that drove the air out of her body and left her sprawled in the dark of the Number Seven service tunnel with blood in her mouth, and she was up and running before her lungs had remembered how to work.

The service tunnel went back into the cone, rough-walled and low, and she knew it by touch; she had walked it three times in the dark before the night she went in, and she counted the turnings with her fingers on the wall. Left, left, right, and the long straight run. Behind her the noise of the terraces came down through the rock as a dull, endless thunder, and somewhere ahead of her was the junction with the inner service ways and the old Hephaestean stair that went up into the inner ward, and the Tabularium, and Rusk.

She reached the junction. A lamp was burning there.

It was a tin lamp, of the kind the thralls kept lit on the eastern terrace, sitting on the floor in the middle of the junction. Beside it, sitting on an upturned slag-bucket with his elbows on his knees, was a thrall. He was young and bald and his forearms were burned black from the wrist to the elbow, and he looked up at her as she came around the corner as if he'd been expecting her for some time.

"He said you'd come this way," the thrall said.

Vell shot him.

She'd been running too hard to aim properly, and the round went through his shoulder rather than his chest. The salamander core burned out of the wound in a gout of orange fire, and he fell off the bucket screaming, and from the side passages around the junction, from the dark on every side of it, came the light of more lamps, a dozen of them, two dozen, and the sound of feet.

She had one round left. She chambered it, and put her back to the wall, and found herself facing the Sworn of Orrhae, a company of them with ash on their foreheads and garrison rifles in their hands, filling the side tunnels and the junction and the way ahead.

At their front was a man in a grey coat.

"Tertia Vell," Dace said pleasantly. "Lethe holds, then Enforcement. Your file says you're very good. I'm afraid I've been looking forward to this."

She fired at him.

The round struck the air a hand's width in front of his face and burst in a disc of white-gold light exactly like the first one, and Vell had time to see, hanging at his throat on a thin chain under the collar of the coat, a second lamp-medallion of the Hearth, before the butt of a rifle struck her across the side of the head and the junction went away.

---

**THE PILGRIM STAIR. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 312, FOURTH BELL**

When she came round, she was on the third terrace, on her knees, with her hands tied behind her back and the barrel of a garrison rifle resting against the back of her neck.

The noise was the first thing she noticed. The terraces below her had gone from screaming to something else, a deep, wordless, rising sound she'd heard only once before, at a hold that was about to be overrun, when the soldiers in the line had all seen the same thing coming at them at once. The second thing she noticed was the heat on the side of her face. She turned her head a fraction and saw the high altar thirty metres above her, smoking, and the scorch of salamander fire across the palace steps behind it, and the Archflamen standing at the altar with his stole torn and his vestments black down one side.

Dace was beside him, speaking to him, a hand on his arm. The Archflamen was shaking his head. Then he put the Haruspex gently aside and came down the steps, alone, past the Vestals, until he stood at the edge of the altar platform looking down at her.

"Is this the one?" he said.

"This is the one." It was the Lictor with his rifle on her neck. "Came out of the old flue, Holiness. Down the vent and into the service ways."

The Archflamen looked at her for a long time. She looked back at him. He had a kind face. It looked very tired.

"What's your name?" he asked.

She didn't answer.

"She's called Vell," Dace said, coming down the steps behind him. "Tertia Vell."

He turned, not to her and not to the Archflamen, but outward, to the terraces and the ten thousand faces below them and the thousands more on the slopes, and raised both his hands for silence. It took a long time to come. When it did, it came in a wave that rolled down the hillside from the top of the Stair to the eastern terrace, until the only sound left was the river.

"Brothers," he said. "Sisters. You saw it. Every one of you saw it. The fire came up out of the river, out of the old vents that go down to the river, and it came for him in the middle of the rite. The deep sent it." His voice was quieter than the Archflamen's, and yet somehow it went further. "The deep is afraid of him. It always has been. It knows what he is, and it knows what he means for it, and it sent a killer up through the rock to put out his light before he could come down to it. And the Hearth turned it aside. The Hearth turned it aside twice!"

The roar that answered him shook dust from the palace steps.

Dace let it go on for a while. Then he raised his hands again, and turned, and looked down at Vell, and something in his face changed.

"We took the assassin in the service ways," he said, in the same quiet, carrying voice. "We expected one of the river's things. A fire-wight, or a salamander, or some poor soul it had taken." He crouched beside her. With one hand, gently, he took hold of the sleeve of her flight suit at the left shoulder, where the grime of the flue had crusted over it, and rubbed at it with his thumb.

Under the soot, on her sleeve, was the silver flash of the Enforcement directorate, the Empire's crowned eye.

"Then we found this," he said.

He said it quietly, and the thralls on the first terrace could see it anyway, and they told the second, and the second told the slopes, and the sound that came up off the cone this time was nothing she had ever heard in her life, and she hoped she never would again.

"The river didn't send her," Dace said, standing, holding up his thumb with the soot on it. "The ceiling did."

**THE TABULARIUM, THE INNER WARD. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 312, FOURTH BELL TO CYCLE 313, FIRST BELL**

Rusk saw the shot from the top of the Tabularium.

She had been at the window since the third bell, with the long glass on the sill and the relay on the desk, watching the procession come down the steps, and she'd seen the round go up the side of the cone in its streak of fire and hit the Archflamen in the chest and stop. She'd seen the white light. She'd seen the second round, and the pendant crack, and the old man go down on one knee and get up again.

She'd known it was over before the crowd did. She'd keyed the internal net with a hand that was, she noted, entirely steady.

"All posts, all posts, Rusk. Stand to. Gate-towers and the bridge. Ammunition draw at the armoury, everything we have, now."

The detachment had been standing ready since the second bell, half of it in the gate-towers and half in the barracks at the foot of the Tabularium, armoured and with weapons loaded. She'd kept them ready every cycle for eleven days without telling them why, and if any of them had worked it out, they had kept it to themselves. They went to their posts in under three minutes. She heard them on the stairs, boots and the clatter of rifles and somebody, somewhere, swearing steadily and inventively under his breath, and she was proud of them, and wished she had told them more.

She went back to the window. On the third terrace, under the altar, a cluster of lamps had gathered around something on the ground, and Dace was talking.

She couldn't hear what he was saying from the Tabularium, and she watched him crouch beside the thing on the ground and lift its arm, and she watched the crowd's answer go up the cone, and she put down the long glass and keyed the relay to the Echo Mast.

"Flash priority. Rusk, Tabularium, to Quarterdeck Nineteen, attention Archmagister Varros. Verdict three hundred failed. Repeat, failed. Subject wore an aegis of the House of the Hearth under his vestments. Marksman taken alive. The Haruspex has identified her as Enforcement before the assembly. I expect the Tabularium to be stormed within the hour. Request immediate support." She stopped, then added, "Request, at minimum, acknowledgement. Rusk."

Eleven seconds.

*Flash received. Ref. TAB-PA-0312. Escalated to Command. Support: under review. QD-19 acknowledges. C. Varros.*

She looked at the last two words for a moment. A human being had written them. Somewhere nine hundred metres above her and nineteen kilometres away, an old man in a cognition chair had read her signal and typed his own name under it with two fingers, so that she'd know somebody had.

"Thank you," she said to the empty office, and picked up her rifle, and went down to the bridge.

---

They came across the gorge just after the fifth bell.

The Tabularium stood on the inner ward with its back to the Echo Mast, and the only way to it from the outer ward was the bridge, a single span of fitted stone forty metres long and four wide, with a gate-tower at the far end, on the outer ward side, and another at the near end, under the walls of the Tabularium itself. The gorge beneath it was a hundred metres deep and full of heat-shimmer from the old flues at its bottom. Rusk had twenty-two soldiers in the far gate-tower, eighteen in the near, and twenty on the bridge itself behind a barricade of overturned records-carts and sandbags that they'd built in the last half hour with their bare hands. She had six heavy rifles and two light mortars and every round in the armoury. She had a relay, and the Mast, and nobody on the other end of it who could reach her in less than four cycles.

The first wave was thralls. They came along the outer ward from the Stair in a mob, with lamps and tools and bars of iron from the casting lines, and a few rifles among them, and they came at the far gate-tower and the barricade on the bridge without stopping, and the Tabularium's heavy rifles cut them down in the gate-arch until the arch was choked with bodies and the ones behind had to climb over them. They kept coming. Rusk had served in the Lethe holds for nine years and seen the deep come up in its thousands, and it had never been like this. The things of the deep came because they were hungry. These people came because they believed, and they died believing, and the ones behind them came over the bodies singing.

"Hold the fucking bridge!" she shouted down the span. "Make them pay for every metre of it!"

They held. The first wave broke on the far gate-tower and pulled back, leaving the arch heaped with dead, and in the gap that followed, Rusk heard the engines.

The second wave was the garrison. Prefect Ostorian's cohorts came along the outer ward behind a pair of Thureos tanks from the vaults, squat black things with the forge-hammer painted on their glacis, and they did it as soldiers did, in good order and by bounds, with mortars walking ahead of them up the outer ward and onto the roof of the far gate-tower. Rusk's mortars answered them. One of the Thureos tanks stopped in the outer ward with its tracks blown off, burning. The other came on, and put its main gun through the arch of the far gate-tower, and then put four more rounds through the tower's upper windows, and the far gate-tower went quiet.

She had lost twenty-two soldiers in a little over two hours. She looked at the far tower, smoking, and at the Thureos sitting in the arch under it, and at the garrison cohorts forming up beyond it, and she thought, without any particular feeling, that she was going to lose the bridge.

"Back," she said. "Back to the near tower. Leave the barricade."

They went back across the bridge under fire, and lost four more on the span. She was the last across. As she reached the near gate a round from the Thureos's coaxial took the soldier next to her in the head and he went over the parapet and down into the gorge without a sound, and she didn't have time to see him hit.

The near gate-tower was older than the far one and smaller, a squat block of basalt at the foot of the Tabularium walls with a single arched gate onto the bridge and a gallery of firing slits above it. She put her heavy rifles in the gallery and the rest of her soldiers on the walls behind it and the stairs of the Tabularium, and they held the near end of the bridge for six more hours.

She would remember very little of those hours afterward. She would remember the bridge, which became, as the hours went on, so heaped with the dead of both sides that the garrison couldn't get the second Thureos across it, and had to send its soldiers over the bodies on foot. She would remember the thralls coming again, and then again, and the Sworn among them now, with ash on their foreheads and the Arsenal's best rifles, and the way they walked into the fire of the heavy rifles without hurrying. She would remember the smell. She would remember, near the end, Lictor Hollan, who was the detachment's armourer and had never fired a rifle in anger in his life, standing at the gate with a tool-hammer in each hand and refusing to move from it, until something went through him.

And she would remember the relay buzzing against her collarbone, over and over, every half hour, with a single line from Quarterdeck Nineteen.

*QD-19. Status.*

She sent back green every time. It was the only thing she could send.

---

At the end of the ninth hour, the Sworn came up through the floor.

There was a way up into the Tabularium from below, through the inner service ways and the old Hephaestean stair that came out in the cellars beside the pens. It was the way Vell had been going to come home. Rusk had put four soldiers on it at the start of the fight, and in the eighth hour she had taken two of them away to hold the gate, and now the Sworn came up the old stair and through the cellars in their hundreds, past the pens of the strays, who screamed and screamed, and up the inner stairs of the Tabularium from behind.

She heard them coming and knew what it was. She was on the gallery of the near gate-tower with nine soldiers left, and the gate below her was still holding, and the bridge in front of her was still heaped with the dead, and she turned with her rifle and saw the first of them come out of the stairwell at the end of the gallery, and shot him, and then there were too many of them.

They took her alive. She'd never know if that had been an order, or if she simply stopped being able to fight before they got around to killing her. She was on the floor of the gallery with her leg broken and her rifle gone and a garrison soldier kneeling on her chest, and through the slits beside her she could see the bridge, and the gorge, and the far gate-tower, where a man in a grey coat was walking slowly across the span toward her over the bodies of her soldiers, holding up a lamp.

They carried her down to him. He met them in the archway of the near gate, under the gallery, with the lamp in one hand. He looked at her for a while, with his head on one side, and his face was entirely calm.

"Lictor-Superior," he said. "Two hundred and nine reports. I've read all of them. You were right about everything, you know. Almost to the cycle." He lifted the lamp and looked at her more closely. "I'm sorry. I truly am. You would have been a very useful woman."

"Fuck you," Rusk said.

"Yes. I expect so." Dace set the lamp down on the stones of the bridge, and reached into his coat, and took out a vial. It was not the small grey glass one. It was larger, and dark, and it was stoppered with black wax, and when he broke the wax the smell that came out of it was nothing she had ever smelled in her life, and it went into her nose and down into her chest and her stomach and made her want to be sick, and made her, for the first time in nine hours, afraid.

"Hold her mouth," he said.

She fought them. It didn't help. A garrison soldier knelt on her shoulders and another held her jaw, and Dace knelt beside her and tipped the vial over her lips. Whatever was in it was thick and black, and it ran over her tongue and down her throat with the slow weight of something that had been at the bottom of a river for a very long time.

"There," he said, almost gently, and stood up, and wiped his fingers on his coat. "Let her go."

They let her go. Rusk lay on the stones of the bridge and stared up past the arch of the gate at the Echo Mast above the Tabularium, at its vanes turning slowly against the dark of the cavern roof. She could feel the implant at the base of her skull, as she always could, a small warm weight, and she reached for it as soldiers did when they knew it was nearly over, to make sure it would take her home.

It was going cold.

She felt it go. It was the most terrible thing she had ever felt in her life. The coin at the base of her skull went cold from the edges in, a little at a time, as the black stuff spread through her, and she tried to hold on to it, and it went on cooling, and it went out.

Dace stood over her, watching her face, with the lamp in his hand.

"Write it down," he said to somebody behind him, a scribe she couldn't see. "Lictor-Superior Rusk. Thirty-one minutes past the ninth hour. Watch her."

She died eleven minutes later, on the bridge, with her eyes open.

**THE VAT HALLS, CEILING DEPOT KAPPA. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 313 TO 322**

The first of them woke on the tenth cycle after the storming, in a vat at the depot at Landing Shelf Kappa, on the ceiling above the Flats, nine hundred metres up the Cinder Stair from the burning Arsenal.

His name was Lictor Gaius Fenn, and he had died with a garrison bayonet in his stomach at the barricade on the bridge in the second hour. He woke as all transfers woke, in a vat of warm golden nectar, with the taste of it in his mouth and nose and his new body heavy and clumsy and naked, and an orderly with a hook pulling him up toward the light, and he came up out of the vat fighting, trying to reach a rifle that wasn't there and screaming at the orderly that the bridge, the fucking bridge, they had to hold the fucking bridge.

The orderly had worked the vats for thirty years and was used to transfers who woke still fighting. She put him on the floor, and held him there, and waited until he stopped.

"You're at Kappa," she said. "You're at the ceiling. You're home."

He stared at her. Then he started to cry, and she let him do that too.

Over the next seven cycles, fifty-seven more of the Tabularium detachment came up out of the vats at Kappa. Fifty-eight in all. They were questioned one at a time by an Enforcement debriefing officer and a lector from Quarterdeck Nineteen, and every word they said was taken down and sent up to the Répertoire and logged. Fifty of them had died on the bridge or in the gate-towers. Four had died in the Tabularium itself, when the Sworn came up from the cellars. Two had died in the pens, defending the strays, which nobody had ordered them to do. Two had been taken alive and killed afterward. Their accounts agreed in every particular that mattered: the fall of the far gate-tower, the Thureos tanks, the ninth hour, the Sworn coming up from below.

One more thing was in all of their accounts, and the debriefing officer wrote it down fifty-eight times before she began to think about what it meant.

At the end, after the last of them had fallen, the Haruspex had gone among them on the bridge. Several of them had still been alive. He had walked from one to the next with a dark flask in his hand, and tipped it over the mouths of the dying, and watched them, and moved on. Lictor Fenn had seen it from the barricade where he lay with his stomach open, just before he died. Lictor Imre Kask had seen it from the gallery of the near tower. Both of them, separately, without having spoken to each other, described the Lictor-Superior being carried to the Haruspex alive on the bridge. Both of them described the flask.

Both of them had died a few minutes later. Neither of them had been given what was in it.

Rusk did not wake.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 322**

Cael Varros read the last of the fifty-eight debriefings at the end of the third watch, on the twenty-second cycle after the verdict, with a glass of dreadful ceiling brandy on the arm of his chair and Lector Pell asleep on the floor of his office under a borrowed greatcoat.

He had read every word. He had read them in the order the Lictors had died, and then again in the order they had woken. And then he had pulled the transfer log for the Tabularium detachment from the depot at Kappa and read that too, from top to bottom, all sixty lines.

Fifty-eight of the lines said *COMPLETE*. One said *VELL, TERTIA. STATUS UNKNOWN. PATTERN NOT RECEIVED*, which meant the Sworn still had her, or had done something to her that the relay could not see. Pell had cried a little when she found out about Vell. Varros hadn't, and wasn't sure why not.

The sixtieth line said:

*RUSK, AEMILIA, LICTOR-SUPERIOR. TRANSFER: NULL.*

He'd seen nulls before. Everyone who'd served in the theatre for any time had. Every so often a pattern came up the relay empty, a blank where a mind should have been, and the vat grew a body that would never open its eyes, and the Répertoire logged it as a null and put it down, sooner or later, to interference. Patterns came up through nine hundred metres of rock and three kilometres of the noosphere, and the deep was full of things that ate signals. Most of the theatre's nulls happened in the Lethe sectors, where the river took memory with it. A few happened at the Arsenal, where the casting lines and the Phlegethon threw up enough noise to drown out a choir.

He looked at Rusk's line for a long time.

Then he opened his query pane.

*CODE 23 – Inquisitio. Ref. TAB-PA-0312, debriefings 1 to 58. Query: cause of null, Rusk, A. Witnesses Fenn and Kask report a substance administered by the Haruspex Dace before death. Query: correlation of substance with null. C. Varros, Archmagister, QD-19.*

He sent it, and waited, and drank his brandy.

The answer came back in eleven seconds.

*Query received. Rusk, A.: null attributed to Phlegethon relay interference, Arsenal. Witness reports of substance classified CODE 18, Speculum Falsum, probable transfer shock. Query closed. Ave Imperator.*

Varros read it twice.

Transfer shock was a real thing. People who woke out of a death in the vats often remembered it wrong, or remembered things that hadn't happened, or remembered things that had happened to other people, and the debriefers learned to expect it. Fifty-eight soldiers who'd died in the same nine hours on the same bridge might easily have shared a few bad dreams. The Phlegethon threw up noise. The relays at the Arsenal were eight thousand years old. The explanation was cheap and it fitted, and he didn't believe a word of it.

He thought about querying again. He'd queried twice in one verdict already, which was twice more than most Archmagisters did in a career, and he had a long life's experience of what the Répertoire did with officers who argued with it, which was to file their arguments somewhere deep and stop reading them. He thought about Rusk, two hundred and nine reports at the bottom of a queue, cross-indexed and pending.

He opened a paper file instead. He kept a few. Nobody else on the Quarterdeck was allowed to touch them. He wrote *RUSK* on the front in his small neat old hand, and put the fifty-eight debriefings inside it, and the transfer log, and his two queries and their answers, and closed it, and put it on the shelf with the others.

"Sir?" Pell had woken up. She was looking at him from under the greatcoat. "What's that?"

"A list," Varros said. "Go back to sleep, Lector."

**THE ACROPOLIS, THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 313, THIRD BELL**

On the night of the three hundred and thirteenth cycle, Sabinus Orrhae stood on the steps of his palace above the burning eastern terrace and declared the Arsenal the Imperator's Hold.

He did it plainly, without music, with Dace a pace behind him and the forty Vestals on the steps below and the cracked lamp-medallion of the Hearth still hanging at his chest over his bandaged ribs. The thralls stood below him on the Stair in their tens of thousands, and the garrison in their ranks, and the Sworn, and he told them that the Arsenal would no longer answer to the College, which had abandoned the deep, nor to the Répertoire, which had priced it, nor to the Command, which had sent a killer to his altar. He told them it was the Lord Imperator's now, and his, and theirs.

He told the thralls that they were free.

He told them that their debts were forgiven, and their brands were nothing, and that no thrall-master of the Hephaestean orders would ever put a hand on them again. Then he named the garrison and the Sworn and every soul on the spur the Imperator's legion, and told them that the ceiling would come down the Stair against them, as surely as the fire had come up the flue, and that when it came, the deep would rise to defend him.

"He went down," the Archflamen said. "He's there now, below us, in the dark. The deep is where the Imperator went. When they come, it will remember him."

Nobody on Quarterdeck Nineteen heard the end of the sermon.

It was carried up the Echo Mast as far as the words "the deep is where", in a single burst of traffic on the Arsenal's ordinary band, and then the Mast went silent, in the middle of a sentence, and did not come back. The night watch on the Quarterdeck reported it to Varros at a quarter past the third bell. He came out of his office and stood behind the relay operator and looked at the dead band on her pane for a while, and asked her to check the relay, and the vanes, and the power, which she did. All of it was working. The Mast was intact, still drawing power from the casting lines and turning its vanes in the heat of the cone, and it had stopped talking to them.

"Log it," Varros said. "Echo Mast, Pyriphlegethon Arsenal, silent from three bell plus fifteen. And keep listening."

---

The Répertoire's next verdict for Tartarus came up the queue an hour later. Varros read it standing behind the relay operator's chair, and then went back to his office and sat in the dark without turning on the panes.

It went out on every band, to every battery in the theatre, in the same unhurried amber as everything else.

*SECTOR 72-A, DEPTH 4. AMMUNITION ALLOCATION CONFLICT. VERDICT: REDIRECT ALL 7.5MM SLUGS TO DEPTHS 88B TO 99C. LETHE SECTORS TO DEFAULT TO PLASMA INCINERATION PACKAGES. PRIORITIZE DENSITY OVER KINETIC ACCURACY.*

*DIVINE MORTAR BATTERIES, ALL SECTORS: ALLOCATION REVISED. PRIMARY SECTORS, ELEVEN KEYED ROUNDS PER CYCLE. SECONDARY SECTORS, FOUR. AVE IMPERATOR.*

In the Lethe holds, three hundred kilometres north-east of the Arsenal, Commander Thaleia read it on the bottom pane of her overlay in the command chair of Sector 74B, with Ilon beside her eating a ration bar, and asked him to read it to her again, slowly, to make sure.

"Four," Ilon said. "Per cycle. Per battery." He put the ration bar down. "Ma'am, the fog came in at the second bell, and we fired forty-one last night."

"I know what we fired last night." She looked at the verdict for a moment longer, and then she pulled up the sector map, and the second positions, which had been dug four rotations ago and never manned, and began, without saying anything else, to plan the withdrawal.
