## Chapter 27: The Crescent

*"Look, she comes up out of the deep with her keys. The old warden of the pit, who kept the Hundred-Handed for the Titans, and kept them badly, and was paid for it. Fifty heads of beasts go around her waist and they all speak. Her tail has a sting on it the length of a lance, and where it touches the ground the ground forgets it was ever stone."*

*Sister-Augur Ione Kestrel of the Hunt, a reading on the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal. Sealed in the Hunt's archive on Luna by order of the Director*

**LUNA, THE AUGURY BENEATH THE FOURTH MOONWALL. ROTATION FIFTEEN, CYCLE 180**

Sister-Augur Ione Kestrel had taken readings for the Hunt of the Director for two hundred and eleven years, and in all that time she had never once seen every probable future she searched come out the same.

She sat in the augury beneath the Fourth Moonwall on the hundred and eightieth cycle of the Tartarean theatre's fifteenth rotation, cross-legged on the cold silver floor of the reading chamber with the bowl of moon-water in front of her and the lamps out, and looked into it for the ninth time in nine hours, and saw it again.

The Arsenal. The Cinder Flats at Depth 40, brown and red under the ash. The cone, and the Acropolis on top of it, and the Mast. And under all of it, under the spur and the shelf and the long dark descents of the Phlegethon, a door.

She had never seen a door in a reading before. Readings didn't show things that plainly. They showed shapes and shadows and probabilities, braided and unbraided, branching and rejoining, and an augur learned over a lifetime to read the braid and say which way it leaned. It always leaned. Some futures were likelier than others. None was ever certain. That was the first thing every sister-augur of the Hunt was taught, at the bowl, at eleven years old: *the water never says always.*

The water was saying always.

She searched another branch. She went back along the braid to the place where the Third had taken the crater at Leaf 57-44, eleven cycles before, and followed it forward along a future where the Third pressed on to the cone at once, and saw the door. She went back again and followed a future where the Third halted and dug in, and saw the door. A future where the Directorate's cuts starved the Third into a long slow stalemate. A future where the Archflamen died in his rooms in the Acropolis tomorrow. A future where the Fleet broke the Mast. A future where the Third's twenty million soldiers simply walked away from the Flats, all of them, back up the cables. Every branch of the braid, every future she could find, ran down to the same place in the end.

The door, under the Arsenal, opening from below.

And coming up through it, the two of them.

She'd seen them in the first hour, and she'd looked away, and she'd spent eight hours since searching every branch of the braid for a future where they weren't there. She hadn't found one. She made herself look at them now, in the water. The warden first. The old warden of the pit, who had kept the Hundred-Handed for the Titans in the deepest prison of Tartarus long before the gods were born, and kept them badly, and been paid for it with a thunderbolt. Kestrel knew her name. Every sister-augur of the Hunt knew it, from the old lists, and had been taught never to say it at the bowl. She came up out of the deep in the water with her keys, vast and dark and terrible, with fifty heads of beasts round her waist, wolves and boars and bears and lions and things that had no names, all of them speaking at once. Her tail was a scorpion's. The sting on the end of it was the length of a lance, and where it touched the ground, in the water, the ground forgot that it had ever been stone.

Behind her came the mother.

Kestrel looked at the mother for a long time. Then she put both her hands flat on the floor of the reading chamber on either side of the bowl and closed her eyes, and said the reading aloud, as an augur must, to the scribe who sat in the dark behind her with her stylus and her slate.

"Look," she said, "she comes up out of the deep with her keys."

---

The Director read it on the hundred and eighty-first cycle.

Kestrel was summoned to the Director's audience chamber on the Sixth Moonwall to answer for it, and stood in front of the Director for an hour while the Director read the reading through three times, slowly, in silence, with the scribe's slate in one silver hand. Kestrel had stood in front of the Director perhaps a dozen times in two hundred and eleven years. She had never once grown used to it. Nobody in the Hunt ever did.

At the end of the hour the Director put down the slate.

"Every branch," she said.

"Every branch, Director. I searched for nine hours. I'll search for ninety, if you order it. I don't believe I'll find another."

"No," the Director said. "Neither do I."

She looked at Kestrel for a long moment with her silver eyes, and Kestrel felt the look go through her and out the other side.

"Seal it," the Director said. "In the archive. My seal. Nobody reads it without my leave." She paused. "Except the Lieutenant. When I've decided what to do with her."

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION FIFTEEN, CYCLE 197**

Kastellan was busy with the supply calculations for the breach when the packet came, and he nearly didn't open it.

The Ninth needed shells on the far side of the Wall, and fuel, and replacements, and everything it needed had to come across no-man's land through the eleven-hundred-metre gap at Leaf 57-44 under the Archflamen's guns, and the Directorate's cuts had come into force on the hundred and sixtieth cycle. He had twelve shells per gun per watch and a battle group inside the Cinder Wall. He'd been at the figures with Dray since the first bell. His decision to bring in the Achillean Order had succeeded. The Third could see its last objective in front of it for the first time in fifteen rotations. The crisis that had begun on the Demus Rim, nine rotations ago, had been contained.

He didn't want to read anything that wasn't a shell figure.

But it was sealed with a crescent, silver, on black wax. He had never seen the seal in his life. He'd heard of it. Everyone in the Empire had heard of it. And Dray, who had brought it in, stood beside the desk without moving until he opened it.

It was a single sheet. He read it twice.

*From the Director of Anti-Espionage and Celestial Surveillance, to the Marshal Commanding the Third Siege Army at the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal.*

*The Hunt has taken readings on the Tartarean theatre. Every probable future my sister-augurs have searched shows the same thing. The Arsenal is about to break open from below.*

*The Hunt has long known that the Archflamen's soldiers pray to the deep rivers, and that the Legates of those rivers have come up to fight beside him. The readings now show that the Archflamen's people will soon be offering themselves to the things below as hosts, and calling more of them up. If they succeed, the Arsenal may not be recoverable at all. The deep geometry beneath Depth 40 is already restless. The calling-up is nearly complete.*

*I hold the Tartarean containment lines jointly with the House of Hades, and have done so for longer than the Third has existed. Under that charge, I will bring a motion before the Conclave at Watchpoint Hades to take the Third Siege Army under my seal.*

*You will forgive me for not asking first. There isn't time.*

There was no signature. There was only the crescent.

Kastellan read it a third time. Then he folded it, and put it in the drawer of his desk, on top of Corvis's list of the *54th* and Castorre's two letters and the letter from Stephanos Pyrrhus asking about his daughter, and closed the drawer.

"Marshal?" Dray said.

"The shell figures, Dray," Kastellan said. "Where were we? The Ninth's forward dump at the gap."

Dray looked at him for a moment. Then he picked up his slate.

**THE NORTHERN FLATS, LEAF 50-29. ROTATION FIFTEEN, CYCLE 199, DARK WATCH**

On the Flats the signs were plain to anyone who could read them, and nobody there could.

The Wrath crews over the cone reported a light deep in the river cloud beneath the Phlegethon, red and slow, a second river burning under the first. Halloran saw it, flying top cover for a Vigil on the hundred and ninety-eighth cycle, and reported it, and the report went to Quarterdeck Nineteen and was filed. The storms of the ceiling geometry flickered without pause, day and night, in long silent sheets of pale light that lit the underside of the ash cloud from end to end. The Echo Mast had begun to hum on frequencies the Third's signallers couldn't filter. The hum came through the bands in every bunker on the Flats now, all the time, under every signal, a long low droning note that made the signallers' teeth ache, and Tribune Cassia Wren of the Signals Directorate wrote a report on it that ran to thirty leaves and concluded that she didn't know what it was.

And on the northern front, the sentries saw soldiers walking.

Trooper Oriel Tansy of the *29th* Trench Regiment was on sentry at the fourth hour of the dark watch on the hundred and ninety-ninth cycle of Rotation Fifteen, on the fire-step of the *2nd* Cohort's forward bay on Leaf 50-29, with his rifle and his periscope and his steel helmet, and he saw them first.

He was twenty-two now. He'd been on the Flats for six rotations. He'd been up four times, through the vats, and come back each time, and Jory Cade had told him after the fourth that he'd do, which from Cade was a speech. He'd seen most things the Flats had to show. He'd seen the Behemoths walking. He'd seen the drowned land. He'd carried a dead breacher of the thirty-one back across no-man's land in his arms, on the first cycle of the Kastellan Offensive, one of the thirty-one that Cade had cut down from the wire.

Nothing on the Flats had prepared him for this.

They came out of the mist in no-man's land, in front of the *29th*'s wire, and walked past it. Soldiers. A dozen of them, perhaps more; it was hard to count in the mist and the dark. In the Third's grey field uniforms, filthy and torn, with their packs on their backs and their rifles slung. Bareheaded. None of them wore helmets. They walked through no-man's land in a long loose straggling line, not in step, not in order, slowly, steadily, toward the Cinder Wall, eleven kilometres away across the ash in the dark, and they didn't look to left or right, and they didn't look at the *29th*'s wire as they passed it, or at the trench, or at him.

"Halt," Tansy said. It came out as a whisper. He tried again, louder. "Halt! Who goes there? Halt!"

None of them halted. None of them turned their heads.

He saw one of them clearly, for a moment, as the line passed closest to the wire, thirty metres away. A young man, thin and fair, with a round soft face and a ration clerk's stoop in his shoulders, walking with his rifle slung and his empty hands at his sides and his face turned toward the Wall, with no expression on it at all.

Tansy stared at him.

"Davin?" he said.

The young man walked on. He didn't turn his head.

Tansy's cousin Davin had been a ration clerk of the Lethe Hold, round-faced and fair and gentle, who'd gone down to the Flats with the *54th* Trench Regiment in Rotation Eight and been listed absent on the Lethe Cut in Rotation Nine. Nobody had ever been able to tell Tansy's aunt what absent meant. Tansy had stopped asking, after a while.

"Davin!" he shouted. "Davin, it's me! It's Oriel!"

The line of bareheaded soldiers walked on into the mist toward the Cinder Wall, and the mist closed behind them, and they were gone.

Tansy stood on the fire-step with his rifle in his hands and his whole body shaking. Then he jumped down off it and ran along the bay to the dugout, and woke Cade.

Cade came out with him to the fire-step, in his shirt, with his rifle, and stood beside him at the parapet for a long time, looking out into the mist where Tansy pointed. There was nothing there. The mist lay on no-man's land, grey and still, and the ash, and the wire, and beyond them in the dark the faint red glow of the cone, eleven kilometres off, and nothing moved.

"They were there," Tansy said. "Jory. They were there. A dozen of them. In our grey. No helmets. Walking to the Wall. I challenged them three times." He swallowed. "One of them was my cousin. Davin. From the *54th*."

Cade looked at him.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

Cade was quiet for a while.

"Put it in the log," he said at last. "All of it. What you saw, and when, and how many, and which way they were walking. And your cousin's name." He paused. "And then go and sit in the dugout and drink something hot. I'll take the rest of your watch."

Tansy put it in the log. It went up to the *29th*'s command bunker at the end of the watch, and from there to the *7th* Line Phalanx, and from there to Quarterdeck Nineteen, where it was filed beside four other reports of the same kind from four other regiments of the northern front that cycle, and nineteen more from the cycles before it. The sentries' reports agreed with one another in every particular. Soldiers in the Third's grey. Bareheaded. Walking through no-man's land toward the Wall in the dark watch. Answering no challenge. Gone when the sentries looked again.

Little notice was taken of any of it. The staff on Quarterdeck Nineteen had a battle group inside the Cinder Wall to supply on twelve shells per gun per watch.

The Third would soon face a new enemy on the Flats.

**LUNA, THE LONG GALLERY BENEATH THE SIXTH MOONWALL. ROTATION FIFTEEN, CYCLE 199**

On the same cycle, a quarter of a world away, the Director received Lieutenant Thalia Grace of the Hunt in the long gallery beneath the Sixth Moonwall on Luna.

No record of the meeting was ever entered into AtRep.

Thalia had been summoned at the end of the dark watch, without warning, by a runner from the Director's household, and she'd come straight from the training floor of the Fourth Moonwall in her sweat-soaked under-armour with her hair sticking up in spikes and the capacitors on her bracers still crackling from the drills. The runner had said *now*, and when the Director's household said *now* it didn't mean after a shower. She walked the whole length of the long gallery under the Sixth Moonwall to the far end, where the Director stood at the great window looking out over the grey curve of Luna toward the blue-white crescent of the world, and stopped five paces behind her, and waited.

The long gallery was a kilometre long and lined from end to end with silver statues of the Hunt's dead. Every Hunter who had fallen in the Director's service since the Hunt began. Thalia had walked past every one of them to get here. She always counted. She'd lost count somewhere past the eleven thousandth, as she always did, and stopped, and walked the rest of the way trying not to look at them.

The Director didn't turn round.

"Lieutenant," she said.

"Lady."

"Sit down."

There was a bench under the window. Thalia sat on it. The Director stayed standing, with her back to her, looking at the world.

"I'm bringing a motion before the Conclave at Watchpoint Hades in four cycles," the Director said. "To take the Third Siege Army under my seal. Hunt-Elder Sarn will speak in support. You'll present it." She paused. "When it passes, you will take command of the Third at the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal, with your own contingent under you. And you'll hold it until the Arsenal is closed."

Thalia stared at the Director's back.

She'd known something was coming. The whole of the Fourth Moonwall had known for a week that something was coming: the Director's household moving at all hours, the sister-augurs going in and out of the Sixth Moonwall with their faces closed, a fast moon-ship loading at the docks under seal. She'd thought it would be the Cocytus. Or Depth 12. Or, if her luck was truly rotten, the Acheron stairs, where something had come up out of the deep that rotation and the Fleet was still trying to put it back. She'd thought, at worst, that she'd be sent with a company of shieldmaidens to hold a stretch of the containment lines somewhere cold and miserable.

She had not thought this.

"Lady," she said. "With respect. I'm not a politician."

"I know."

"I'm a Hunter. I'm a Lieutenant of the Hunt. I lead a contingent of six and two Hounds. I've never commanded anything larger than a Moonwall company in my life." She heard her own voice rising and couldn't stop it. "The Third is twenty million soldiers, Lady. In its fifteenth rotation of siege. It's got a Marshal. It's got Phalanx-Generals and colonels and a staff on Quarterdeck Nineteen that's been fighting that siege since before I was born. A siege that size needs a Marshal. It doesn't need me."

"The Third has a Marshal," the Director said. "It needs the crescent."

Thalia was silent.

The Director turned round at last, and looked at her.

Thalia had served the Director for longer than most of the Hunt had been alive. She had never once been able to hold that look for long. She held it now, for as long as she could, which was about four seconds, and then looked at the floor.

"Lady," she said, to the floor. "Is this because of the Needle?"

"Yes."

Thalia closed her eyes.

The Themis Needle. She'd known it would be. She'd been waiting for it for most of a rotation, since the cycle she'd come back from the Needle with her contingent and nothing to show for it, and stood in front of the Director in the audience chamber on the Sixth Moonwall and reported that she'd failed. She'd come within a metre of him on the Needle. The boy carrying Percy Jackson's signature. Within a metre, with her hand out, close enough to take him. And she had frozen.

She had never frozen before, not once in all her years with the Hunt.

"I froze," she said. "On the Needle."

"Yes."

"I've never frozen before, Lady."

"I know," the Director said. "That's the trouble, Thalia. Neither of us knows why you did. And both of us need to know whether you'll do it again." She came and stood in front of the bench, and Thalia looked up at her. "Tartarus will tell us."

Thalia looked at her for a long time.

"It's a punishment," she said.

"Call it what you like."

"Everyone in the Hunt will call it a punishment, Lady."

"Then everyone in the Hunt will be right." The Director's voice didn't change. "You will go down to the Arsenal. You will take the Third. You'll close the door the Hunt has read beneath the Arsenal, before what's behind it comes up. And you'll find out, on the Flats, whether you're still my Lieutenant." She paused. "I'd like you to be."

Thalia sat on the bench and said nothing.

The Director turned back to the window, and looked out at the world for a while longer. Thalia thought the audience was over. She stood up.

"Thalia," the Director said, without turning.

"Lady?"

"There are no heights in Tartarus."

Thalia stood motionless for a moment. Then she bowed, and turned, and walked back down the long gallery under the Sixth Moonwall, past the silver statues of the Hunt's dead, and this time she didn't count them.

The Hunt has always regarded the appointment as a punishment, and so did Thalia Grace. But everyone in the Hunt who heard what the Director said to her at the end of it understood exactly what it meant. Thalia's fear of heights was the worst-kept secret on Luna.

**WATCHPOINT HADES, THE OLD AUDIENCE HALL. ROTATION FIFTEEN, CYCLE 203**

The Conclave of the Tartarean theatre sat in full session in the old audience hall at Watchpoint Hades on the two hundred and third cycle, with a long list of business in front of it. It ran from the removal of a dozen hold quartermasters for theft to the trial of a Haruspex of the Lethe Hold for heresy, and first on the list was the Hunt's motion.

Thalia had never been inside the old audience hall. She stood in the antechamber behind the Hunt's delegation with the Director's seal on her left pauldron, in full armour, with the capacitors on her bracers damped down so they wouldn't spark in front of the College, and looked through the high bronze doors at the hall, and thought that it was the most depressing room she had ever seen.

It was old and vast and dark. A great round vault of black basalt cut into the rock of Watchpoint Hades in the theatre's first century, with tiers of stone benches rising round its walls toward a dome lost in shadow, and a floor of black glass at its centre, and in the floor, inlaid in silver, the oath. Every soldier of the Third swore it at the vats, the first time they went in. *To hold the line. To keep the door. To come back up.* The words ran round the edge of the floor in a ring of letters a metre high, worn smooth by the boots of every delegate who had ever crossed it.

Hunt-Elder Melaina Sarn stood in front of Thalia in the antechamber, leaning on her staff.

She was older than anyone else in the hall would be except the Lord Marshal, and more silver alloy than flesh. She'd lost both legs, an arm and most of her face in the theatre's first Grand Offensive, so long ago that the Répertoire's records of it were kept in a vault of their own, and fought on, and gone on fighting, in the Hunt's service, on every front of Tartarus since. Her legs were silver now, and her left arm, and the left side of her face was a smooth silver mask with a single moonstone eye set in it that glowed faintly in the dark of the antechamber. The right side was old and deeply lined and calm.

"Lieutenant," she said, without turning round.

"Elder."

"You're frightened."

Thalia considered lying. She'd never managed to lie to Sarn. Nobody in the Hunt ever had.

"Yes," she said.

"Good. So am I." Sarn shifted her weight on the staff. "The College has nearly half the votes, with Enforcement. They'll vote against us to a member, and they'll do it shouting. The rest are split. We've sounded them out, and they're split down the middle, and they'll stay split until somebody makes them choose." She turned her head slightly, and the moonstone eye caught the light. "That's you."

"Elder, I don't..."

"You'll speak for two minutes," Sarn said. "No longer. You'll tell them the truth, and then you'll stop. Don't argue with the College. I'll argue with the College. I've been arguing with the College since before most of its Houses were founded, and I enjoy it." She faced the doors again. "Here we go."

The Conclave entered to the oath.

The Lord Marshal of the Tartarean Theatre came first, an old, bent, white-haired man in black, walking slowly across the floor of black glass over the silver letters to the high seat at the far side of the hall, and the hall rose for him. Then the delegations, one after another, to their tiers: the Pontifical College in white and gold, forty of them, led by a tall lean silver-haired Flamen with a hard handsome face whom Thalia didn't know. Enforcement in black, behind them. The Dionysian Logistics Directorate in grey, under a small severe dark-haired woman with a slate under her arm whom Thalia recognised from the file: Logothete Irene Vasska. The Forge, in bronze. The Pyrrhus Command, in red. The House of Hades, in grey and black, with the twin-headed staff.

The Hunt entered last.

Sarn went first, across the floor of black glass, her silver legs ringing on it with every step and her staff striking it between them, slowly, upright. Thalia followed her with the Director's seal on her pauldron, and felt every eye in the hall on her, and walked across the silver letters of the oath to the Hunt's tier and sat down.

---

The College spoke first. It always did.

Thalia listened to them for an hour, and then another, and learned that the hard-faced silver-haired Flamen was named Aurelian Cato, and that he was the College's senior delegate to the Conclave, and that his voice was warm oil. The College had sought command of the war since the first rotation, he said. The Archflamen of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal was a Flamen of the College, confirmed by the College and preaching in its name. He had spread his heresy among twenty-seven million soldiers of the Third under the College's seal. He was the College's apostate. He was the College's to judge. The College would not see its apostate taken from it by an office of surveillance whose remit was the moon.

The Enforcement delegates sided with the College. They usually did. The College would let Enforcement run the trials.

Then the reports from the Flats were read in, by a clerk of the Répertoire, in a flat tired voice, for an hour. The siege was in its fifteenth rotation. By the Répertoire's count the Third had transferred more than forty million soldiers on the Cinder Flats, and close to two million had come back blank or not at all. Every metre of the Flats had been bought at a price the Répertoire could state to the gram of slurry. The clerk stated it. Nobody in the hall moved while he did.

Then Sister-Augur Kestrel was examined.

They examined her for three hours. The College's delegates went at her one after another, standing at the front of their tier and putting their questions across the floor to her where she sat alone on a stool in the middle of the black glass, inside the silver ring of the oath, with her hands folded in her lap. How many branches had she searched? How many hours? Had she searched every branch? How could she know she'd searched every branch? Wasn't it true that the water never said always? Wasn't it the first thing every sister-augur of the Hunt was taught? Then how could she sit there and tell the Conclave that it said always now?

"I don't know how," Kestrel said, every time. Her voice was quiet and tired and quite steady. "I only know that it does."

At the end of the third hour, the Lord Marshal called Thalia.

---

She went down from the Hunt's tier onto the floor of black glass and stood in the centre of the ring of silver letters, beside the empty stool where Kestrel had sat, and looked up at the tiers of the Conclave rising round her into the dark, all those faces, and felt her throat close.

*Two minutes*, Sarn had said. *The truth. Then stop.*

"Something's coming up beneath the Arsenal," Thalia said.

Her voice came out louder than she'd expected, and flatter, and it carried. The hall had been built to carry voices.

"The Hunt's augurs have read it. You've just heard one of them for three hours. You've had her reading for two cycles, all of you, and you've had the reports from the Flats, and you know what the Legates of the deep rivers are and you know they've been on the Flats since Rotation Eight." She looked up at the College's tier. "You can pass the motion. You can give the Third to the Director, and let the Hunt go down and close the door before what's behind it comes up. Or you can send it for another review, and sit here and argue over whose apostate the Archflamen is."

She paused.

"And if you do that," she said, "there won't be anyone left in this hall to judge anybody."

The hall erupted.

She'd expected it. Sarn had told her to expect it. She hadn't expected how loud it would be. The whole of the College's tier was on its feet at once, forty Flamens in white and gold, shouting, and Enforcement behind them, and Aurelian Cato at the front of them with his hard handsome face gone red, pointing at her across the floor of the hall and shouting that the Hunt was threatening the Conclave, that the Hunt was threatening the College, that it was an outrage, that it would be entered in the record, that it would be answered on the ceiling. The Lord Marshal was calling for order. Nobody was listening to him.

Thalia stood in the middle of the ring of the oath and didn't move.

*Then stop*, Sarn had said.

She stopped. She turned round and walked back across the floor of black glass to the Hunt's tier, and sat down beside Sarn, and folded her hands in her lap so nobody would see that they were shaking.

"Good," Sarn said quietly. "That was good."

"They hate me."

"They hated you before you opened your mouth, Lieutenant. Now they're frightened of you as well. That's better." Sarn rose, slowly, on her silver legs, leaning on her staff. "My turn."

---

The debate lasted most of the cycle. Twice the Lord Marshal had to call for order.

The second time was after Sarn told Aurelian Cato, in a voice that didn't rise at all and carried to every corner of the hall, that she had held the door of Tartarus since before his House was founded, and that she would not hand it to a man who had confirmed the Archflamen of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal in his office with his own seal.

Cato went white. The College's tier went silent. And in the silence, from somewhere in the tiers of the Forge, somebody laughed.

The vote came at the end of the cycle. It was as close as everyone had expected.

The College voted against the motion to a member, and Enforcement with it. The Forge voted for it. The House of Hades voted for it. The Pyrrhus Command voted for it, after a long whispered conference among its delegates in red, and Thalia saw one of them look across the hall at her with an expression she couldn't read. And it came down to the Directorate.

Logothete Irene Vasska stood up at the front of the Directorate's tier, small and severe and grey, with her slate under her arm, and looked across the hall at the Hunt for a long moment.

"The Directorate abstains," she said.

Three of her delegation abstained with her.

The motion passed by two votes.

---

Vasska found Thalia afterward, in the antechamber behind the Hunt's tier, while the Conclave was emptying and the College's delegates were still shouting in the corridors outside.

Thalia didn't know her. She knew the file. Logothete of the Dionysian Logistics Directorate, who had come down to the Flats in Rotation Nine and written a report of four hundred and six leaves on the conduct of the siege that had ended Lord-General Castorre's command. Who had argued then for the removal of the Third's command and been overruled. Who had gone back up the cables afterward, and not been heard of again by the Third, until today.

"Lieutenant," Vasska said.

"Logothete." Thalia hesitated. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I didn't vote for you." Vasska's voice was dry and precise. "I abstained. It's a different thing, and I'd like you to understand the difference. I didn't do it for the Hunt. I don't know the Hunt and I don't care about its readings. I did it for the Third." She looked at Thalia. "Somebody has to finish it. Somebody has to finish that siege, Lieutenant, and I've spent nine rotations watching people fail to. I want the somebody to be able to count."

"I can count," Thalia said.

"We'll see." Vasska took the slate from under her arm and held it out. "Here. Begin with the shells."

Thalia took it. It was a list. Every shell dump on the Cinder Flats, every gun line, every Ordnance Phalanx, with the stocks of each on the two hundredth cycle of Rotation Fifteen, and the Directorate's new allocation beside them, and in the margin, in a small precise hand, notes. *Short. Short. Stockpiling against orders, leave it. Short. Lying.*

She looked up from it. Vasska was already walking away.

---

The consequences of the vote were felt on the ceiling worlds before they reached the Flats.

The College's delegation protested the result to Olympia Sacra within the cycle, as the seizure of a war of faith by an office of surveillance. And the College let it be known that its temple legions, held back since Rotation Nine against a share in the command, would not now be sent to the Flats on any terms. Enforcement withdrew its Lictor-Superior from Quarterdeck Nineteen, though it left his Lictors in the regimental bunkers. The Directorate made no protest. Vasska's abstention had been her own decision, taken without instruction from the Directorate's masters, and the Directorate's masters let it stand. It relieved them of a siege they'd already decided to starve.

The Director's order went out the same cycle.

It placed the Third Siege Army under the Hunt of the Director, with Thalia Grace, Lieutenant of the Hunt, in command on the Flats. With her would go her own contingent: Atlanta, Celyn, Naomi, Josephine, Hemithea and Phoebe, and the Hounds Columbina and Scaramucius. With them would go thirty-eight more Hunters of the Director's household, each with a small retinue, who would be placed in the command bunker of every regiment on the Flats, to observe, and, when the time came, to lead from the front. Each of them could call on the Director's other weapons. The Psiloi, the Director's oath-sworn marksmen, who had fought in every war of the Hunt and could put a round through a Laistrygon's eye at two kilometres, and whose squads would guard the Hunters in the regimental bunkers. Enforcement's assassins, whom nobody liked to call on. And the flame hunters and shieldmaidens of the Sixth Moonwall, the oldest fighting companies of the Hunt, trained for nothing in their lives but fighting the things that came out of the pit.

The plan had been made long before the vote. The fast moon-ship *Selene's Arrow* was already at the docks on Luna, loading the Sixth Moonwall's best companies for the Flats, under orders to place themselves at the Lieutenant's disposal.

Thalia sent envoys of her own, that night, before she left Watchpoint Hades. Naomi went up to the Achillean Order's Hearth on the ceiling to ask the Order to come back in strength and finish what it had begun. It was a request. The Order took no orders. A request also went to the Cohort of Nemesis at Rhamnous Primaris, which had fought on the docks of the Phlegethon in Rotation Seven and gone home. Thalia had little hope of that one. The Cohort answered to Nemesis alone, and Nemesis kept her own accounts.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION FIFTEEN, CYCLE 205**

Kastellan read the Director's order on the two hundred and fifth cycle, standing at his desk, and when he'd read it he sat down.

It required him to give up command of the Third at once. He could take a post under the Lieutenant, or resign pending a new appointment, without blame or charge. The Directorate would review his conduct of the siege in the usual way.

At the bottom, in a different hand, small and quick and slightly untidy, somebody had added a line.

*In the Hunt's judgement you've done well, Marshal. You saved the Third from defeat, broke the inner belt and opened the Wall. Nobody could have asked much more. T. G.*

He read that line several times.

"Dray," he said.

Dray was standing by the door. He'd brought the order in himself. He'd read it on the way, Kastellan suspected, and Kastellan didn't blame him.

"Marshal."

"Is there a regiment on the Flats without a colonel?"

Dray looked at him. Then he understood, and his face changed.

"Marshal..."

"I've fought this siege from this desk for six rotations, Dray. I've never once seen the Wall from the ground. Not up close. Not from a fire-step." Kastellan looked at the map of the nulls on the wall, the clean ring round the Acropolis and the pale wash in the south. "I'd like to see it. Before the end. Find me a regiment. Or a cohort, if there's no regiment."

Dray was silent for a long time.

"The *35th*," he said at last. "It's been without a colonel since Osk went null in the crater, Marshal. The Command never found anyone to replace him."

Kastellan nodded slowly.

"Ask the Lieutenant," he said, "if she'll give it to me."

She gave it to him the same cycle, by signal, from Watchpoint Hades. The signal said, in full: *Yes. Thank you. T. G.*

---

He cleared his desk on the two hundred and sixth cycle.

There wasn't much in it. He'd never kept much. The returns went to Dray for the Lieutenant's staff. The map of the nulls stayed on the wall. The lump of slag that Castorre had used as a paperweight he left on the desk, where he'd found it six rotations before. And from the drawer he took the things he'd kept there: Corvis's list of the *54th*, fourteen thousand one hundred names in a small neat hand; the letter from the Archflamen and the letter from Callix, that Castorre had given him at the handover; the letter under the Pyrrhus seal from Stephanos Pyrrhus, asking about his daughter; and the Director's packet with the crescent on it.

He looked at Stephanos Pyrrhus's letter for a long time. *Find out, if you can, what became of my daughter.* He'd tried. For six rotations he'd tried. The Répertoire had nothing. The Fleet had nothing. The ships that had gone into Segment Twelve after her had been struck from the books, and the books were closed. He'd written to Stephanos Pyrrhus four times to say so.

He put the letters in the inside pocket of his tunic, all of them. Then he put on his greatcoat, and picked up his helmet, and went down the cables to the Flats.

A dozen of his staff officers went with him. He hadn't asked them to. Dray wasn't one of them. Dray stayed on Quarterdeck Nineteen, as the Lieutenant's chief of staff, at Kastellan's own request.

"Somebody's got to tell her the price, Dray," Kastellan said to him, at the cable head, at the end. "She'll want to know it. Tell her."

"And then stop, Marshal?"

Kastellan almost smiled.

"No," he said. "Tell her it, and keep telling her. Until she makes you stop."

Abel Strand was on the fire-step of the *35th*'s forward trench on Leaf 57-44 when the new colonel came down the communication trench on foot, on the two hundred and eighth cycle, in a plain field greatcoat without a Marshal's insignia, with a dozen grey-faced staff officers trailing behind him, and stopped in the bay, and looked up at him.

"Centurion," the new colonel said.

"Colonel."

"I'm Kastellan. I've got the *35th*." He looked at Strand's steel leg. "You're Strand. *19th* Cohort. Nine hours behind the blocks on the first assault."

Strand looked at him in astonishment.

"Yes, Colonel."

"I read the report. The reclaimer who found you wrote it." Kastellan climbed up onto the fire-step beside him, slowly, an old man in a greatcoat, and stood there and looked over the parapet at the crater, and at the gap in the Wall beyond it, and at the cone. He stood there a long time. "Well," he said at last. "There it is."

"Colonel?"

"The Wall," Kastellan said. "I've been looking at it on a map for six rotations, Centurion. It's bigger than I thought." He turned to Strand. "Show me round, would you? I'd like to meet the regiment."

Strand showed him round. Gault came up from the firing station, humming, and met him in the third bay of the *2nd* Cohort, and the two of them stood together on the fire-step for most of an hour looking at the crater, not saying much. Kastellan met every centurion of the *35th* that cycle, and most of the optios. He slept that night in the regimental command dugout on a camp bed, in his greatcoat, with the letters in his pocket.

**LANDING SHELF KAPPA. ROTATION FIFTEEN, CYCLE 241**

On the two hundred and forty-first cycle, Thalia Grace came down the cable to Landing Shelf Kappa in a supply car, with her contingent and the two Hounds and forty tonnes of shells.

It was a descent of nine minutes. She made it with her eyes shut.

She sat in the corner of the supply car on a crate of shells with her back against the wall and her eyes squeezed shut and her hands gripping the edge of the crate, and listened to the cable hum, and felt the car sway, and didn't look out of the window once. Atlanta sat beside her and talked the whole way down about nothing at all, the food on the *Selene's Arrow* and the state of the Hounds' coats and whether Josephine had really bet Hemithea a month's ration of honey that the Third's soldiers would salute them, and Thalia had never in her life been so grateful to anyone for talking nonsense. Columbina lay across her boots, heavy and warm. Scaramucius had his head on her knee.

"There are no heights in Tartarus," Atlanta said, at the fifth minute, quite seriously.

Thalia opened one eye.

"I'm going to kill her," she said. "When I get back. I'm going to kill the Director."

"No you're not."

"No," Thalia agreed, and shut the eye again. "But I'm going to think about it."

The car settled into its cradle on the shelf at Kappa with a long scream of brakes and a jolt, and the doors opened, and Thalia opened her eyes and stood up and walked out of it onto the shelf, and looked at the Flats.

From Kappa, the cone of the Acropolis stood twenty kilometres away. She could see the Echo Mast on its summit, a thin black needle against the red river cloud behind it, and she could hear it, faintly, even here: a long low droning hum that seemed to come up out of the ground and the air and the inside of her own head all at once. Between the shelf and the cone the trenches of fifteen rotations crossed the ash from one horizon to the other, line after line, belt after belt, broken and rebuilt and broken again, grey and brown and black under the ash, and in them, she knew, twenty million soldiers of the Third, waiting.

She looked at it for a long time.

A signals officer of the Third came across the shelf toward the Hunt, a thin young man with a signaller's flash on his collar and a slate, and saluted uncertainly, unsure whether he was meant to.

Josephine nudged Hemithea. Hemithea sighed.

"Lieutenant," the signals officer said. "With the compliments of Colonel Kastellan of the *35th* Trench Regiment, who says he'd have come up to meet you himself but he's in the line. And a request, Lieutenant, from Commander Thaleia of Lethe Hold." He looked at his slate, embarrassed. "The Commander's signals and the Lieutenant's have already begun to go astray, Lieutenant. Twice. The names. The Commander asks if the Hunt's traffic could carry the Lieutenant's full name and seal, to tell them apart."

Thalia looked at him. Then, for the first time since the long gallery under the Sixth Moonwall, she laughed.

"Tell the Commander yes," she said. "Full name. Full seal. Every signal. Give me the slate."

He gave it to her. It was the order of command. She signed for the Third Siege Army on the shelf at Kappa at the third hour of the watch, in her small quick untidy hand, *Thalia Grace, Lieutenant of the Hunt of the Director*, and pressed the crescent seal on her pauldron to the slate beside it, and handed it back.

"Right," she said, to her contingent. "Let's go and look at this door."
