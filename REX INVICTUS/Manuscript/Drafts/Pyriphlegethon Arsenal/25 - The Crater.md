## Chapter 25: The Crater

*"Every one of them that does not come back brings the Imperator nearer. There is nothing in the Empire that can stop what we began to call down so many rotations ago. Soon it will come up at our asking."*

*Haruspex Ilan Dace, entered under CODE 31 by the Conclave at Watchpoint Hades, Rotation Twelve*

---

**HOPLON NINETEEN-FOUR, LEAF 57-44. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 230, FIRST BELL**

Centurion Abel Strand of the *19th* Cohort of the *35th* Trench Regiment felt the Great Mine go off through the floor of the Hoplon, and then through the soles of his boots, and then through his teeth, and he thought, with great clarity, sitting in the dark of the troop compartment with fifty of his soldiers packed round him in their plate: *well, that's done it.*

He didn't know what it had done. Nobody had told them. The *35th* had been told to keep its heads down at the first bell, and at the first bell the whole of the Flats had come up under them, and the Hoplon had rocked and wallowed on its tracks in the assembly trench, and something heavy had come down on the roof with a crash that dented the plate above his head, and somebody at the back of the compartment had started praying aloud. Then the driver's voice came over the band, high and cracked and astonished.

"Centurion. Centurion, the Wall. The Wall's gone."

"Gone where?"

"Just gone, Centurion. There's a hole in it you could fly the *Anvil* through."

"Then drive us into it," Strand said.

The Hoplon moved off before the dust of the mine had settled. He felt it lurch forward out of the assembly trench and grind up over the parapet of the *35th*'s forward line and come down hard on the other side, and then they were out across no-man's land, at speed, crashing and lurching across the cratered ground toward the breach. There were thirty Hoplons in the first wave. Two cohorts of the *35th* rode in them, the *19th* and the *21st*, fifty soldiers to a carrier, and at the breach the Hoplons would drop their ramps and put eight hundred breachers onto the rubble at once, enough to overwhelm whatever the mine had left alive. Behind them came the Thureos, and a company of Colossi, and the rest of the *35th* on foot, and the shock breachers of the grenadier cohort in their Kentauros carriers. Somewhere overhead, the Wrath bombers. Somewhere far back, the Phaethons of the *10th* Ordnance Phalanx, already boxing off the breach from reinforcement.

He'd been told the plan. He'd been told it four times. It had sounded good each time.

He sat in the dark with his soldiers round him and the Hoplon pitching under them, and listened to the Archflamen wake up.

It began with mortars. He heard them through the hull, the hollow cough of the tubes somewhere ahead and then the bursts round the carrier, close, sharp, the fragments rattling off the side plate. Then heavier. The deep slamming crump of divine mortar rounds, keyed, that set the hull ringing with a high thin note he felt in the fillings of his teeth. Then heavier still. Something came down on the ground beside the Hoplon so hard that the whole carrier lifted on one side and came down again with a crash that threw half the compartment off the benches, and the driver was shouting on the band, and Strand understood that it was the Arsenal's own siege guns, firing from their pits round the cone.

"Steady," he said. He didn't know whether anyone could hear him. "Steady. Hold on. Not long now."

Through the vision slit at the front of the compartment, past the driver's shoulder, he caught a glimpse of the Hoplon ahead of them. Nineteen-Three. It was two hundred metres ahead, grinding across the ash toward the breach, and as he watched, a shell came down out of the sky straight into its open troop bay, through the firing hatch in the roof, and it went up.

He saw it burst. He saw the roof of the troop bay lift and come apart and a column of fire go up out of it, and then the dust closed over it.

The *35th*'s relay at the regimental command bunker logged forty-four uploads from Nineteen-Three within two seconds, Strand learned afterward. Six of them were null. He knew every soldier in Nineteen-Three. Half of them had been in his cohort for six years.

He didn't say anything. There was nothing to say. The Hoplon ground on.

Lance-cannon on the Wall opened up on them at four hundred metres. He heard the long flat crack of them, and felt the first hit on the Hoplon's front plate, a ringing sledgehammer blow, and felt the carrier shudder and keep going. The Hoplons' front plates had been built for exactly this, heavy and thick and sloped, and they held, most of them, and the Thureos behind them were firing back at the parapet. Through the slit he saw another Hoplon off to the left take a lance round and burn. He saw a third lose a track and slew round broadside and stop. He saw a fourth explode.

"Two hundred metres," said the driver. "One hundred and fifty. One hundred. Centurion..."

"Ramp," Strand said. "Ramp down the moment we stop. *19th*! On your feet!"

They came up off the benches round him in the dark in a great clash and scrape of plate, fifty soldiers getting up at once in a compartment built for forty, and he got up with them and turned toward the ramp at the back, and the Hoplon hit something and stopped dead, and threw them all forward.

The ramp came down.

---

The breach lay in front of him, and he had never seen anything like it in his life.

He came down the ramp into grey light and smoke and falling ash, with the *19th* coming down behind him, and in front of him the ground rose. It rose in a long broken slope toward the rim of the crater, and the rim was rubble. The whole lip of the crater the Great Mine had made was a rampart of broken blocks, thrown up out of the earth by the blast and fallen back round the hole in a ring: the blocks of the Wall itself, and of its footings, great slabs and lumps of the Arsenal's cast basalt, some of them the size of the Hoplon he had just come out of, piled on each other in a long jagged ridge four and five metres high, smoking.

Nobody had expected it. Nobody had planned for it. The Hoplons couldn't cross it. The Thureos couldn't. It would take the *35th*'s breachers on foot to climb over it, and beyond it, he supposed, was the crater, and beyond the crater the broken ends of the Wall, and the towers along them on either side of the gap.

The towers opened fire.

The heavy guns on the Wall on either side of the breach fired down onto the ground in front of the rubble in long grazing bursts, low along the ash, from both sides at once, and caught the *19th* coming off the ramps. Strand saw it happen. He saw a whole section of the *1st* Maniple go down in the ash together at the foot of the ramp of the next Hoplon, nine of them, all at once, and he heard their implants tick through the band, one after another.

"Go!" he was shouting. "Go, go, go, get to the rubble, get into the rubble, get into cover!"

They went. The *19th* went forward across the last fifty metres of open ground to the foot of the rubble at a stumbling run, in their plate, through the fire from the towers, and fell into the cover of the blocks, and lay there. Behind them, the Hoplons began to back out. Their job was done. They backed away from the rubble across the ash toward the *35th*'s lines with their guns firing over the breachers' heads at the towers, and Strand lay among the blocks with his face in the ash and watched them go and felt, briefly, absurdly, abandoned.

From here on, the fight belonged to the infantry.

He got up. He got the *19th* up. They went forward into the rubble, climbing, over the blocks and between them, under plunging fire from the towers on either side of the gap that came down on them from above, so that there was no cover from it anywhere, and soldiers fell among the blocks as they climbed. Many of the officers fell in those first minutes. Strand saw two of his centurions go, and an optio, and the cohort's signifer with the cohort's standard. The towers were picking out the officers, he understood. They were watching for the ones who stood up and waved their arms and shouted, and shooting them.

He kept standing up and shouting. Somebody had to.

He was halfway up the rubble when the mortar round came down among the blocks beside him, and he never heard it at all.

---

He woke lying on his back in a gap between two blocks, looking up at a narrow strip of brown sky, and the first thing he knew was that he was cold, and the second was that he couldn't feel his left leg.

He lifted his head and looked down. His left leg was gone below the knee. There was a great deal of blood, and his plate was torn open along that side, and something white was sticking out of the ruin of his knee that he looked at for a moment and then didn't look at again.

He lay back. He looked at the strip of sky.

He knew what he was supposed to do. Every soldier of the Third knew. He was supposed to die. If he died, his implant would carry him to the relay at the *35th*'s command bunker eight hundred metres away in less than a second, and he'd wake up at Watchpoint Hades in a few weeks with two legs. He'd done it before, eleven times. It was nothing. A soldier of the Third with a leg off in the middle of an assault was supposed to finish it, as quickly as he could, and get back in the line.

But he wasn't dying.

He lay between the blocks and thought about it with a strange cold calm. He'd got a tourniquet on at some point, he saw. He didn't remember doing it. It was round his thigh above the knee, tight, and the bleeding had slowed to a seep. He wasn't dying. He wasn't going to die, not quickly. He was going to lie here in the rubble with his leg off for hours, and the battle would go on round him, and he was going to live.

And while he lived, he couldn't upload.

He thought about the grenade.

He had one. Every soldier of the Third had one by now, on a cord round the neck, under the plate. The last grenade. It had been the custom since the thirty-one, since Rotation Five. Officers had forbidden it and centurions had ignored the order and most of them had carried one too, and Strand had carried his for seven rotations, and he could feel it now, a hard round lump against his breastbone under the plate.

He thought about it for a long time.

They were four kilometres from the Mast. Everyone in the *35th* knew the figures, or knew the rumours, which in the bays came to the same thing. Twelve kilometres out from the cone, the nulls ran at one in ten. Nobody had a figure for the rim of the crater, with the Mast humming in the brown dark over the cone just there, behind the smoke, so close he could almost hear it, and the guess in the *35th*'s bays was one in four. Three chances in four that if he pulled the pin on the grenade under his plate he'd wake up at Watchpoint Hades in a few weeks with two legs. One chance in four that he'd wake up at Watchpoint Hades with two legs and nothing in his head at all, and sit in a ward somewhere on the ceiling for the rest of his life looking at the wall.

He'd always thought three in four were good odds. He'd said so, in the bays, to the young ones, more than once. Lying in the rubble with the grenade against his breastbone, he found that he'd been wrong.

He lay in the rubble and looked at the sky and didn't pull the pin.

**THE FAR SLOPE. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 230, THIRD HOUR**

Provost Edrin Solk of the *35th*'s provost section had never in eleven years of service commanded anything larger than a squad of provosts, and at the third hour of the first assault on the breach at Leaf 57-44 he found himself commanding the *21st* Cohort, with nobody else left to do it.

He was a provost. Everybody in the *35th* knew it, and everybody in the *21st* knew it, and he could see them knowing it in their faces as they lay round him among the blocks on the crater's rim, looking at him. A provost. One of the black-coated men with the silver flashes on their collars, who stood behind the line with pistols and wrote down names. Who had shot two soldiers of the *29th* on the first cycle of Antilochus and been shot themselves by soldiers who were never identified. Who had made seven officers null by order. Solk had been a provost in the *35th* for eleven years, and he had never shot anyone, and he had never made anyone null, and it didn't matter in the slightest. He was a provost.

The *21st*'s last centurion had died twenty minutes earlier, ten metres from where Solk lay, shot through the throat from a tower on the Wall as she stood up to wave the cohort forward. The *21st*'s optios were dead or down. Solk had been following the cohort up the rubble with his provost squad, as the orders said provosts were to do, and when the centurion fell the cohort had stopped on the rim and gone to ground, and lain there, and looked round, and there had been nobody.

Then they'd looked at him.

He lay among the blocks with his pistol in his hand, and looked down into the crater.

It was a hundred and ninety metres across, and deep, a great raw pit in the Flats with slopes of shattered basalt and glass running down into it from the rim, still smoking, still glowing red in places, and at the bottom of it a floor of rubble and fused rock, and on its far side the slope went up again to the far rim, and at the top of the far slope were the broken ends of the Wall, and the gap between them. Beyond the gap, through the smoke, he could see the shanty-town of the cone's foot, burning.

The Thureos had swept the far slope clear of defenders. He could see them working, below and behind him, back on the near side of the rubble: the tanks hull-down behind the blocks at the foot of the rim, firing their main guns up over the rubble and across the crater at the broken ends of the Wall, and at the towers on either side, and at the far slope. They couldn't cross the rubble. But they could shoot over it, and they had. The far slope was empty. The ends of the Wall were smashed and burning. If the *21st* could cross the crater and climb the far slope to the gap, it would be through the Wall.

He looked round at them. A hundred and forty of them, perhaps, out of four hundred, lying in the rubble on the rim in their plate, looking at him. A provost.

"All right," he said. "On me."

He got up. He walked forward over the rim of the crater and down the slope into it, with his pistol in his hand, and didn't look back to see whether they were following. If they weren't, he didn't want to know.

They followed.

He heard them come over the rim behind him, all of them, in a long clatter of plate, and come down the slope into the crater after him, and he walked on across the crater floor, over the rubble and the fused glass, with the *21st* Cohort behind him, and nobody fired at them. He couldn't believe it. They crossed the whole floor of the crater, a hundred and ninety metres, and nobody fired at them at all. The Thureos had done their work. The towers on either side were burning. He reached the foot of the far slope and started to climb it, and the *21st* climbed it behind him, and the gap at the top of it got closer, fifty metres, forty, thirty, and he began to believe it.

They were twenty metres from the top when the Archflamen counter-attacked.

---

He saw them come over the top of the far slope out of the gap, and he understood in that moment that the Archflamen's commanders had known all along. They had known the assault would come. They had drawn in every soldier they could reach from the stretches of the Wall on either side, along the Wall's inner corridors, out of reach of the shelling, and they had held them back in the rubble at the top of the crater until the last moment. And the soldiers they had held back were Laistrygons.

He'd seen Laistrygons before. Every soldier of the Third had. He had never seen them like this.

They came down the slope at a run, forty or fifty of them, giants, four metres tall, grey-skinned and huge-shouldered, in the leather aprons of the casting lines, with casting-line tools in their great hands: rock borers, slag hammers, long iron pry-bars, ingot tongs. And they were burning. Their eyes were burning. They had been dosed with furnace-draught, the heat-well stimulant the Arsenal's overseers had once used to get a double shift out of them on the casting lines, a black bitter liquor that the Third's intelligence officers had found in captured stores in Rotation Six and read about with horror. It made them fast. It made them feel nothing. It made them mad. They came down the slope onto the *21st* as it climbed, roaring, with their eyes red as coals and foam on their lips, and a cohort caught on a slope by enemies twice its height had no chance against them at all.

Solk shot the first one in the face with his pistol at four metres, and it kept coming.

The rock borer it was carrying was a casting-line tool, a long iron shaft with a spinning toothed head on the end of it, driven by a little engine on the haft, built to bore holes in solid basalt. He saw it come round toward him, screaming. He saw the teeth.

His upload was clean. He woke at Watchpoint Hades nineteen cycles later.

**WATCHPOINT HADES, THE VATS. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 249**

He woke in the vat with the slurry draining away round him and the vat-tender's face looking down at him through the glass, and the first thing he did was put his hands on his stomach.

It was there. All of it. He lay in the draining vat and held it and shook.

The vat-tender opened the lid and helped him sit up. She was an old woman in a grey Répertoire smock, with tired kind eyes, who had been doing this for forty years and had seen everything, and she gave him a towel and a cup of something warm and waited while he coughed the slurry out of his lungs.

"Solk, Edrin," she read off her slate. "Provost. *35th* Trench Regiment. Transferred cycle two-thirty, Rotation Twelve. Upload clean." She looked at him. "You're lucky, Provost. Most of the ones from that cycle came in damaged. A lot of them didn't come in at all."

He drank the warm stuff. His hands were shaking so badly he spilled half of it.

"The *21st*," he said. "The cohort. The *21st* Cohort of the *35th*. Did they..."

The vat-tender looked at her slate for a while.

"I've got a hundred and nine of them through the vats from that cycle," she said. "So far. Eleven null." She hesitated. "The Répertoire lists the *21st* Cohort as reduced to cadre. There's nobody left of it on the Flats, Provost. Not that's alive."

Solk sat in the vat with the towel round his shoulders and looked at the wall.

"They followed me," he said. "I'm a provost. They looked at me and I got up and walked into the crater and they followed me. All of them. I didn't even look back."

The vat-tender put her hand on his shoulder for a moment. Then she went on to the next vat.

**THE CRATER. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 230, FOURTH HOUR TO DARK WATCH**

The Laistrygons carried their charge down the far slope and into the bottom of the crater, and the fighting there became a butchery.

Strand heard it from where he lay among the blocks on the near rim. He couldn't see it. He could only lie on his back in the gap between the two blocks with his leg gone and the strip of brown sky above him and listen, and he listened to it for hours. The roaring of the Laistrygons. The screaming of the *35th*'s soldiers. The scream of the casting-line borers and the dull wet crunch of the slag hammers on plate. And the ticking, through the band in his helmet, which was still on and still working, of implants, one after another, so many of them and so fast that after a while he stopped hearing them as separate sounds at all, and they became a single long continuous rattle in his ear.

He learned afterward that one Laistrygon fell for every dozen soldiers it killed. He could hear it happening.

At the fifth hour the Archflamen's gunners shelled the crater.

They shelled all of it. The floor, the slopes, the rim. Friend and enemy, without distinction, the *35th*'s soldiers and the Laistrygons together, and the shells came down into the crater round him and on the rubble of the rim, and the blocks round him jumped and shifted, and dust and stone came down on him in the gap, and he lay with his arms over his face. The relay at the *35th*'s command bunker filled with uploads faster than its crews could count them. One of the relay crew told him so, much later, in the hospital at Kappa, sitting by his bed. She said that they'd stopped counting at the sixth hour, and just watched the numbers run.

The *35th*'s tanks couldn't cross the rubble. Without them, the breachers couldn't fight their way up the slope against defenders who fought for every metre, believing the Wall would fall on them if they gave one. The second wave of Kentauros carriers and foot centuries came up through the smoke behind the first and went over the rim into the crater and fared no better. The fighting went on into the dark watch. Time after time the breachers went over the rim, and time after time the Archflamen moved guns along the Wall to sweep the crater, or the Laistrygons counter-charged and drove them out.

In the dark watch, the *35th* withdrew to its own trenches.

Nobody came for Strand. He hadn't expected them to. He lay between the blocks in the dark with the grenade against his chest and listened to the *35th* go back past him over the rubble toward the line, the scrape and clatter of plate on stone, the voices, the wounded being dragged, and then silence. The shelling stopped. The Laistrygons had gone back up into the gap. The crater was quiet.

He lay in it, alive, for four more hours.

---

At the ninth hour after the mortar round, the reclaimers came.

He heard them first. Soft footsteps, in the dark, on the rubble round him, many of them, and the faint chink of instruments. Then a light. A small hooded lamp, moving among the blocks, and in its glow he saw a figure bent over something on the ground three metres away: a figure in the grey smock of the Répertoire's reclaimers, kneeling over one of the *19th*'s dead with a small curved knife in her hand. She was cutting the implant out of the back of the dead soldier's neck. She did it quickly and neatly, with a small twist of the knife, and dropped the implant into a pouch at her belt, and moved on to the next.

Behind her, in the dark, came the Quiet Ones. He saw their shapes, tall and still and hooded, moving among the dead with their long hands. They didn't speak. They never spoke. They took up the bodies after the reclaimers had done with them, gently, and carried them away into the dark toward the Third's lines.

That was how it was done. Every soldier of the Third knew. When the firing died down after an assault, the reclaimers of the Répertoire went over the ground with the Quiet Ones behind them, cutting out the implants of the dead who hadn't uploaded, so that the Répertoire could check them and log them. A soldier who'd died slowly, or died buried, or died too deep for a relay, still had an implant. It still had a pattern on it, or what was left of one, and the Répertoire wanted it.

The reclaimer came to the gap between the two blocks where he lay, and held up her lamp, and looked down at him.

He looked back at her.

She was young. Younger than most of his soldiers. She had a thin pale tired face under the grey hood and her knife in her hand, and she looked at him lying there in the light of her lamp with his leg off and the tourniquet round his thigh, and he saw her understand that he was alive.

"Oh," she said.

"Hello," Strand said. His voice came out as a croak.

The reclaimer stared at him. Then she put down her knife and her lamp and knelt beside him in the gap, and put her hand on his forehead, and turned her head and called out into the dark, high and urgent, the first loud voice Strand had heard in four hours.

"Here! This one's alive! I've got a live one, here, quickly!"

The Quiet Ones came. They took him up out of the gap between the blocks, gently, in their long hands, and carried him back across the crater rim and down the rubble and over the ash to the *35th*'s lines, and the reclaimer walked beside him the whole way holding the lamp.

He still had the grenade. He gave it to her at the parapet, on its cord. He didn't know why. She looked at it in her hand for a moment, and then put it in the pouch with the implants.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 232**

The first assault on the breach had cost the *35th* eleven thousand transfers in a single cycle, nine hundred of them null, and nineteen of its thirty Hoplons.

By the standards of the Flats it was a modest figure. Kastellan had seen regiments lose more in an hour on Antilochus. But the Répertoire flagged it to him that night in a separate note, and he read the note twice. The Répertoire was right. It was the proportion. Almost every soldier of the two leading cohorts had been lost, the *19th* and the *21st*, the *21st* to the last soldier, and a regiment couldn't lose its leading cohorts in that manner more than a few times before it stopped existing as a fighting formation at all.

Colonel Vidar Osk came to Quarterdeck Nineteen on the two hundred and thirty-second cycle and stood in front of the Marshal's desk with his helmet under his arm and the duelling scar on his chin white against his grey face, and said that the first attempt had failed.

"I know," Kastellan said.

"I'd like a second, Marshal. I'll prepare it properly. And I'll lead it myself."

Kastellan looked at him for a long time.

"With my staff," he said at last. "You'll plan it with my staff. And with the whole of the *10th* Ordnance Phalanx in support. Not a battery less."

"Yes, Marshal."

"Colonel." Kastellan leaned forward. "I met your Major Gault yesterday. At the firing station. He told me you asked him what Prefect Voss's name was, before he fired the mine."

Osk said nothing.

"He told me you said she was wrong." Kastellan's voice was quite even. "Was she?"

"I don't know yet, Marshal," Osk said.

**LEAF 57-44. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLES 232 TO 246**

The staff concluded that the first attack had failed on the rubble, which no armour could cross, and that it had been too narrow.

For fourteen cycles the Phaethon batteries of the *10th* Ordnance Phalanx shelled the breach every watch with their heaviest rounds, firing from their pits south of the Demus Gorge, to break the blocks of the rim small enough for tanks to pass over. The rest of the *10th* shelled the Wall and its towers on either side of the gap, to wreck the parapet and the old automatic guns the founders had built along it against the swarms. Gault watched it from the *35th*'s parapet, every cycle, with the bronze lever in his cuff. He said afterward that it had been a man trying to break a mountain with a teaspoon. It was a large teaspoon, though. By the end of the fourteen cycles, the bombardment had consumed a large share of the *7th* Line Phalanx's shells, and some of the blocks on the rim were noticeably smaller.

In the first attack the Archflamen had held the breach by bringing soldiers along the Wall's inner corridors from the stretches on either side, out of reach of the shelling. The second attack would go in along the whole of the *35th*'s front, so that those soldiers would be pinned by attacks on their own stretches of the Wall, and the Colossi would go in with the flanking attacks to blow new breaches with their guns. With several breaches to hold, the staff believed, the Archflamen's strength would be spread out and less able to meet the main blow.

Strand heard about the plan in the hospital at Kappa, from a centurion of the *35th* who was in the next bed with a broken back and came out of the vats nine cycles later. He lay and listened with his leg in its frame.

"It sounds good," he said.

"It does, doesn't it," said the centurion.

The second assault went in on the two hundred and forty-sixth cycle of Rotation Twelve.

The Archflamen was better prepared as well. His gunners had had two weeks to range the ground in front of the breach, every metre of it, and the Hoplons of the second wave met a storm of lance fire and missiles on their way in, and many of them joined the wrecks of the first attack in no-man's land and never reached the rubble. Osk's did. Osk rode in the lead Hoplon of the *35th*'s *2nd* Cohort, with his signifer beside him and the regiment's standard, and his Hoplon reached the rim, and the ramp came down, and Osk led his soldiers up over the rubble and down into the crater.

He found it held again in strength.

Gault saw him go. He was watching from the *35th*'s forward trench through a periscope, as he'd watched every assault since the mine, and he saw the *2nd* Cohort go over the rim into the crater in the smoke, and he saw the regiment's standard go over with it, crimson and gold, on the far side of the rubble, and go down into the crater.

He saw it fall, about twenty minutes later. He saw a Laistrygon's slag hammer come down out of the smoke on the far slope of the crater, once, twice, where the standard was, and the standard go down, and not come up.

Colonel Vidar Osk was killed in hand-to-hand fighting among the blocks on the crater floor by a Laistrygon's hammer, together with his signifer. He was logged null. The relay received his upload, empty, at the forty-first minute of the assault. His body was never found.

To the north and south of the breach the wider attack had some success, and the Colossi blew two smaller breaches in the Wall with their guns. Neither could be taken and held. One of them changed hands eight times in a single cycle before the *35th* was forced out of it for the last time. And the second assault was beaten back, as the first had been.

Gault went back to the firing station after it was over, and sat at the table in the dark, and didn't hum.

**THE MEAT-GRINDER. ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 300 TO ROTATION THIRTEEN, CYCLE 340**

For the third assault, Kastellan brought the *9th* Assault Phalanx into the line.

It had come down to the Flats in Rotation Eleven with the convoys that followed his relief: an armoured formation raised on Callistus, four regiments, with two hundred and forty Thureos, thirty Colossi and a regiment of breachers in Hoplons. Callistus had paid for it in the cycles after the inquiry into the civilians of the fire barges, and Callistus had asked, Kastellan was told, that it be used well. He'd held it in reserve behind the *7th* Line Phalanx for a rotation against the day the Wall was breached. It had been raised to exploit a breach, once someone else had won one: to pour through a gap in the Wall in its tanks and carriers and drive on to the cone before the Archflamen could close it.

Kastellan now committed it to winning the breach. It was a task it hadn't been raised for, and he knew it.

Once again the Third went up into the breaches in waves. Once again it was thrown back, by an Archflamen who was now fighting with everything he had left. Over the following rotation there were four more assaults on Leaf 57-44, each ending in a bloody retreat. The Behemoths of Talos came into the fighting, and the drowned Behemoths came across the Flats to meet them, and the ground round the crater became the bloodiest of the whole siege. Both armies fed regiments into it, and it ate them. The soldiers of the Third had a name for it before the end of the third assault, and the name went up the line to Quarterdeck Nineteen and into the reports, and stayed.

They called it the meat-grinder.

**THE CRATER FLOOR, LEAF 57-44. ROTATION THIRTEEN, CYCLE 211**

Breacher-Centurion Mira Cassel of the *9th* Assault Phalanx's breacher regiment stood on the floor of the crater at the third hour of the fourth of the assaults that followed, with eleven centuries of her regiment round her in the rubble, and felt the ground go cold under her feet.

She didn't know what it meant. She'd been on the Flats for a rotation and a half. She'd heard the sappers talk about the cold, in the canteens, how the rock went cold before the Frost Masons came, but she'd never been in a gallery in her life and she'd never felt it, and she stood on the crater floor in her plate with the shells coming down round her and the Laistrygons coming down the far slope again and looked down at her boots, puzzled, and saw frost forming on them.

The Frost Masons had been digging under the crater since the second assault.

Nobody in the Third had heard them. The sappers learned afterward how it had been done, from the bearing of the blast and the shape of what was left: a gallery driven from somewhere behind the Wall, deep, far deeper than any gallery the Forge had cut, under the floor of the original crater, at the slow, patient, deliberate pace the Hephaestean sappers had come to call the Cocytus pace and to dread more than anything else under the ground. A flake at a time. A fingernail at a time. A hundred cycles of it, under the noise of seven assaults and a hundred thousand shells, so slowly and so silently that not one listener of the Forge, in all the posts round Leaf 57-44, had heard a single sound. And at the end of it, under the crater floor, a chamber, packed with ice.

The sappers never learned how much. They never learned how the Frost Masons fired it. They knew only that when it went off, beneath eleven centuries of the *9th* Assault Phalanx on the crater floor, it went off with a force that the *35th*'s soldiers in their trenches a kilometre away compared afterward to the Great Mine itself.

Mira Cassel never felt it. She was looking at the frost on her boots.

The floor of the crater went up under the eleven centuries in a single vast eruption of rock and ice and fire, and came down again on top of them, and many of them were buried so deep in the rock that their implants never found a relay. Nine hundred names went onto the Deep List in a single second.

When the dust settled, the crater was deeper and wider than it had been, and more choked with rubble than ever.

Gault was watching from the *35th*'s parapet. He watched the dust settle over the new crater for a long time. Then he said, to nobody in particular, "Well. That's fair."

The sapper beside him, a young woman named Wynn Tallis, looked at him.

"Major?"

"We did it to them," Gault said. "They've done it to us. That's fair." He turned away from the parapet. "I don't have to like it."

---

The Archflamen's losses in the crater were heavy too. The Répertoire estimated that over the rotation he had fed a hundred and seventy thousand soldiers into the defence of the breach, among them most of the Laistrygons still alive in the Arsenal, and that his reaction forces behind the Wall had lost more than two hundred tanks.

His losses were final, as the Third's were not. Kastellan's staff took what comfort they could from the arithmetic.

It was thin comfort. The Archflamen's soldiers were being replaced from sources the Répertoire couldn't count, and the Third's were coming back from the vats slower with every return. The third assault and the four that followed it cost the *9th* Assault Phalanx and the regiments fed in beside it some six hundred thousand transfers, and reduced the *9th* to less than half its strength in tanks. Callistus wrote to ask what had become of its Phalanx. Kastellan answered the letter himself. Dray never saw what he wrote.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION THIRTEEN, CYCLE 250**

Kastellan had found no way to take the breach, and feared that it was eating the army.

He asked the Forge for more Behemoths. Cradle-Lord Varne brought him the Forge's answer in person, on the two hundred and fiftieth cycle of Rotation Thirteen, walking up from the cradle-yard to Quarterdeck Nineteen out of his cradle, slowly, with his escort of four servicing hands and his bronze lantern, and standing in front of the desk.

"No," Varne said.

Kastellan looked at him.

"The lords of the Forge have asked me to tell you that the Forge Cohort Talos has lost eleven engines on the Cinder Flats," Varne said. "Four Brontes and seven Steropes in your offensive in Rotation Ten. The *Steadfast* among them. The bell has been rung at the Forge eleven times in three rotations, Marshal. It hasn't been rung so often since the Ninth Descent." He paused. "They will not risk the rest of the Forge's engines in a crater."

"And you, Cradle-Lord?"

"I go where I'm sent." Varne's old eyes were steady. "And I'm sent here. With what's left. The *Anvil* and ten others. We'll fight in your crater, Marshal, when you ask us to. We'll fight anywhere on the Flats you ask us to. But there'll be no more coming down the cables."

Kastellan nodded slowly.

"Thank you," he said.

The fighting at Leaf 57-44 was the fiercest on the Flats, but the whole of the Flats was still one battlefield. There were attacks and counter-attacks, raids and counter-raids, along the whole of the front, every watch. The Forsworn kept up their attacks. The Unremembered raided far behind the Third's lines, coming out of the mist off the Lethe Cut to destroy gun pits and shell dumps and then vanishing back into it. The *1st* Line Phalanx on the northern front was wholly occupied by the Grey Seventh shelling, which still came over in every Arsenal barrage and still sent soldiers back to Watchpoint Hades blank, or not at all. The drowned Behemoths stayed on the Flats to add their weight to any major attack, and the Talos engines had to go wherever they appeared, until Varne's cohort was dispersed across the Flats in twos and threes. Reports came up to Quarterdeck Nineteen faster than Kastellan's staff could read them.

The war beneath couldn't give him another breach either. The Frost Masons had learned from the Great Mine and were counter-mining better than ever. Beneath the ground the deadlock was as complete as above it, the two sides so evenly matched that every blow was met and neither had the strength to finish the other.

After a rotation of savage fighting and more than eleven million further transfers, the tempo of the war slackened. Kastellan's offensive had run its course. The Archflamen had matched it and, at enormous cost, held.

In places the Third's regiments could see the Cinder Wall from their trenches. In others they were still in the ruins of the inner belt. And the Archflamen was no longer surrounded. The Third's southern ring had been given up after the Demus Rim, and from the south the Archflamen could send columns round the Third's flank across the open Flats to the east. Far from the trenches, the Third's outrider squadrons fought a hard war of their own on long patrols across the ash, to find those columns before they reached anything that mattered. Kastellan read their reports with the rest. He knew some of the outriders' names. One of them was Hedda Brakk.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION FOURTEEN**

Another rotation of the siege passed in that way, with heavy losses, little ground, and no end in sight.

Kastellan had one piece of good news, and Dray brought it to him at the start of Rotation Fourteen. The Fleet's sweeps down the river and through the low geometry had found no trace of the *Eye of Light*. The battleship that the Forge of Hephaestus had built for Apollo's fleet under registry AC-09-1642, which had been driven down through the ceiling at Grid Delta, Sector Nineteen-F, rotations before the revolt, and been kept by the river, and come back up the Phlegethon with a drowned crew to fight the Fleet in the low geometry and ambush the relief convoy, was gone from the augurs. Most of the Fleet's officers believed she had withdrawn down the river too badly damaged to risk another engagement. Varne, when Kastellan asked him, said nothing for a long time, and then said that the river kept what it was given, and gave it back when it chose. The cables and convoys to Landing Shelf Kappa were secure again.

Against that stood the nulls.

In Rotation Fourteen the Third's nulls on the Flats passed five percent of its transfers for the first time. On the leaves within twelve kilometres of the cone, they ran at nearly one in five. The Répertoire had by then established that the nulls were concentrated round the Echo Mast. It hadn't established why. Its technical officers proposed three times that the Mast be made a priority target for the Wrath bombers. Each proposal was refused by the Fleet, whose air officers judged the Mast too strongly defended by the lances on the summit of the cone to be reached, and the Mast went on humming over the Flats.

The Répertoire tried to count what was left of the Archflamen's army, and the count made no sense.

There had been six million two hundred thousand souls on the spur at the start of the siege, none of them with implants worth the name, so that every one the Third killed was gone for good. If the Third had killed one Arsenal soldier for every five transfers it had suffered, the rate assumed in the Répertoire's first plan, then more than seven million had died since the siege began. More than the Arsenal had ever held. The figure was impossible, and the Répertoire's kill counts from the Flats were plainly inflated by a factor nobody could determine.

The truth, Kastellan thought, reading it, was worse. The Arsenal had been reinforced by the Forsworn, the Cinderborn, the Frost Masons and the Unremembered, by the drowned, and by whatever came up the river at every reformation, and the Archflamen was nowhere near the end of his soldiers. The soldiers he had had once been thralls, and casting-line gangs, and a garrison militia that drilled once a rotation. They were veterans now. The siege had made them good soldiers, and they believed that something below them was on their side, and that victory would be theirs whatever happened at the front.

Archflamen Sabinus Orrhae himself hadn't been seen or heard from in a long time. Nobody in the Third knew whether he still lived, in his rooms in the Acropolis. Some of Kastellan's staff proposed sending an Enforcement assassin into the cone after him. Kastellan doubted it would make any difference. He judged it more likely, he told Dray, that the Legates of the deep rivers had long since taken the war out of the Archflamen's hands, and that the siege was being fought to their plans now.

"Whose plans, Marshal?" Dray asked.

Kastellan didn't answer. He was looking at the map of the nulls, at the clean ring round the Acropolis, which had grown, a little, every rotation, and at the pale wash of the Grey along the Lethe Cut, which had grown too.

**THE HOSPITAL AT KAPPA. ROTATION FOURTEEN, CYCLE 30**

Abel Strand left the hospital at Kappa on the thirtieth cycle of Rotation Fourteen, on a leg of steel and leather that the Quiet Ones had made for him, and went down the cables to the Flats to rejoin the *35th*.

He could have transferred. The medicus had offered. A quick clean death on the operating table, under anaesthetic, two kilometres from a relay at Kappa where the nulls ran at under one in a hundred, and he'd wake up at Watchpoint Hades in a few weeks with two legs, and nobody would think any the worse of him. Most soldiers in his position took it. He had thought about it for a long time, lying in the ward with his leg in its frame.

He'd said no. He didn't really know why. He told the medicus that he'd got used to the leg, and that was true, but it wasn't the reason.

He went down the line to Leaf 57-44, and found the *35th*'s trenches where he'd left them, and found that the *19th* Cohort was a new cohort now, filled from the drafts, with a new centurion who was nineteen and looked at Strand's steel leg with horror. He found that the *35th* had no colonel. Osk was null, and his body was in the crater somewhere, and the Command hadn't found anyone to replace him.

He went to the parapet, and stood on the fire-step on his steel leg, and looked over at the crater.

It was bigger than he remembered. Deeper. The Frost Masons' mine had seen to that. The rim was a long jagged ridge of broken blocks and fused glass and twisted steel, and below it, in the hollow, the wrecks of seven assaults lay in the rubble, Hoplons and Thureos and Colossi, burned and broken and half buried, and among them, everywhere, the dead, too many for the Quiet Ones to have taken up, though they had tried. And beyond the crater, the broken ends of the Wall, and the gap, and through the gap, the cone. The Acropolis on its summit, under its wards, gold. The Mast above it, humming.

A reclaimer was working among the blocks on the near rim, in the grey half-light, with a hooded lamp, cutting implants out of the newest dead. He watched her for a while. He couldn't tell from this distance whether she was the one who'd found him.

He hoped she was. He hoped she'd kept the grenade.
