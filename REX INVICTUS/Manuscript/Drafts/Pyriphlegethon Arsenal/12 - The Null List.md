## Chapter 12: The Null List

*"Transfer is a delay in service. The soldier's obligation to the Empire continues on waking."*

*Regulations of the Third Siege Army, Book Two, Article One*

---

**WATCHPOINT HADES, WARD NINE. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 50**

The list ran down the long wall of Hanno Ricci's office in strips of paper, and by the fiftieth cycle of Rotation Seven it had reached the floor, turned the corner and gone eleven metres along the wall beside the door.

He had started it with a single sheet pinned at eye level in Rotation Five, the week they took him off the Flats. It had been a simple list then: name, regiment, cohort, cycle, place, cause. A null was an upload that reached the vats empty or failed to reach them at all, and there had always been a few. Ricci had wanted to know how many, and where. Somebody in Ward Nine had to know something, and the Command plainly didn't. Then the sheet had filled up, and he had pinned another below it, and another, and when the wall ran out above the skirting board he had begun to cut the sheets into strips and pin them end to end, and the strips had gone round the corner. The orderlies called it the Wall. The patients didn't call it anything. Most of them didn't come into his office.

He stood in front of it now with a cup of the watchpoint's dreadful tea going cold in his hand and looked at the place where the Wall changed.

It was easy to find. Before Rotation Six the strips were thin, a name every hand's breadth or so, scattered down the wall in a long trickle. After the forty-first cycle of Rotation Six they were thick, names crowded on names, and after Leaf 52-33 they were solid, four hundred and nine of them in a single block that took up nearly a metre and a half on their own. He had marked those four hundred and nine with a black stripe down the margin, and the eighty-eight of the Cohort of Nemesis that the Fleet had sent him a list of, out of courtesy, with a black stripe and a small silver wheel. Styx-iron. The black blades cut what the implant was trying to save, and the implant uploaded an empty pattern, and the vat-masters at Watchpoint Hades poured it into a body and the body lay in the vat with its eyes open and nothing behind them. That was in the *9th* Styx Legion's archive. That much, at least, somebody understood.

He had marked the rest, too, the ones that could be explained. A blue stripe for the river: the soldiers taken by the Phlegethon at Depth 40, whose uploads the river claimed, a handful every rotation since the first barrage. A green stripe for failed implants, the ones that had been damaged by a blast or a round or simply worn out, which had fired and sent nothing, or had not fired at all. A red stripe for the Hold Ostrava drafts in Rotation Three whose implants had come from a bad batch and whose uploads had arrived as noise, which the Directorate had acknowledged and apologised for and then quietly blamed on the Third.

Once he had taken out the black, the blue, the green and the red, there should have been nothing left on the Wall.

There were nine thousand, one hundred and forty names left on the Wall.

Ricci drank his tea, made a face and put the cup down on the corner of his desk.

They were ordinary soldiers of the Third, killed by ordinary shells in ordinary trenches. Their implants had fired. Their uploads had been logged leaving the relay pylons, every one of them, with a signal strength and a pattern size and a timestamp, and the logs were in a stack on his desk a metre high to prove it. And they had arrived at Watchpoint Hades empty.

More soldiers than the Third had ever lost in a rotation. And he had only begun counting them properly in Rotation Five.

He had been a medicus for thirty-one years, the last nineteen of them with the *29th*, and he had been taken off the Flats in Rotation Five after nineteen transfers in two rotations, which the Regulations considered a reasonable number and the Third's chief medicus had considered a reason to give him a desk. He had not argued. He had been tired in a way he didn't have words for, after the nineteenth, and when they had offered him Ward Nine he had taken it with a gratitude that embarrassed him. Ward Nine was the ward for the soldiers who came back wrong. It had seemed, at the time, an easier job than the Flats.

He went to the window. Ward Nine was on the third tier of Watchpoint Hades, and from his window he could look down the long curve of the vat halls, rank on rank of the great glass cylinders standing in their cradles under the pale lamps, most of them full, each with a dark shape floating in it, waiting. Somewhere down there, in the next few hours, another hundred soldiers of the Third would open their eyes and come up out of the fluid coughing and be helped out by the vat-masters and wrapped in grey blankets and walked down the long corridor to the reception halls, and be given a ration and a slate with their name on it and their new posting, and in a week or two they would be back on the Flats.

And one or two of them, every cycle, would be sent up the stair to him.

---

There were thirty-two beds in Ward Nine and twenty-nine of them were full.

Ricci did his round at the start of every watch, and he did it himself, though the orderlies could have done it and the junior medicus he'd been given in Rotation Six could certainly have done it. He went down the long room between the two rows of beds with his slate and stopped at each bed and spoke to its occupant, if its occupant would speak to him, and wrote down what they said.

Most of them didn't say much. Most of them were tired, the bone-deep tiredness he knew from himself, and they lay in their beds and looked at the ceiling and answered his questions in short flat voices, and he wrote it down. Trooper Ivo Imre, *37th*, nineteen transfers. Optio Halla Venn, *26th*, thirty-three transfers. Gunner Teodor Orrick, *3rd* Ordnance, fourteen transfers, who had come back from the last one unable to remember the name of the gun he had served on for six rotations, though he remembered the names of all its loaders and could describe the scratch on its breech where a splinter had hit it in Rotation Two.

Those were the ordinary wrong ones. Ricci had seen soldiers like them on the Flats for thirty years. Something went missing in the transfer, a little at a time, and after enough transfers it was enough to notice. A name, a face, a word. Nobody knew why. The vat-masters said the patterns were perfect. The Répertoire said the losses were within tolerance. The soldiers said nothing, mostly, and went back down the Stair.

The other kind of wrong one was newer, and he had nine of them.

He came to the first of them at the end of the left-hand row. Trooper Odile Fane of the *44th*, *2nd* Cohort, killed by a shell on Leaf 51-31 on the nineteenth cycle of Rotation Seven, uploaded from the pylon at Leaf 50-30, three hundred metres behind her bay. Her upload had arrived at Watchpoint Hades on time. It had not arrived empty. It had arrived with something gone out of the middle of it, and the vat-masters, who had never seen that before, had poured it anyway and sent her up to Ricci when she woke.

She was sitting up in bed with her knees under her chin and her arms wrapped round them, looking at the wall. She was twenty-four, by the slate, and had been a cutter in the hull yards at Hold Brannoc before the draft, and she had transferred four times before Leaf 51-31 and woken from all four with nothing missing at all.

"Fane," Ricci said. "Good morning."

She looked at him. Her face was thin and pale and quite calm.

"Medicus," she said.

"How are you this morning?"

"The same."

He sat down on the end of her bed. He wasn't supposed to do that. He did it with all nine of them.

"Tell me again," he said. "If you don't mind. The seven seconds."

She looked back at the wall.

He had heard it from her eleven times now, and from the other eight a little less often, and he had written all of it down. He was no longer sure why he kept asking. Partly, he thought, in case it changed. Mostly in case he had heard it wrong the first eleven times.

"There's the shell," Fane said. "I don't remember the shell. I remember Corporal Dunne saying something about the soup. Then nothing. Then I'm going up." She was quiet for a moment. "You're not supposed to remember going up. Everyone says so. You die and you wake up in the vat and there's nothing in between. But I remember going up. It's dark, and it's fast, and there's a sort of pull, upward, and I know where I'm going, I can feel the ceiling up there, the watchpoint, the vats. I'm going home." She stopped.

"And then?"

"And then there's somebody else there."

Ricci waited.

"In the dark," Fane said. "With me. I can't see anything. There isn't anything to see. But there's somebody there, very close, and they're cold, and they're interested in me. It's the coldest thing I've ever felt. And they ask me my name." She turned her head and looked at him. "I don't tell them. I don't know why. I've thought about it and thought about it and I don't know why I didn't. I just knew I shouldn't. So I didn't, and they asked again, and I didn't tell them again, and then they..." She stopped. Her hands tightened round her knees. "They took something. Out of the middle. I felt it go. And then I was in the vat."

"Do you know what they took?"

"No." She looked back at the wall. "That's the thing, Medicus. I don't know. I've gone through everything I can remember, my mother and my brother and the yards and the draft and the *2nd* and Corporal Dunne and everything, and it's all there. Nothing's gone. But something is. I felt it go." She was quiet for a long time. "I keep thinking it was something important. I keep thinking it was the thing I'd have wanted to keep, if I'd known."

Ricci wrote it down, all of it, in the same words as the eleven times before, which were the same words as the first time. It hadn't changed.

"Thank you, Fane," he said.

"Medicus," she said, as he got up. "The other eight. Do they say the same?"

He thought about lying to her, and didn't.

"Near enough," he said.

She nodded slowly, without looking at him.

"Then it's real," she said.

---

The signals tribune came up from the technical section that afternoon, as she had every few days since Rotation Six, and sat in the chair on the far side of Ricci's desk with the Wall behind her and her own slate on her knee.

Her name was Cassia Wren. She was a small neat woman of fifty with grey hair cropped close and the inkstained fingers of somebody who still wrote her calculations out by hand, and she had been sent to Ward Nine in the first place to explain to the medicus why there was nothing wrong with the relay network. She had spent three cycles going through his logs with him. On the fourth she had come back with a slate of her own and asked to see the Wall.

"Most of them died near pylons," she said now, without preamble. "I've checked every one of the nine thousand against the pylon map. It's eighty-one percent within four hundred metres. Ninety-three within eight hundred. The ones that weren't near a pylon died in the same cycles as the ones that were, in clusters, on the same leaves."

"The ones who weren't near a pylon uploaded through a pylon anyway," Ricci said. "Everyone does. That's what the pylons are for."

"Yes. But the ones who were close to a pylon, the closer they were, the more likely they were to arrive empty. And the ones who arrived damaged, your nine, every one of them died within three hundred metres." She turned her slate round and pushed it across the desk to him. "Look at the timing."

He looked. It was a graph of upload times. A soldier's implant fired at the moment of death, and the pattern went out to the nearest pylon, and the pylon threw it up through the relay chain to the ceiling and to Watchpoint Hades. It took seven seconds, near enough, from the Flats. Seven seconds in the open, between the pylon and the ceiling. The graph showed the uploads that had arrived intact as a fat grey hump around seven seconds. The ones that had arrived empty were a thin black line along the bottom, starting at seven seconds and trailing off to the right.

"They're late," Ricci said.

"Every one of them. A fraction of a second, most of them. A few of them, two or three seconds. Your nine were the latest of all." Wren took the slate back. "Something is holding them, Medicus. In the air. For a moment. In the seven seconds. And when it lets them go, they're empty, or most of them are."

"Or something's been taken out of the middle."

"Yes."

They sat for a while without speaking. Outside the window, down in the vat halls, a bell was ringing for the change of watch.

"What can do that?" Ricci said at last.

"Nothing we built," Wren said. "The relay chain is a pipe, Medicus. It's a very old pipe and a very good one, and it doesn't listen to what goes through it. To take hold of an upload in the open, you'd need something that could hear it. You'd need something that was listening on the same band, all the time, across the whole of the Flats, and could reach out and..." She stopped and spread her inkstained hands. "And could take hold of a soldier's pattern for half a second and decide what to keep."

"The Mast."

"The Mast has been broadcasting down the river since the forty-first cycle of Rotation Six," Wren said. "On our band. Every one of my signallers can hear the carrier tone if they turn the volume up and put their ear to the speaker. My section thinks that if it can speak on our band, it can hear on it too. We think it has been listening since the beginning. Since before the beginning, maybe. And we think that since Rotation Six it's been doing more than listening."

Ricci looked at the Wall, at the thick black block of the four hundred and nine and the thin trickle above it and the crowded mass of names below.

"I'll write it up," he said.

**THE REAR TRENCHES OF THE 44TH, LEAF 50-29. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 80**

Marrow came back down the Stair on the seventy-ninth cycle of Rotation Seven, after a month's rest at Kappa that the regiment's medicus had ordered and Colonel Durrant had signed, and on the eightieth cycle she went to see the colonel and asked to be moved.

Durrant's dugout was in the rear trenches of the *44th*, behind Leaf 50-29, three metres down under a roof of steel joists and ash-filled sacks, and he had decorated it with a rug from somewhere and a little shelf of books and a framed print of a sunlit harbour on a world Marrow didn't recognise. He was at his table with a slate in front of him when she came in, and he looked up at her with an expression she had begun to see on a good many officers' faces since Leaf 52-33, which she could only describe as wariness.

"Centurion," he said. "Welcome back. Sit down. How was Kappa?"

"Quiet, sir. Thank you."

"Good. Good." He looked at her. "You've something to ask me."

"Yes, sir. The *6th* Cohort's on Leaf 51-32 now. I'd like it moved."

"Moved where?"

"Anywhere along the regiment's front that's furthest from a relay pylon, sir." She had thought about how to say it on the whole long climb down the Stair. In the end she had decided to say it plainly. "There's a pylon at Leaf 50-31, two hundred metres behind Bay 4 of the *6th*. I'd like the cohort somewhere the nearest pylon is a long way off. The further the better. I know there aren't many places like that. I'd like one of them."

Durrant didn't answer at once. He picked up the slate in front of him and turned it round so she could see it. It was a list of names.

"You're the seventh this rotation," he said. "Seventh centurion of the *44th* to come in here and ask me that. Bracken asked yesterday. Ennis asked the day before. You know what Ennis said? He said he didn't mind dying. He said he'd died forty times and he didn't mind it. He said he minded not coming back." Durrant put the slate down. "Where have you all heard it?"

"Heard what, sir?"

"Don't, Centurion. Please. Where have you heard it? About the pylons."

Marrow considered him for a moment. He was a decent man, Durrant, and he had learned a great deal on the Flats in a short time, and he had not lost his nerve, which was more than she could say for some of the colonels she had served under.

"Everyone's heard it, sir," she said. "There's a medicus at Watchpoint Hades. In the ward for the ones who come back wrong. He's been keeping a list of the nulls. Everyone who's been through the watchpoint since Rotation Five knows about his list. And there's a signals tribune who's been helping him. And there's a trooper of our *2nd* Cohort in his ward who came back with something missing, and she told one of the orderlies, and the orderly told a gunner of the *3rd* Ordnance who was in the next ward, and the gunner told his battery when he came back down." She paused. "Most of the null list died near pylons, sir. The closer they were, the more likely it was. That's what everyone's heard."

"It hasn't been confirmed."

"No, sir."

"The Command hasn't said anything."

"No, sir."

"Then why..." He stopped. He looked at her for a long time across the table, and she saw him decide not to finish.

"I saw Dorran go, sir," she said quietly. "On Leaf 52-33. I saw the black sword go into him and I watched for the flicker and there wasn't one. That was Styx-iron. I know that. Everyone knows that. But I've been on the Flats a long time, sir, and I've seen a lot of soldiers go, and since Rotation Six I've been watching for the flicker every time. On Leaf 51-32, in the last month before I went up, I watched eleven of my soldiers go to ordinary shells in Bay 4. Two of them didn't flicker. Bay 4 is the bay nearest the pylon." She took a breath. "I'd like the cohort moved, sir."

Durrant looked at the slate with the seven names on it. Then he picked up his stylus and wrote her name at the bottom.

"There's a stretch of the regiment's front on Leaf 52-34," he said. "On the left, against the edge of the Ganger's Cut. It's wet and it's cold and the drainage is shit and the *8th* Cohort has been asking to be relieved from it for a month. The nearest pylon is eleven hundred metres back." He didn't look up. "The *6th* can have it at the end of the cycle."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me, Centurion. The *8th* will thank you. I'll be asked by the brigade why I'm moving my cohorts about to suit a rumour, and I'll tell them I'm doing it to suit my centurions, and the brigade will tell me that centurions don't get to choose where they die." Now he looked up. "And the brigade will be wrong. You do now. It seems."

---

The *6th* Cohort moved to Leaf 52-34 at the end of the eightieth cycle. The trench there was everything Durrant had said: wet, cold, half-flooded with grey seep in the low bays, its revetments rotting and its fire-step crumbling, and it smelt of the Ganger's Cut, where the ash ran down into a long shallow ravine full of standing black water. The *8th* Cohort went out of it with an air of profound relief and some puzzlement, and the *6th* came into it, and Marrow walked the length of it from end to end and then climbed up onto the rear parados and looked back across the rear trenches toward the nearest pylon.

It was a long way back. A thin grey mast with the dish on top and the little red lamp blinking, eleven hundred metres away over the rear lines, barely visible through the haze.

She looked at it for a long time.

Behind her, in the trench, the soldiers of the *6th* were settling into their new bays. She could hear them talking. She had been listening to soldiers talk in trenches for most of her life, and in the last month she had heard the talk change, in some small shift she could never have pointed to or written down. The soldiers who had been on the Flats for a long time, who had died ten or twenty or forty times and come back down the Stair after every one, had always talked about death in the tone you kept for a bad week in the barracks. A delay. An inconvenience. Something you got through. "When I went up at Gamma 7," they'd say, or "the time I caught one on the Cinder Stair," and they'd laugh about it.

Nobody laughed about it now.

They said "if." She had heard it a dozen times in the last month. "If I go up." "If I come back." "If I'm still here." The soldiers of the Third had never said "if" about it before. It hadn't occurred to them that there was anything to say "if" about.

Optio Brandt came up onto the parados beside her and looked back at the pylon too.

"Long way off," he said.

"Yes."

"Good." He was quiet for a while. "Centurion. I've a thing to tell you, and you won't like it."

"Go on."

"Since you went up to Kappa," Brandt said, "the *6th*'s been in two attacks. One on the twenty-second, with the rest of the regiment, against the nest on 53-34. One on the sixty-first, a cohort raid, against a sap. Both times I took them over the top myself." He paused. "They went slow, Centurion. Both times. I've been taking soldiers over the top for twenty years and I've never seen soldiers go as slow as that. Nobody hung back. Nobody refused. Everyone went. But they walked. They walked across the wire and every step cost them something. And when the fire came, they went to ground faster than I've ever seen, and they stayed down longer, and when I got them up again they came up slower." He looked at her. "It's the list, Centurion. They're thinking about the list."

Marrow said nothing for a long time.

"I'm thinking about the list too, Saul," she said at last.

"I know you are. So am I." He looked back at the pylon. "That's what I don't like about it."

**THE KATABASION, BENEATH THE CINDER CONE. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 58**

Prefect Ostorian came down the long stair to the Katabasion when he was summoned, which in Rotation Seven had begun to be most cycles, and he came down it this time with the strong sense that he was walking to his own court-martial.

He had held the belts of the Arsenal for seven rotations. He had held the outer belt until the Third broke it, and he had held the second belt since, against everything the Third had sent: the general attack of Rotation Four, the Saturation, the endless grinding raids, the mines, the gas. He had lost a great many soldiers doing it and killed a great many more, and every rotation the Third spent in front of the second belt was a rotation it did not spend in front of the inner belt or the Acropolis. That was the whole art of it. You held the outside so that the inside never had to fight. His grandfather had taught him that, standing on the walls of a fortress that no longer existed on a world that had been dead for two centuries, and he had never had any reason to doubt it.

The Archflamen was in his chair at the head of the long table, under the bones of Legio Prima Katabasis, with his hands folded on the table in front of him. Dace sat at his right. Hesper Calloway sat at his left.

She had come back from the docks fifteen cycles before with her breastplate split from side to side, and she still wore it. Ostorian had heard that Dace had offered her another and she had refused it. The crack went across the black parade armour of the *9th* Styx Legion just below the collarbones, and through it, in the lamplight, Ostorian could see something that was not quite skin, grey and still and faintly damp, that did not move when she breathed. She didn't seem to breathe much.

"Prefect," the Archflamen said. "Sit, please."

Ostorian sat.

"The Legate has a proposal," the Archflamen said, "regarding the disposition of the garrison. I wanted you to hear it from her."

Hesper looked at Ostorian across the table. Her grey eyes were tired and quite without hostility, and somehow that made it worse.

"The Cohort of Nemesis will come back, Prefect," she said. "It said so. It always does. When it comes back, it won't come to the docks, because the docks are gone. It'll come to the spur. It can fly over your belts as if they weren't there, and land on the Acropolis, or in the vaults, or on the lance silos, and two thousand of them in the Acropolis with its garrison on the Flats would be the end of all of this in a cycle. Whatever comes up the river to help us has to climb the cliffs onto the spur now, and somebody has to be on the spur to receive it. So the spur needs soldiers, and the vaults need soldiers, and the silos need soldiers. Good soldiers. Your garrison cohorts." She paused. "The only place to find them is the second belt."

"How many?" Ostorian said.

"Thirty thousand."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the Archflamen.

"Holiness," he said. "The second belt is the Arsenal's defence. It's the only real one we have left. Every rotation the Third spends in front of it is a rotation it doesn't spend in front of the inner belt. If I take thirty thousand of my garrison off it, the Third will see it within a month, and when the Third sees it, the Third will come through it."

"The second belt is a killing ground, Prefect," Hesper said quietly. "That's all it is. It's a place to kill the Third's soldiers where the Mast can hear them. It's done that well. It'll go on doing it, whoever holds it."

"Who will hold it?"

"Thralls. Trained by my people. Stiffened by detachments of the Sworn." She folded her hands on the table in front of her, the long grey fingers interlaced. "They don't need to hold it well, Prefect. They only need to hold it long enough, and die in the right places."

Ostorian looked at her hands, and then at her face, and then at the crack across her breastplate, and the grey still thing under it.

"With respect, Legate," he said. "You aren't a soldier of this Arsenal. You've been here for two rotations. I've held its belts for seven. I've buried more of my soldiers on that belt than you have under your command. And the purpose of a defence is to defend, not to arrange for the enemy's soldiers to die where something you serve can listen to them."

The silence in the Katabasion stretched on. Somewhere at the far end of the hall a Vestal's lamp guttered and steadied.

"I serve the river, Prefect," Hesper said. "As you'll find, in time."

The Archflamen raised one hand, gently, before Ostorian could answer.

"Prefect," he said. "I'm grateful. I've always been grateful. No one has served this Arsenal better than you have, or at greater cost, and I want you to know that I know it." He looked at Ostorian with his kind tired eyes. "But the Legate is right. The Cohort will come back, and it will come to the spur. We can lose the second belt and live. We can't lose the Acropolis. The garrison goes to the spur."

Ostorian sat still.

"Yes, Holiness," he said.

"You keep your command, of course. The belts are still yours. I'll want you to see to the thrall cohorts with the Legate's people, and to make sure the Third doesn't find out too soon." The Archflamen smiled at him, briefly. "No one can do that better than you."

"Yes, Holiness."

He went back up the long stair afterward alone, past the niches of the Vestals and the lamps and the bones, and at the top of it, in the cold grey light of the gallery under the Cinder Cone, he stopped and stood for a long time with his hand on the wall.

He understood what had happened. He had been a staff officer long enough to recognise it when he saw it. He still commanded the belts. He would still be summoned. He would still sit at the table in the Katabasion when he was asked to, and give his reports, and be thanked. But the defence of the Arsenal was not his any longer. It was directed now from the Katabasion, by the Archflamen and the Legate and the Kindler Dace, who attended the Archflamen every cycle, and Ostorian attended when summoned.

He thought about his grandfather on the walls of the fortress. You held the outside so that the inside never had to fight.

Hesper didn't want the outside held. She wanted it bled.

He took his hand off the wall and went back to his headquarters to begin moving thirty thousand of his soldiers off the second belt.

**THE WESTERN FLATS, LEAF 57-40. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLE 61**

Colonel Damaris Lowe had held a reformation peak on the Acheron shelves for two rotations with half a regiment when the river came up and took her colonel, and the Directorate had given her the regiment for it four months ago, at thirty-four, and the first thing she did when the *52nd* came down the Cinder Stair onto the western Flats was to walk the whole of the regiment's new front on foot, from end to end, in the dark, with her optio and a lantern and a map.

It took her nine hours. The *52nd*'s front ran across the western Flats on Leaves 56 to 58, facing the second belt where it came round the western shoulder of the spur, and it was the worst ground she had ever seen. She had expected bad. She had seen the maps and the Répertoire's ground survey and read the reports of the regiments that had held the western Flats in the rotations before, and she had expected the ash and the craters and the seep and the wire. She had not expected the bones.

There were bones everywhere. They came up out of the ash in every crater and every trench wall, the bones of soldiers of the Third from the general attack of Rotation Four, from the Saturation, from the raids of Rotations Five and Six, and the bones of the Archflamen's soldiers lying among them, all of them grey with ash and tangled with wire and rags of uniform, and in places there were so many of them that the trench walls were built of them. Nobody had ever cleared them. There had never been time, and there had never been any point. The ones who could come back had come back. The ones who couldn't were part of the ground now.

"Shit," said her optio, Bram Keddy, somewhere around the fourth hour, when they came round a traverse on Leaf 57 and found the trench floor paved with skulls. "Shit, Colonel."

"Yes," Lowe said.

"How do they live in this?"

"They don't, much." She held up the lantern and looked at the trench wall, at the bones in it, and at the old dark stains on the bones. "Keep going. I want to see the end."

They came to the end of the regiment's front at the sixth hour, on the western edge of Leaf 58, where the *52nd* met the *50th*. The *50th* was Colonel Silvan Thrale's regiment, veteran, like hers, from the Tartarus front, and Thrale was at the junction waiting for her with his own optio and his own lantern. He was a tall bony man of sixty with a long sad face and a voice that was always slightly hoarse, and they had served together on the Acheron shelves fourteen years ago and had not much liked each other then.

"Damaris," he said.

"Silvan."

"Have you seen the bones?"

"I've seen the bones."

"They told me in the briefing that the western Flats were quiet," Thrale said. "Quietest part of the Third's front. Quietest since Rotation Four." He looked out over the parapet, toward the dark shoulder of the spur and the second belt below it. "I asked why. Do you know what they said?"

"Tell me."

"They said because there's nobody left on the western Flats to make a noise."

Lowe said nothing. She looked out over the parapet too. The second belt was a long low line of darkness three kilometres away across the ash, darker than the ash, with the Mast standing up behind it on the spur against the faint red glow of the ash cloud, black and thin and impossibly tall.

"Where's the *54th*?" she said.

"On your left. Against the edge of the Lethe Cut." Thrale's long face grew longer. "The Lord-General put them there to keep them out of the way. Have you met Vadas?"

"Not yet."

"You'll like him. Everyone likes him. He ran the ration office of the Lethe Hold for twenty years and he's the best-natured man I've ever met, and he has no idea at all what he's doing." Thrale paused. "Neither have his soldiers. Most of them came off the ration rolls in Rotation Two. Lethe Hold drafts. Bakers and clerks and dockhands. They've never seen a reformation peak. They've never seen a Flats. Half of them have never transferred, Damaris. Not once. They don't know what it's like."

Lowe thought about that. She had transferred twenty-two times in eighteen years on the Tartarus front. She remembered the first time, how she had come up out of the vat coughing and terrified and certain she had been killed and gone into the dark, and how long it had taken her to stop being frightened of it.

"Then they'll find out," she said.

---

She met Vadas the next cycle. Thrale was right. She liked him. He was a round, pink, cheerful man of fifty-five with a fringe of white hair and small bright eyes, and he met her at the junction of the *52nd* and the *54th* with a flask of brandy and a tin of Lethe Hold biscuits that his wife had sent him, and he insisted she have both.

"Colonel Lowe," he said. "An honour. An honour. They told me about Acheron. Two rotations with half a regiment. I can't imagine it. I don't think I could hold a peak for two cycles with a whole one." He laughed, cheerfully. "But I'm learning. I'm learning. The Lord-General's staff have been very kind. They've given us the best part of the line, they tell me. The quietest. Against the Cut. Nothing ever happens against the Cut."

"Nothing happens until it does, Colonel," Lowe said.

"Quite right. Quite right." He beamed at her. "That's what I tell my lads. Dig in, I tell them. Dig in deep, and keep your heads down, and watch the wire, and nothing can touch you. They're good lads. Bakers, most of them. Clerks. A few dockhands. They don't know much yet, but they're keen. They're keen." He looked out across the ash toward the second belt. "Do you think it'll be soon? The attack? Everyone says there'll be an attack."

"I don't know, Colonel. Probably."

"Good. Good. My lads want to get stuck in. They've been waiting since Rotation Two." He drank from his flask and wiped his mouth. "We'll show them, won't we? The Lethe Hold. We'll show them what the Lethe Hold can do."

Lowe looked at him for a moment, at his round pink cheerful face and his small bright eyes, and thought of the bones in the trench walls on Leaf 57.

"I'm sure you will, Colonel," she said.

---

**THE WESTERN FLATS, LEAF 57-40. ROTATION SEVEN, CYCLES 61 TO 400**

The Eighth Line Phalanx spent the rest of Rotation Seven digging in.

The Lord-General gave it the time, and Lowe was grateful for it. She used every cycle. The *52nd* dug its trenches deeper and drained them and revetted them with steel and ash-sacks instead of bones, and it laid its own wire and learned where the old wire was, and it ran its own saps out toward the second belt and sat in them at night and listened. It learned the ground. Every centurion of the *52nd* walked the regiment's front a dozen times, in the dark and in the half-light, until they knew every traverse, every crater, every dead patch of ground the Arsenal's guns couldn't reach and every open patch where they could. The new drafts came down from the depot at Kappa every few cycles and filled out the cohorts, and Lowe's centurions took them out into the saps at night and showed them the second belt and told them what it was.

And she listened to the Flats.

There was a great deal to hear. The Third had been on the Flats for seven rotations, and every regiment that had held the western leaves before the Eighth had left its stories behind it, in the dugouts and the saps and the latrines, scratched on the revetments and carved into the bones. She read the stories. She read the names on the trench walls and the dates beside them and the regiments they belonged to, and the messages. *Mind the sap on 57-41, it's mined.* *They come at the third bell.* *Don't trust the Styx Watch.* And one, in a dugout on Leaf 58, in neat small capitals scratched into a steel joist: *IT LISTENS.*

She sat in that dugout for a long time looking at it.

The rumours reached the *52nd* within a month of its arrival, the same as they had reached every regiment on the Flats: the medicus at Watchpoint Hades, the nine thousand on the Wall, the pylons, the seven seconds. Lowe heard them from her centurions and her centurions heard them from the soldiers, and she didn't try to stop them. She had been on the Tartarus front long enough to know that you couldn't. In the sixtieth cycle she had a slate from the Third's chief medicus, routed to every colonel on the Flats, saying that Medicus-Centurion Ricci's study had been sent to the Command with three recommendations: that the pylons be moved back from the line, that soldiers in the forward bays keep their distance from them, and that the Echo Mast be treated as the Third's principal target. In the ninetieth cycle she had another, saying that the Command had referred the first two recommendations to the Répertoire, which had judged that moving the pylons would lengthen the time uploads spent in the open and increase the losses it was meant to prevent. The third, it said, had already been made by the Répertoire itself.

None of them was acted upon.

Lowe read the second slate twice. Then she called her centurions together in her dugout and read it to them, all of it, and told them that the *52nd*'s pylons would stay where they were because the Command said so, and that she would think none the worse of any centurion who put a cohort's forward bays as far from them as the ground allowed.

"Is it true, Colonel?" one of them asked. Centurion Livia Marsh, of the *4th* Cohort, who was twenty-six and had transferred three times. "The list. The Mast. Is it true?"

Lowe looked at her for a moment. She thought about the dugout on Leaf 58, and the small neat capitals on the steel joist.

"I don't know, Centurion," she said. "I think it might be. I think the Command thinks it might be. And I think that whether it's true or not, we've a second belt to take, and nobody else is going to take it for us."

---

The spotters began to notice it in the last month of the rotation.

Lowe noticed it first in the counter-attacks. Twice in the three hundred and sixtieth cycles, the *52nd*'s raiders took a sap-head on the second belt's forward wire and held it until dawn. Both times, the counter-attack came later than she expected and lighter, and when her raiders came back they said the soldiers who had come at them were different. Thinner. Slower. They fought with the same blank-faced ferocity as always, but there was nothing behind it, no skill, no cunning, and they died easily. Thralls, her raiders said. Mostly thralls. They had seen a few garrison soldiers among them, in the old brown-grey, and once or twice a soldier in a black cloak who moved differently from the others and never came close, but mostly thralls.

She sent it up to brigade. Brigade sent it up to Quarterdeck Nineteen. And in the three hundred and seventy-first cycle, the Third's spotters, with the Répertoire's help, made the first full count of the Archflamen's cohorts on the second belt since Rotation Four, and found thirty thousand fewer than at the start of the rotation.

The news came down the Flats faster than any slate. In the last month of Rotation Seven, twice, a regiment of the Third took a nest on the second belt and held it overnight: the *37th* on Leaf 55-36, and the *50th*, Thrale's, on Leaf 59-42, right next to Lowe. No regiment had done that since the Saturation. Thrale came down the line to see her afterward, grey with ash and hoarse and grinning, which she had never seen him do in fourteen years.

"They're gone, Damaris," he said. "The good ones. The garrison. Half of what was in that nest were thralls, and they didn't know what to do with us once we were in. They just stood there. They stood there, and we killed them."

"Where have they gone?"

"Who cares?" Thrale said. "Somebody's frightened them. Somebody's made the Archflamen pull his good soldiers off the belt and put them somewhere else. They say it was the Cohort of Nemesis, at the docks." He laughed, a short hoarse bark. "Nobody told us. Nobody told anyone. They came and they went and they took thirty thousand of his best off the belt on their way out, and nobody told us a thing."

Lowe looked out over the parapet at the second belt. It looked the same as it always had: a long low line of darkness across the ash, with the Mast behind it on the spur.

"Then we'll have to make the most of it," she said.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 1**

On the first cycle of Rotation Eight, the Répertoire informed Lord-General Castorre that he could have his breakthrough.

The assessment came under the Répertoire's own seal, at the start of the morning watch, and it was the longest Corvis had ever seen from them: forty pages of figures, maps and probabilities, which she read twice and then summarised on a single slate for the Lord-General, as she always did. The second belt had been thinned by an estimated thirty thousand of its garrison soldiers since the Cohort's raid on the docks. Its remaining garrison was composed largely of thralls with Forsworn instructors and detachments of the Sworn. Its weakest stretch was on the western Flats, where it came round the western shoulder of the spur, opposite the Eighth Line Phalanx. A general attack there, made at once, with the Eighth as the main effort and the Seventh and the Ninth in support, had a probability of breaking through the second belt of 0.61.

Castorre read the slate.

"Point six one," he said.

"Yes, sir. It's the highest probability the Répertoire's given us since Rotation One."

"And the cost?"

Corvis had known he would ask, and she had the figures ready.

"Between forty and sixty thousand transfers in the first twenty cycles, sir. Null rate estimated at four to six percent." She paused. "Higher in the forward bays. Higher near the pylons. The Répertoire doesn't put it that way. But it's in the tables."

Castorre put the slate down. He looked at it for a long time.

"Four to six percent," he said. "Of forty to sixty thousand."

"Yes, sir."

"That's three thousand soldiers of the Third who won't come back. At the least."

"Yes, sir."

He sat still for a while. Then he reached into the inside pocket of his coat and took out the two letters he kept there, the Archflamen's and Callix's, and laid them on the desk side by side, and looked at them.

Corvis said nothing. She had seen him do it before.

"The Eighth as the main effort," he said at last, without looking up. "Which regiment of the Eighth?"

"The *52nd*, sir. Colonel Lowe. The *50th* on her right, the *54th* on her left, against the Lethe Cut."

"The *54th*."

"Yes, sir. The Répertoire's plan has them making a supporting attack along the edge of the Cut, to cover the *52nd*'s left flank. It says the Cut will protect them."

Castorre put the letters back in his pocket. He picked up his stylus.

"Very well," he said. "Tell the Répertoire it can have its attack. And tell Colonel Lowe..." He stopped. He thought for a moment. "Tell Colonel Lowe I want to see her. Here. Before she goes."

"Yes, sir."

She went out. Behind her, through the open door, she heard him sign it, the slow scratch of the stylus across the slate, and then nothing at all for a long time.
