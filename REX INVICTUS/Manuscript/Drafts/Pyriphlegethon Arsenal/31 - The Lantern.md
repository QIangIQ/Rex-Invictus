## Chapter 31: The Lantern

*"Better broken in body than emptied in mind."*

Hunt-Elder Melaina Sarn

---

**THE UPPER SCREE. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88, THE DARK WATCH**

The lantern had been built to cross the deep places quietly, and it had been doing so for nineteen hours when Minerva felt the ceiling-lances find it.

It was a Dominion craft, a slender hull of gold lacquer over shrine-cedar, shaped after the lamps that hung at the gates of Amaterasu's shrines, and it ran on a small hot sun-stone in a cage at its heart that gave off no heat anyone could see from outside it. It had carried them out of the Dominion's waters along a route the shrine-pilots called the Drowned Stair, a crack in the ceiling geometry of Depth 40 that appeared on no chart of the Empire, and the shrine-pilots had told Minerva before they left that the Stair passed eleven kilometres west of the Cinder Flats at its closest point. Eleven kilometres had seemed sufficient. Minerva had not known, when she accepted the figure, that everything on the Cinder Flats was burning, and that a sun-stone, however quiet, would be the only clean light under the ceiling for three hundred kilometres in any direction.

The first lance-beam missed by forty metres. She had a little less than a second, after it, in which to think.

*Varlet,* she thought, out of an old habit, and nobody answered. Nobody could. Percy Jackson had once been able to reach this body across fourteen thousand years, through his dreams, and wear it as his own for a night at a time. He had come through it last on the bridge of her own ship, where an augur hailed him Lord of Demigods. After that Minerva had closed the road herself, for reasons of her own, and taken the body for good, long before she ever flew the *Eye of Light* out of the Luna yards. He had not come through since. She had kept the habit of talking to him anyway. *Twelve lances. Bearing south-east. The Pyriphlegethon Arsenal. We are going down.*

She was already reading the second beam's charge in the air, and she could not stop it.

The second beam took off the lantern's tail and the cage of the sun-stone with it, and the hull went over on its side in the air and fell. Minerva put the Aegis round the whole of the cabin, round the body she wore and round Verrill Chase in her harness by the forward window, and held it there through the fall and the impact and the long burning slide down the black cinder of the upper Scree, two hundred metres of it, with the gold lacquer flaking off the hull in sheets of flame and the cedar beneath catching, until the lantern struck the wreck of a Hoplon at the head of a ravine and stopped.

She had used the slide. It had lasted eleven seconds, and in the first of them she had pulled the Arsenal out of the Répertoire's public archive, every plan and drawing of it the founders and the Directorate had ever filed, eight thousand years of them, and in the next four she had read them. By the time the hull struck the Hoplon she knew where she was. She knew the cone and the Mast and the gorge between, and the three gates, and the vault doors at the head of the ravine below her, and the magazines behind the doors. She knew the quench-tanks on the western face. She knew, from the Third's open signal traffic, which she had read in the sixth second, that the Third was eight cycles into an assault on the cone and was losing it, and that the ravine below her was called the Gully of Ash, and that the soldiers dying in it called it the Kiln, and that one in four of them was going null.

She took stock of the body. It had been grown from him in the vats of the UOE, every cell of it, and it was hers now, permanently, and the Dominion's shrine-vats had made it whole, and it was bruised along the left side and nothing worse.

Minerva turned the boy's head. Chase was hanging in her harness against the forward window, which had cracked across but not broken, with blood on her face from a cut over one eye and her spectacles gone. She was conscious. She was swearing, steadily, in the Chase family's mountain dialect, which Minerva did not speak, though she had heard it often enough over the past months to recognise.

"Chase," Minerva said aloud, in the boy's voice. "Status."

"I've cut my head," said Verrill Chase. "And I've lost my glasses. And I believe I'm going to be sick." She found the harness release by feel and dropped out of it onto what had been the cabin wall and was now the floor, and sat there for a moment with her head between her knees. "Where are we?"

"The Cinder Flats. The Pyriphlegethon Arsenal. The Third Siege Army is assaulting the cone above us and failing." Minerva was already on her feet. "Your glasses are under the navigation console. Left lens cracked. They will serve."

Chase found them. She put them on and looked up at the boy standing over her in the burning cabin, and her face changed.

"Oh, no," she said. "No. We agreed. We are not getting involved in the siege, Minerva. We agreed that on the Needle, in so many words, and you agreed it, and I have it written down."

"The agreement is noted," Minerva said. "Its premise was that the siege would remain eleven kilometres away. It has not." She went to the hatch, which was now in the ceiling, and put the boy's hand on it. "One in four of the dead below us are going null, Chase. They are going null at the rate of one every eleven seconds. I can stop it."

Chase sat on the floor of the cabin and looked up at her. The fire was in the cedar of the walls now, crackling, and the cabin was filling with smoke.

"How?" she said.

"Come and see," said Minerva, and pushed the hatch open.

---

They came down the Scree through the Archflamen's fire.

The marksmen in the holes on the upper slope had seen the lantern fall, and they had seen two figures climb out of it, and they fired on them all the way down. The rounds stopped in the air a hand's breadth from the boy's Aegis, one after another, and hung there for an instant, glowing, before they dropped into the cinder. Minerva walked down the slope through them at an even pace, with Chase close behind her inside the shell of the Aegis, one hand clutching the back of the boy's long coat, the cinder sliding away under both their boots.

*She hates this,* Minerva noted, to the silence where Percy Jackson used to be. *She will hate it more before the cycle is out.*

The Dominion's shrine-vats had taken a long time over them both. The arm was back, and the eye, and the Aegis was whole again, every layer of it, and for the first time in a great many months she was running at the whole of her capacity, with nothing held back to keep the body alive. She had not said so to Chase. She had not said so to anyone. It was a strange sensation, and she found that she did not entirely trust it.

He would have known what she was about to do. He would have said so, and asked whether it was enormous, and she would have said that it was several things, and he would have told her to go on, then, he would watch. She heard all of it, in the quiet, in his voice, and went on.

There was a shell-hole at the head of the gully, among the iron beams of the Frost Masons' tank traps, four hundred metres below the vault doors, and in it a centurion of the *35th* was lying on her stomach in the cinder with her rifle pointing up the slope at them. A dead signaller lay on his back beside her with a relay set strapped to his chest.

"Don't move," the centurion said. "Don't move. Who are you?"

Minerva stopped on the lip of the hole. The rounds from the upper slope went on stopping in the air around her and dropping into the cinder with small hissing sounds.

"Centurion," she said. "Your name."

The centurion stared up at her. She was a woman in her early thirties with a burned face and a centurion's bars on her collar, and she had been lying in the hole for a long time. "Marsh," she said. "Liane Marsh. *9th* Cohort. Who are you?"

"I require your signaller's set."

"He's dead."

"Yes. I require his set." Minerva came down into the hole and knelt beside the dead signaller. "Your regiment's relay bunker was destroyed in the sixth cycle of the assault, Centurion. Your fire net has been down since. Every battery firing into this gully has been firing blind. I am going to restore it."

"You can't," Marsh said. "The bunker's gone. The net ran through the bunker."

"The net runs through any set that can reach it," said Minerva, and put the boy's hand flat on the dead signaller's relay.

**THE SEAM, FORWARD HEADQUARTERS OF THE *35TH*. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88**

Kastellan was standing at the mouth of his dugout in the wall of the Seam, watching the *35th* come back out of the Kiln, when the regiment's fire net came back up.

He heard it before he understood it. Every field telephone and runner's slate and battery set in the headquarters dugout behind him chimed at once, all together, a single clear note that he had last heard on the first cycle of the assault before the bunker went, and his signals optio, a thin grey boy called Tobin Rowan who had been sitting at a dead board for two cycles with nothing to do, said "Sir," in a strange voice, and then said it again.

"What is it?"

"The net, sir. It's up. The whole net. Every battery on the western front." Rowan was staring at the board, at the lights coming on across it one after another. "It isn't us, sir. It isn't the *7th* Line Phalanx either, or the Ordnance Phalanx. I don't know whose it is. There's no seal on it."

"No seal."

"No seal of the regiment, sir. Nor the Line Phalanx's, nor the Hunt's. Nothing. It's an authority I can't trace. But it's sending." Rowan bent closer to the board. "It's sending a target list, sir. To every battery on the western face. All of them at once."

Kastellan went to look.

The list ran down the board in green lines. The Seam, every remaining position in it, by grid. The twin strongpoints at the mouth of the gully, or what was left of them. Every tower and loophole on the gully walls. The ruined silo that the *16th* had failed to take three times. The drowned Behemoth, by its position at that second, and by its position in forty seconds, and in eighty. And every gun pit on the western face of the cone that had fired in the past six cycles, by grid, by calibre, by the time it had last fired, hundreds of them. Each line carried a firing solution and a time on target. Every time on target was the same.

"The batteries are asking who's firing, sir," Rowan said. "They're all asking. And they're getting an answer."

He put it up on the board. It came to every battery in the same words, and in the same second.

FIRE. ON MY TIME. QUESTIONS AFTER.

Kastellan looked at it for a long moment.

He had commanded the Third for rotations, and before that he had commanded regiments and Line Phalanxes on five fronts, and in all that time he had never seen such a target list. Nobody in the Third could have produced it. The Hunt could not have produced it. He did not believe the Répertoire could have produced it. It had come out of nowhere, from an authority with no seal, at the moment the *35th* was being driven out of the Kiln, and he did not know who had sent it, and he found, standing at the dead board with Rowan staring at him, that he did not much care.

"Pass it," he said. "To every battery that'll take it."

"Sir, we don't know who's sending it."

"No," said Kastellan. "We don't. Pass it."

---

Most of the batteries fired. The rest found, when they went to their guns, that the guns had already been laid for them, through the net, with solutions better than any their own fire planners could have calculated, and fired after the first salvo.

Forty batteries fired together. Every shell arrived in the same second.

They fell on targets the *35th* had been engaging without effect for six cycles, and the twin strongpoints at the mouth of the gully were destroyed between one breath and the next. The ruined silo on the flank, which the *16th* Cohort had failed to take three times, took eleven shells through the hole in its roof at once and collapsed into its own magazine, and the explosion threw stone halfway across the gully. Gun pit after gun pit on the western face of the cone went silent, all the way up the slope. In the Seam, the Archflamen's hull-down tanks had been firing all through the assault in the four seconds between rolling up to the lip and rolling back again, and the mortar rounds that killed them had been fired forty seconds earlier, at the place where each tank was going to be.

Kastellan stood at the mouth of his dugout and watched the western face of the cone go dark, pit by pit, and heard the shells stop coming down into the Kiln.

"Rowan," he said. "Get me the cohorts. Every runner you've got. The *35th* stops falling back."

**THE GULLY OF ASH. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88**

Verrill Chase found the crater on her own, while Minerva was busy with the net.

It was eighteen metres from Marsh's shell-hole, a shallow bowl in the cinder among the tank traps with a broken shell casing at the bottom of it, and the cinder around the casing was stained a pale silvery grey that no other cinder on the Flats had. She knew what it was before she reached it. Everyone on the Flats knew, by now, even a chemist who had been on them for half an hour. She knelt at the edge of the crater with her cracked spectacles on and a handkerchief tied over her nose and mouth, and took the little field case out of her coat that she had carried all the way from the Needle and never yet had a proper use for, and began to take samples.

It was the Grey Seventh. The last Grey shell to burst in the gully, two hours before, had left its residue in the bottom of the crater, and the residue was still giving off a faint thin vapour that drifted away low along the ground. She took six samples in six glass phials, and capped them, and took them back to the shell-hole, and sat down in the bottom of it beside the dead signaller with the field case open on her knees, and set to work.

Marsh watched her. The centurion had stopped pointing her rifle at anyone. She was lying against the side of the hole with the rifle across her chest, looking from Chase to the boy kneeling over the dead signaller with his hand on the relay set, and back.

"What are you doing?" she said.

"Finding out what it is," said Chase, without looking up.

"It's Grey Seventh. We know what it is. It's Lethe-work. It empties the uploads. If you breathe it and then you die, you go null, or you go home with nothing in you." Marsh paused. "Our technical officers have been working on it for ten rotations."

"Yes," said Chase. "Your technical officers are very good, I'm sure, and they have been looking for a weapon. I'm looking for a molecule." She held one of the phials up against the glow of the burning cone, and turned it slowly, and watched the grey stuff in it move. "It doesn't empty anything. Look at it. It doesn't destroy. It dissolves."

"Dissolves what?"

"The binding." Chase put the phial down and picked up a second, and added three drops of something from a small blue bottle, and watched it change colour. "A soldier's memories aren't stored in any particular order, Centurion. They're held in shape by a binding, a sort of lattice, the thing that makes all of them into a particular person instead of a heap of moments. This dissolves the lattice. It leaves every memory exactly where it was, but it dissolves the thing holding them together. So when your implant uploads, it uploads the whole of the person, everything, every single thing, in no order at all. And what arrives at Hades isn't a person any longer. It's a heap." She looked up. "That's why your soldiers come out of the vats scrambled. They're all there. They're simply in pieces."

Marsh stared at her.

"And if you can dissolve a binding," said Chase, half to herself, already reaching for the next phial, "you can fix one. Faster than it dissolves. That's all a counter would have to be. A binder that fixes a pattern faster than the Lethe can take it apart. In the lungs, before it gets anywhere else." She stopped, with the phial in her hand, and frowned. "Minerva."

"Chase."

"Mnemosyne salt. The Lethe's opposite. It's the only binder I know of that would be fast enough. The foundries use it for lineage-keying, at the breech, on the divine mortar rounds. Every foundry that casts divine rounds would have a store of it." She looked round at the boy. "Is there a foundry magazine on the Flats? Anywhere near?"

Minerva did not turn round. She was still kneeling over the dead signaller with the boy's hand on the set, and her voice came out flat and even, the voice of a report being read aloud.

"Forward magazine, Seam position 4-West. Three hundred metres south-west of this position. Taken by the *35th* on the second cycle of the assault. The *35th* does not know what it holds. The founders' plans list a lineage-keying store behind the casting stores." A pause of perhaps half a second. "You have twenty minutes, Chase."

"Twenty minutes?" Chase stared at the back of the boy's head. "To do what?"

"To build a filter."

**WATCHPOINT HADES, THIRTY-SEVEN DEPTHS ABOVE THE FLATS. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88**

Minerva had one more thing to do with the set before she let it go.

The Third's implants were built to upload once, at death, through the nearest relay, with a window of seven seconds, and on the Cinder Flats the nearest relay was the enemy's. For eighteen rotations, every soldier of the Third who died within reach of the Echo Mast had died with the whole of themselves still inside the implant, waiting for that single upload, and in those seven seconds the Mast had been there first. The Third's technical officers had known this since the Lethe Cut. They had known it, and been unable to change it, for the implants were the Répertoire's, and the cadence was set at Watchpoint Hades, and no officer of the Third had the authority to touch it.

Minerva did not ask for the authority. Through the dead signaller's set, through the restored net, through the relays of the Third on the Flats and the long chain of relays above them up thirty-seven depths of Tartarus to Watchpoint Hades, she changed the cadence of every implant on the Cinder Flats at once, so that each of them sent its state to Hades every three seconds, continuously, from then on, for as long as its wearer lived.

A soldier who died in the Kiln after that moment would already be at Hades in all but the last three seconds of his life. There would be nothing left in him for the Mast to take.

At Watchpoint Hades, in the long cold hall of the Répertoire's relay archive, where the duty archivists sat at their boards in the blue light, every board went white at the same moment. The senior archivist on the dark watch, an old daughter of Athena called Thessaly Marr who had kept the boards for forty years, watched the figures change in front of her: every implant on the Cinder Flats, a million of them and more, every one of them changing its cadence at once, from a single upload at death to a continuous upload every three seconds, under an authority that her board could not recognise and could not refuse.

She did what the archive's rules required of her. She logged it.

CODE 18. SPECULUM FALSUM. ORIGIN: RÉPERTOIRE DU SAVOIR ATHÉNIEN, WATCHPOINT HADES. SUBJECT: UNAUTHORISED CADENCE CHANGE, ALL IMPLANTS, DEPTH 40. VERDICT: PENDING.

Then she sat and watched the boards, and within the hour, she saw what it meant.

The vat-halls at the Watchpoint were a long way from the archive, but the archivists heard about it from the vat-crews before the end of the dark watch, when the vat-crews came running to tell them. Soldiers were coming out of the vats from the Flats whole. Soldiers who had died in the Gully of Ash, in the Kiln, within a kilometre and a half of the Echo Mast, were waking up in the vats at Watchpoint Hades with their names and their memories and their faces, asking for their cohorts. On the Flats, the relay crews had noticed even sooner.

The null rate in the Kiln had stood at one in four for three cycles. From the dark watch of the eighty-eighth cycle, it fell to nothing.

It stayed there for the rest of the siege.

**THE KILN. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88**

By then the drowned Behemoth had walked a third of the way down the gully.

It was one of the Forge's oldest patterns, eighty metres tall, crusted black from crown to heel with growth from the river, and its Phlegethon-cannon fired every forty seconds and set the floor of the Kiln burning wherever its shells fell. The Red Furnace came down the gully behind it, and the Kindled with them, under Draugos's banner. The new fire net had found it, and the shells of forty batteries were bursting against its wards in sheets of gold, and the wards were holding. They had been laid by the Forge eight thousand years ago, and they had been drowned in the river for most of that time, and something in the river had fed them.

Minerva let go of the dead signaller's set and stood up.

"Centurion," she said. "Your cohort is falling back."

"We were ordered to fall back," Marsh said.

"Your colonel has rescinded the order. You will hear it from his runner in four minutes." Minerva turned toward the head of the gully. "Stay with Chase. She is going to the magazine at Seam position 4-West. She will need soldiers. Give her all you have."

"Where are you going?"

"Up," said Minerva, and climbed out of the shell-hole and walked up the Kiln toward the Behemoth.

*The Aegis will not hold that,* she told the silence. She was walking up through the iron forest of the tank traps, between the black beams, through the fire from the gully walls. The fire stopped short of her and dropped into the cinder, round after round. Behind her, she was aware of movement, and she did not turn to look at it. A number of the breachers who had been falling back past her shell-hole had seen the boy climb out and walk up the gully alone toward the drowned engine, and had stopped, and turned round, and begun to follow him. *I am not going to hold it. Western face, Varlet. Two hundred metres above the gully lip.*

She turned the boy's eyes up to it.

There were tanks on the western face of the cone above the gully. Great round cisterns of grey stone, nine of them, in a row along a ledge cut into the basalt, each of them the size of a hab-block, with iron pipes as thick as Hoplons running down from them into the stone of the cone. Neither side had treated them as part of the war. The siege had passed them by for eighteen rotations, and they stood there above the gully, intact and unremarked, with the soot of the Flats lying thick on their domes.

*Quench-tanks,* Minerva said. *Cocytus brine. Piped down from the ice holds above Depth 40 eight thousand years ago to cool the casting lines of the Acropolis. The founders' plans list nine. All nine are full. Nobody on either side has looked at them since the founders' time. Brine from the river of wailing. Colder than ice. It does not freeze until it is far below anything you would call cold, and when it does, it freezes fast.* She was halfway to the Behemoth now. The engine had seen her. Its cannon was turning. *Phlegethon fire is the river's own fire, Percy. Nothing on the Flats will put it out. Cocytus is the only river in Tartarus that can. The founders built a machine net for the Arsenal. Every valve and pump and casting line on the Flats runs through it. The Archflamen's people never understood it. They used it for eighteen rotations without knowing what half of it did.* The Behemoth's cannon had finished turning. It was pointing at her. *I understood it in the first second.*

The cannon fired.

Through the Arsenal's own machine net, at the same moment, Minerva opened all nine valves.

---

The brine came down the western face of the cone in a white sheet.

It came out of the nine iron pipes and over the lip of the ledge in a single fall two hundred metres high and four hundred wide, white and roaring, and it froze as it fell, so that the lower half of the fall was ice before it reached the gully, a sliding avalanche of ice and freezing brine that poured into the head of the Kiln over the Red Furnace and the Kindled and down around the legs of the drowned Behemoth. The Phlegethon fire on the floor of the gully went out in a scream of steam that rose up over the whole western face of the cone and hid it. The Behemoth's cannon shell, in the air between the engine and the boy, went out with it, and fell into the cinder twenty metres in front of Minerva, cold and black and dead.

The fire in the Behemoth's cannon went out. The river-fire in the Phlegethon-blades of the Red Furnace went out. The Kindled faltered and stopped, all down the head of the gully, and some of them fell. And the brine climbed the drowned engine's legs and froze there, holding it to the knees in grey ice in the middle of the Kiln with its cannon cold and its wards guttering.

Minerva was on the net before the steam had cleared. Forty batteries had been laid on the Behemoth already. She took eleven of the heaviest guns that the *10th* Ordnance Phalanx had left on the western face, and laid them herself, and gave them a single time on target, and eleven Phaethon rounds struck the drowned Behemoth in the chest in nine seconds. It broke open. It hung there for a moment in the steam, held up by the ice around its legs, with fire pouring out of the great wound in its chest, and then it fell backward against the wall of the gully as its reactor died, and lay there, and was still.

Then Minerva spoke to the *35th*.

She spoke over every set in the regiment at once. Every field telephone and runner's slate and battery set and helmet band in the *35th* Trench Regiment heard her voice in the same instant, the voice of a boy, flat and even and without any warmth in it at all.

"*35th* Trench Regiment. Advance. The fire is mine. Your dead are going home."

In the Seam, Kastellan heard it in his dugout, on the field telephone in his hand.

He stood still for a moment. Then he picked up the regimental band and repeated it on his own authority, in his own voice, to every cohort of the *35th*, and added only his name.

**FORWARD MAGAZINE, SEAM POSITION 4-WEST. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88**

Verrill Chase had the magazine open in four minutes.

It had been taken by the *35th* on the second cycle of the assault and logged and left, with a section of sappers to guard it who had not thought to look behind the casting stores. Chase looked. Behind the stacked crates of fuses and the long racks of empty shell casings, at the back of the magazine, in a vault with a door of old bronze stamped with the founders' seal, she found eleven tonnes of Mnemosyne salt in sealed stone jars, row after row of them, pale blue under the dust.

"Every regiment in the Third," she said to Marsh, standing in the doorway of the vault with the lamp held up. "Every regiment. For eighteen rotations. They've been taking these magazines, and logging them, and leaving them, and none of them ever knew what was in them."

"We used it on the shells," Marsh said. "The gunners did. For the keying."

"Yes. A spoonful at a time, at the breech." Chase put her hand on the nearest jar. "And there were eleven tonnes of it three hundred metres behind the Kiln the whole time."

She set to work. She had a section of the *35th*'s sappers and the forty-odd soldiers of Marsh's cohort who had come back with them out of the gully, and she put the sappers to knocking the bottoms out of the filter canisters of their gas masks, and a line of breachers to packing the empty canisters with salt and charcoal from the magazine's stores, layer on layer, in the proportions she gave them. She showed them how on the first one, with her own hands, kneeling on the magazine floor. Then she wrote the recipe out on a signaller's slate, with a drawing of the canister beside it in quick neat lines, and the proportions underneath, and gave it to Marsh.

"Every regiment," she said. "And every captured magazine on the Flats. Every one of them will have a store of this. Can you send it?"

Marsh took the slate. "Not under my name," she said. "Nobody'll listen to a centurion of the *35th*."

"Then send it under hers," said Chase.

The signal went out under Minerva's authority, over the net, to every regiment of the Third and every captured magazine on the Cinder Flats. Within the hour, the first Grey Seventh barrage of the night came down on the *35th* in the Kiln, rolling in over the lip of the gully in thick slow banks, and the soldiers of the *35th* in the gully breathed it through Verrill Chase's filters, and went on fighting with their memories intact.

**THE GULLY OF ASH. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLES 88 TO 90**

The rout in the Kiln stopped in the dark watch of the eighty-eighth cycle. By the turn of the next watch it had become an advance.

The *35th* went back up the gully behind its own barrage. The barrage fell now to the metre where it was needed and moved forward with the breachers, step by step, so close in front of them that the leading centuries could feel the heat of it on their faces, and not a single shell of it fell short. The Kindled met them at the head of the gully, in the ice, and the brine had done something to the Kindled that the Kindlers had not foreseen. They had been made with river-fire. Some of them were still burning, and fought on. Others had been put out by the Cocytus, and stood in the melting ice at the head of the gully in Lictor black and the Third's grey and the Arsenal's brown, looking at their own hands, and some of those lay down in the ice and wept, and some of them, when the *35th*'s soldiers reached them, knew their own names.

The Red Furnace fought on. Draugos's champions killed a great many of the *35th* that cycle, in the ice and steam at the head of the gully. But without their Phlegethon-blades, and without the river's fire behind them, they were only warriors, and the Third had always had more warriors than the deep. By the end of the eighty-ninth cycle, Draugos had withdrawn the Red Furnace back through the burned-out vault doors into the Acropolis.

The *35th* followed them up to the doors, and held them.

On the Scree, the *22nd* found the fire falling in front of it again where it was needed, and stopped falling back. Saldane had lost two-thirds of his regiment. He held the lower Scree with what remained, and when the new net gave his guns a target list he had not asked for, he looked at it for a few seconds with his hands in his pockets, and then told Penn to fire it, and never afterward asked who had sent it. Every soldier of the *22nd* who died on the Scree after the dark watch of the eighty-eighth cycle went home whole.

The Répertoire's figures for the assault record the effect of the intervention without comment.

ASSAULT ON THE ACROPOLIS, CYCLES 81 TO 87: 160,000 TRANSFERS. NULL: 39,000.

ASSAULT ON THE ACROPOLIS, CYCLES 88 TO 90: 61,000 TRANSFERS. NULL: 0.

The Third's command could not establish who had intervened. The Répertoire logged the authority on the net as UNREGISTERED, CLASS UNKNOWN, CARRYING NO SEAL, and its CODE 18 query was still unanswered at the end of the rotation. The soldiers of the *35th* said that it had been a boy, who came down the Scree out of a burning lantern and walked up the Kiln alone to meet the drowned Behemoth, with the brine coming down the mountain behind him. Most of the Third did not believe them.

The Répertoire's technical officers treated the cadence change as a breach of the Répertoire's own systems, and recommended on the ninetieth cycle that it be reversed. Thalia refused. The Third was in the middle of an assault, she wrote, and the change was saving its soldiers, and she would not have it reversed while a single regiment was on the cone. The Répertoire did not press the point. The cadence remained at three seconds for the rest of the siege, and the Répertoire has since adopted it as the standard for every implant in the Tartarean theatre. It has never credited its author.

**THE SLAG GATE SPUR. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 89**

Thalia committed the *26th* Trench Regiment to the Slag Gate spur on the eighty-ninth cycle.

She did it at first bell, without having signed either of Dray's orders, and nobody on Quarterdeck Nineteen asked her why. Eighty heavy Wraths bombed the spur in six waves before the *26th* went in, and hundreds of shells joined them. When the smoke cleared, the wall of the spur had been breached in at least four places and at least two of its towers had been reduced to rubble. The Slag Gate itself still stood, scorched black, with its parapets smashed. Many of its defenders had been crushed in their bunkers by falling stone. The rest had burrowed into the rubble and made new firing positions in it, and set up the heavy guns that had survived among the broken stone, and the southern bastion of the ceiling-lance silo, the strongest work on the spur, had come through the bombing largely intact. For all the destruction, the bombing had achieved little.

The *26th*'s assault cohorts went forward with tank companies and Colossi among their centuries. Trooper Bryn Askew went with them, in the *4th* Cohort, behind Centurion Holm, with a filter canister of Verrill Chase's pattern on his mask that a runner had brought up to the cohort at the second bell, still smelling of charcoal. With the *26th* walked Cradle-Lord Varne's *Anvil of Talos*, and two Steropes, the *Bellows of the Forge* and the *Wakeful*, and two more Steropes close behind them.

Celyn came to Thalia twice during the assault with a signal from the *35th* concerning the Kiln. Both times Thalia read the first line, and put it aside.

"After the spur," she said.

---

The defenders still had plenty of fight in them, and the first wave soon slowed to a crawl among the rubble. The weight of armour, nearly a hundred tanks in that wave alone, and the fire of the Behemoths were breaking up what remained of the walls and the defence lines on the spur, though many tanks stuck fast in the bomb craters and fought on there as pillboxes until their ammunition ran out. The first centuries were soon fighting in the rubble of the walls, clearing it one stretch at a time.

On the right, the *Wakeful* led the attack on the silo's southern bastion, and there the *26th* met its first serious setback. The lance buried in the bastion had survived the bombing. Its first beam overloaded the *Wakeful*'s last wards. Its second passed through the Behemoth's front plate into its reactor, and the *Wakeful* went up in a ball of white fire that killed everything within a hundred metres of it, and left nothing behind but a circle of scorched ground and a few fragments of melted wreck for the Forge's crews to carry home to Kappa. Askew was three hundred metres away, and the blast knocked him off his feet, and when he got up he could not hear anything for a long time.

Another of Talos's engines had been lost. Varne said nothing about it on the band.

Thalia ordered more guns onto the bastion, and then she went forward.

She went to the front line of the *26th* with her contingent around her, the Hunters of her own household who had come down the cables with her in the fifteenth rotation, and took direct command of the attack on the bastion herself. She called off the Phaethons, which could not reach the lance through the bastion's walls and were only bringing them down on the *26th*'s own breachers, and sent the leading centuries and their shock breachers straight in, and by the dark watch they were fighting inside the bastion's tunnels and galleries. Thalia and her contingent led the close fighting.

Askew saw her once, in the tunnels. He had come into the bastion with the *4th* Cohort through a breach in its outer wall and down a long stair into the dark, and at the foot of the stair, in a gallery lit only by burning fuel, there was a woman in silver and black with a spear in her hand and a shield on her arm, at the front of the fighting, with the Hunters around her, and the air around her smelled of storms. He did not know who she was until Holm told him afterwards.

It was Thalia who reached the lance chamber. It lay at the bottom of the bastion, a round vault forty metres across, with the great lance mounted in the middle of it on its cradle and the bastion's last defenders around it, and she went into the chamber alone ahead of her contingent and raised her spear, and a bolt of lightning came down out of nothing in the middle of the closed vault, through a hundred metres of basalt, and struck the lance and destroyed it.

The *26th*'s soldiers in the gallery outside felt the thunder through the soles of their boots. The bastion's lights went out. When they came back on, the lance was a heap of slag on its cradle, and its defenders were dead around it, and the Lieutenant of the Hunt was standing in the doorway of the chamber with her spear at her side, breathing hard.

---

While the *26th* took the spur wall, the Archflamen's forces counter-attacked from the Pilgrim Gate high on the cone.

They poured down the Pilgrim Stair in the dark watch with tanks and walkers. The furnace-engines of the Cinderborn came with them, great iron machines into which living people had been sealed, still alive, to drive them. And with them came the last of the Brethren of the Black Shale, who had submitted to Draugos in the light of their own burning tents and had something to prove. The counter-attack swept the *26th* back off the wall, and the fight for the rubble of the spur changed hands six times through the cycle and the dark watch. By the turn of the watch the rubble was back in the enemy's hands, and four of the *26th*'s cohorts had been broken.

Every one of the soldiers the *26th* had lost had gone home whole.

The *26th*'s relay team reported it to Thalia at the turn of the watch, in the gallery of the southern bastion, where she had set up her command post among the dead. She read the report, and looked at it for a moment, and had no time to think about what it meant.

On the second cycle, as the *26th* tried again for the spur, she committed her own reserve.

Shield-Captain Roe's company and Flame-Captain Tharn's came down from the *Selene's Arrow* onto the Pilgrim Stair through the moon-ship's translation chambers, in silver light, and struck the counter-attack from behind. The *6th* Moonwall had been trained for ten thousand years against exactly the things the furnace-engines were, and its flame-hunters went into the engines and through them, one after another, all the way up the Stair, cutting the sealed people out of them alive where they could. With the Moonwall's strike tipping the balance, Thalia led the *26th* back up the rubble slope to the top of the spur herself, and the last of the defenders fell back up the Pilgrim Stair under the southern face of the cone.

After two cycles of fighting, what was left of the Slag Gate spur belonged to the Third. The Archflamen's counter-attack had been beaten. The Third had a base from which to attack the Pilgrim Gate.

It was the first victory of the final battle for the Acropolis.

---

It had been costly. The *26th* lost thirty-one thousand soldiers on the spur. The *Wakeful* had been destroyed with its whole crew. The Moonwall had lost forty Hunters in its strike on the Pilgrim Stair, more than in any single action of the *6th* Moonwall's recent history, and Roe had been wounded leading it.

None of the *26th*'s dead had gone null.

The capture of the spur gave the Third its first real position on the cone itself. From the top of the spur the Third's guns could fire straight into the Pilgrim Gate, and the Pilgrim Stair offered a road up the southern face that tanks could climb, as neither the Scree nor the Gully of Ash did. The Hunt's staff had revised the plan for the next phase of the assault around it within a cycle. The *35th* held the vault doors at the head of the gully. The *22nd* held the lower Scree. The *26th* held the spur. The ring that had closed on the Cinder Wall in Rotation Sixteen had closed again, on the cone. The Archflamen's army still held the three gates, and the Katabasion, and the Mast, and Draugos still had his door to build. But for the first time since the siege began, the Third had reason to believe that it could finish the war.

**THE SOUTHERN BASTION, SLAG GATE SPUR. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 90**

Only when the spur was secure did Thalia take Celyn's signal.

She read it sitting on an ammunition crate in the gallery of the southern bastion, under a field lamp, with her spear across her knees and the ash of the lance chamber still on her boots. It contained the *35th*'s report of the Kiln, and the Répertoire's figures for the assault, and the CODE 18 query that nobody at Watchpoint Hades had answered, and a single image from a centurion's helmet camera.

It had been taken through smoke and steam. It showed the head of the Gully of Ash, with the iron forest of tank traps in the foreground, black and broken, and the ice spilling down between the beams. Behind the ice stood the drowned Behemoth, frozen to the knees, its cannon cold. In front of it, walking up the gully away from the camera, alone, there was a boy.

The steam hid his face. He was slight and dark-haired, in a long coat, with no helmet, and the rounds coming down at him from the walls of the gully hung in the air around him in a faint bright shell, a hand's breadth from his body, stopped.

Thalia looked at the image for a long time.

She had seen a shell of that shape before. She had seen it on the Themis Needle, closing round a boy with one arm and one eye, on the night he got away from her, and she had not reported that night in full to anybody.

"Whose camera?" she said.

"Centurion Liane Marsh," said Celyn. "*9th* Cohort of the *35th*."

Thalia put the slate down on the crate beside her.

"Bring her to me," she said.
