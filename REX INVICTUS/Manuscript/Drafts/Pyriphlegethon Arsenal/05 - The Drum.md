## Chapter 5: The Drum

*"I do not expect my first wave to meet much resistance."*

*Colonel Petra Anselm, 37th Trench Regiment, report to the 7th Line Phalanx, Rotation One, cycle 78*

---

**COMMAND POST OF THE 37TH TRENCH REGIMENT, WESTERN FLATS. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 78**

Colonel Petra Anselm had been told the Drum was a soft spot so many times that she had started to believe it, which, she would reflect later, was the first mistake.

"Leaf 22-14," said her chief of staff, for the fourth time that watch, tapping the overlay on the map table. "Fewest nests per kilometre on the whole belt. The Répertoire says they built it in a hurry. It says three nests on the crest and two in the dip behind it, and a single trench line where everywhere else has three."

"I know what the Répertoire says, Dain."

"I'm just reminding you, ma'am."

"You've reminded me four times."

"And you've looked happier every time," said Major Dain Corbel, who had been her chief of staff for six years and was the only person in the *37th* who could get away with saying things like that. He was a short, round, cheerful man with spectacles he didn't need, which he wore because he said they made the regiment take him seriously. "I'm doing it for morale."

Anselm laughed despite herself and pulled the map toward her.

The command post of the *37th* Trench Regiment was a dugout twelve metres under the ash of the western Flats, roofed with three layers of steel plate and two of basalt, and it shook every few seconds with the guns. It had been shaking for four cycles. The *2nd* Ordnance Phalanx had opened fire on the seventy-fourth in support of the regiments' own batteries, and since then it hadn't stopped, not for a minute, a solid continuous roar of fire that came down through twelve metres of earth and set the lamps swinging on their chains and shook a fine black dust from the ceiling onto the map table, so that Corbel had to keep brushing it off the overlay with his sleeve. Anselm had stopped hearing it on the second day. It had become the sound the world made.

The map showed the Drum. It was a low cinder rise on the western Flats where the outer belt crossed a gentle swell of ground, about two kilometres across and forty metres high at the crest, and the Archflamen's thralls had dug their line along the top of it. On the overlay, the line was red, and it was crossed out in black in a great many places. Those were the places the spotters had marked as destroyed.

"Show me the latest from the periscopes," she said.

Corbel brought it up. It was a composite from the forward spotters, taken at the start of the watch, and it showed the Drum as it now looked from the *37th*'s forward trench, twenty-six hundred metres away. It looked like the surface of something that had been boiled. The cinder rise had been turned over so many times by the shells that the outline of the Archflamen's forward trench had disappeared entirely, and the roads that used to cross it were gone, and the wire was a tangle of black threads lying flat on the ash. Here and there a lump of concrete stuck up out of the rubble.

"Three million six hundred thousand shells," Corbel said. "On three leaves. Nobody's ever fired that many in the Third. Nobody's ever fired that many anywhere."

"Anything still alive on it?"

"The Répertoire says it doesn't expect much." He shrugged. "Some of the deep shelters will have held. They'll have thralls in them, shaken up, deaf, half-dead. When the barrage lifts, they'll come up and find our lads standing on top of them."

Anselm looked at the boiled surface of the Drum for a long time.

She was forty-seven. She'd been colonel of the *37th* for nine years and a soldier of it for twenty-nine, since she was drafted from the ceiling world of Ossaea at eighteen with a rifle and a bad attitude, and she had been killed seven times, which was low for a line officer of her age, and which she put down partly to luck and mostly to never once in twenty-nine years having led an attack across open ground. The *37th* had fought eleven reformation peaks out of the Phlegethon in her time. It had held the line against them, and dug up to them, and shelled them flat, and it had never once gone over a parapet into the fire of an enemy who had machine guns and knew how to use them. She'd read the Tactica on it. She'd read the manuals. She had studied every assault on a trench belt in the history of the Empire, and in all of them the attacker had won when the guns had done their work, and the guns had done their work on the Drum, and she could see it with her own eyes.

She pulled the report pane toward her and began to write.

*To the 7th Line Phalanx. From the 37th Trench Regiment. Bombardment of Leaf 22-14 continues as planned. Periscope survey indicates destruction of enemy forward works and wire across the regimental front. Regiment is formed and ready. I do not expect my first wave to meet much resistance. P. Anselm, Colonel.*

She read it twice, and sent it.

**THE FORWARD TRENCH, 14TH COHORT. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 79, FOURTH BELL**

Trooper Kel Imber had been killed three times, and on the morning of the first assault on the outer belt, standing on the fire-step of the *14th* Cohort's forward trench with his rifle at the port and his bayonet fixed and his heart going faster than he had ever felt it go, he found that it didn't help at all.

"Steady," Centurion Adra Voss was saying, walking slowly down the bay behind them. "Steady, *14th*. Steady. Listen for the whistle."

The barrage was still falling on the Drum. It was falling twenty-six hundred metres in front of them, and it had been falling for five cycles, and from the fire-step Imber could see it over the parapet as a wall of light and smoke, gold and red and the dull deep orange of the Phaethon shells coming down from eighty kilometres back. The noise of it had stopped being noise days ago. It was a weight. It sat on his chest and his ears and the top of his skull and pressed down, and he could feel it in his teeth, in his fingers on the stock of the rifle, in his feet on the fire-step.

"It'll lift any minute," said Teo Brask beside him. Brask was nineteen, the same as him. They'd come down from the ceiling in the same draft, four years ago, and been in the same section ever since, and they'd both been killed three times, though never on the same day. Brask's face was white under the soot and his helmet was slightly too big for him. "Any minute now."

"I know."

"Then it's twenty-six hundred metres. At the walk." Brask laughed, a short dry sound. "At the walk, Kel. They want us to walk."

"I know, Teo."

"I'm just saying."

Imber didn't answer. He was looking at the Drum.

The *14th* Cohort was in the centre of the *37th*'s front. On either side of it, along the trench, the other cohorts of the regiment were standing on their fire-steps as well, six hundred and forty to a cohort, rifles at the port, bayonets fixed, waiting, and beyond them on both flanks the *22nd* and *33rd* Trench Regiments were waiting to go over in their supporting attacks. Imber had heard the colonel's report read out at the morning stand-to. She didn't expect much resistance. He'd been glad to hear it. He'd looked at the Drum through a periscope every cycle for a week and seen nothing on it alive.

The whistle blew.

It blew at the fourth bell, exactly as the barrage lifted, and he knew it was the fourth bell because the barrage lifted. One moment the Drum was a wall of fire, and the next it wasn't. The fire was rolling back, away from them, over the crest of the Drum and down the far side, toward the second line of the outer belt, and the Drum was suddenly just a low dark hump of smoking ash in the first light of the watch with nothing on it, and the noise had gone with the fire, and in the sudden quiet, under the ringing in his ears, Imber heard Centurion Voss shouting.

"Up! *14th*, up! Over! Go, go, go!"

He went up the ladder. He went over the parapet. He came down on the ash on the far side in a crouch and straightened up with his rifle at the port, as he'd been trained, and on his right Brask came over and straightened up beside him, and along the whole of the *37th*'s front, from one end of the trench to the other, the soldiers of the Third came up out of the ground and formed their lines and began to walk.

It was quiet. That was the strangest thing. He would remember that afterward, the quiet. The barrage was rolling away over the Drum, and the guns of the Third had lifted their fire to the rear, and for twenty metres the only sounds in the world were the ringing in his ears and his own boots in the ash and the boots of the cohort around him, six hundred and forty soldiers walking forward at an even pace with their rifles at the port.

At twenty metres, the Drum opened fire.

---

It came from the crest. It came from three places on the crest that he could see, low black openings in lumps of concrete that the spotters had crossed out in black on the overlay, and from somewhere behind them that he couldn't see, and it came as a long, flat, hammering roar that swept across the ash toward the *14th* Cohort's line at waist height, and crossed it.

Imber saw the line go down.

It went down in a ripple from left to right as the fire swept across it, soldiers folding over at the waist and dropping into the ash with their rifles still at the port, and he heard Brask beside him make a small surprised noise, and then the fire was on him and he was face-down in the ash without knowing how he'd got there, with the hammering going over his head so close that he could feel the wind of the rounds on the back of his neck.

He lay still. The fire went on. It swept back across the line from right to left, and then from left to right again, steady and unhurried, and wherever it found something above the ash, it hit it. He could hear it hitting things. He could hear the soldiers of the *14th* screaming.

Then the shells came.

They came from the far side of the Drum, from the Archflamen's mortars dug in behind the crest, and they came down on the ash among the *14th* Cohort in patterns. He would understand that later. At the time it was just shells, divine shells, gold and red and orange, bursting around him one after another, and he pressed his face into the ash and dug his fingers into it and waited to die a fourth time. Later he understood the patterns. The Archflamen's gunners had staked every crater on Leaf 22-14 in Rotation Zero. They'd walked their ranging shots across the Drum before the Third ever came down the Stair, marking every metre, and now they brought their shells down on the *14th* Cohort in long careful lines that walked from the Third's wire to the enemy's and back again, and nothing that lay between the two was safe.

Something hit the ash beside him. He turned his head. It was Brask, rolling into the shallow scrape of a shell crater beside him, with his helmet gone and blood all down one side of his face.

"Kel," Brask said. "Kel, I'm hit. I'm hit, Kel."

"Get in the crater," Imber said. "Get down. Get into the crater."

They got into the crater. It was shallow, a metre deep, the scrape of a small shell that had come down there some time in the last five cycles. Imber crawled into it on his belly with Brask beside him, and pressed himself into the bottom, and lay there with the fire hammering over the lip of it a hand's breadth above their heads.

Brask was crying. Imber didn't blame him.

"It was supposed to be clear," Brask said. "It was supposed to be clear, Kel. They said it was clear. They said there'd be nothing left on it."

"I know."

"Five fucking cycles. Five cycles they shelled it. How the fuck is anything still alive on it?"

"I don't know, Teo."

Imber turned over onto his back, carefully, keeping his head down, and looked up past the lip of the crater at the sky over the Drum. It was full of smoke and the bursts of the Arsenal's shells and the long red streaks of tracer from the nests on the crest, and somewhere off to his left he could hear Centurion Voss shouting, and then he couldn't hear Centurion Voss anymore.

---

He found out afterward that the thralls had been twenty metres down. They'd sat out five cycles of bombardment in their deep shelters in the basalt under the Drum, deaf and half-mad, while three million six hundred thousand shells came down on the rubble over their heads. When the barrage lifted, they'd come up their shafts and manned their guns. It had taken them a little under a minute. The barrage had lifted at the fourth bell exactly, and the *14th* Cohort had gone over the parapet at the fourth bell exactly, at the whistle, as the Répertoire had planned, and had walked twenty metres at the even pace the Third had been trained to for six hundred years, and the Archflamen's thralls had had all the time in the world.

He found out afterward, too, how many of the *14th* Cohort had died in that first watch. Six hundred and forty had gone over in nine maniples. Five hundred and seventy-one were transferred or wounded by the end of the watch, including every officer in the cohort. Centurion Voss was killed sixty metres out, leading the *3rd* Maniple forward out of the craters. Her optio was killed trying to reach her. Both of the cohort's tesserarii, all nine of the maniple leaders, every section leader but two. Every soldier who stood up to lead the others was killed in turn, one after another, as they rose, and the others watched them do it.

Imber didn't stand up. He lay in the crater with Brask for seven hours.

---

At the sixth bell the second wave came over.

He heard it before he saw it, a deep, grinding, metallic sound coming up behind him out of the *37th*'s trenches, and he turned over onto his belly and put his eyes to the lip of the crater and looked back, and saw the breachers coming.

He'd never seen the *37th*'s heavy maniples in the attack before. Nobody had. They came out of the forward trench in a long line, two hundred kilograms of plate to every one of them, great square grey figures with their visors down and their heavy rifles and breaching charges and their shields of layered steel, walking forward across the ash toward the Drum at the same even pace as the first wave, and he thought, *they'll get through*. They'd get through. The rifles of the nests couldn't touch them. Nothing on the Drum could touch them.

He watched them come up level with his crater, and pass it, walking, the rounds of the nests pinging off their plate in showers of sparks, and he watched them go on toward the crest.

Then the Archflamen's gunners brought down their shells.

The plate stopped rifle fire and was no use at all against shells or the heavy guns in the nests, which opened up as the breachers came within range, slow, deep, thumping guns that fired once every few seconds and every time they fired a breacher went down. The shells walked across them in their patterns, from the Third's wire to the enemy's and back, and the heavy guns of the nests picked them off one at a time, and the breachers kept walking, because they'd been trained to walk, and Imber lay in his crater and watched the second wave of the first assault on the outer belt get destroyed on exactly the same ground as the first, in exactly the same way, two hundred metres in front of him.

"Oh, fuck," Brask said, beside him, quietly. He'd stopped crying some time ago. "Oh, fuck me, Kel. They've watched it all morning. They've watched it all fucking morning through the periscopes, and they've walked out into it anyway."

Imber didn't say anything.

At the end of the seventh watch the attack was halted. Nobody told the craters. They found out when the shells stopped coming from the Third's side and kept coming from the Arsenal's.

---

They crawled back after dark.

It took them nine hours. The Archflamen's gunners fired flares over Leaf 22-14 all night, at irregular intervals, white magnesium flares that hung in the air over the Drum on little parachutes and lit the whole of the ground between the lines in a hard white light, and whenever a flare went up, anything moving on the ash below it was shot at. Imber learned to lie still when the flares went up. He learned to move only in the dark between them, a few metres at a time, on his belly, dragging Brask by the collar of his plate, from one crater to the next, back toward the Third's wire.

There were other soldiers out there. He could hear them all around him in the dark, crawling, moaning, calling for help. Some of them called for their mothers. Some of them called for their centurions, who were dead. Some of them called for the Third's medici, who couldn't come out onto the ash under the flares. Once, a few metres to his left, somebody started screaming and didn't stop, until a flare went up and a rifle on the crest cracked, and then it stopped.

Brask died in the fourth hour. Imber didn't notice when it happened. He was dragging him over the lip of a crater, and Brask was heavy, and then he was heavier, and when Imber looked down at him in the light of the next flare his eyes were open and he wasn't breathing. Imber lay beside him in the crater for a long time.

He'd be back. That was the thing. He'd be back in a fortnight, waking up in a vat at the Watchpoint with nectar in his mouth and a new body and the same face, and he'd come back down the Stair and find Imber in the forward trench and say, *fucking Drum*, and Imber would say, *fucking Drum*, and they'd go on. That was the deal.

He left Brask in the crater, and crawled on.

---

At roll that night, in the forward trench of the *37th* Trench Regiment, the survivors of the *14th* Cohort numbered sixty-nine.

They stood in a line along the fire bays of the forward trench, filthy and bleeding, with their rifles and without, in the dim light of the trench lamps, while a centurion of the *15th* Cohort who had been sent over to take charge of them went down the line with a slate and wrote their names. Sixty-nine soldiers. Fewer than a single maniple at full strength. Imber stood at the end of the line with Brask's blood dried black on his hands and his rifle gone, somewhere out on the ash, and when the centurion came to him and asked his name, he gave it, and his number, and his section, and then, because the centurion didn't move on, he said, "Teo Brask. Fourth hour. In a crater. About eight hundred metres out."

The centurion wrote it down. Then he looked at Imber for a moment.

"First time over?" he asked.

"Yes, Centurion."

"Right." He tucked the slate under his arm. "Get some food in you, and some sleep. Your mate'll be back in a fortnight. The third wave goes in tomorrow at the fourth bell."

---

**THE DEEP SHELTER UNDER THE DRUM. ROTATION ONE, CYCLES 74 TO 79**

Galba Ostorian had spent five cycles twenty metres under the Drum, and at the end of them he had decided that if he ever got out of the war alive he would spend the rest of his life somewhere silent with no ceiling.

The deep shelter under Nest Gamma 7 was cut into the basalt at the bottom of a shaft that went down from the nest in a long spiral stair, a single long chamber the size of a barracks hall, roofed with the rock of the shelf, and it held four hundred thralls of the *11th* Imperator's Cohort, and their officers, and six of the Sworn, and the Prefect of the Imperator's legion. It was hot. It stank. It had no air but what came down the shaft, and no light but tin lamps, and for five cycles the Third's shells had been falling on the rubble twenty metres overhead without a moment's pause, so that the whole chamber shook with them continuously, and dust and grit fell from the ceiling in a slow endless rain, and the lamps swung on their chains, and nobody could hear anything said to them unless it was shouted into their ear from a hand's breadth away.

Ostorian sat on an upturned crate near the foot of the stair with his back to the rock and his eyes closed and waited.

He'd come down on the second cycle of the bombardment, against the advice of everyone in his staff and in particular against the advice of Dace, who had told him pleasantly that the Prefect of the Imperator's legion was too valuable to bury under a hill. He'd come down anyway. He'd watched the Third prepare this attack for three weeks from the second belt, through the long glass, the guns coming forward and the shell dumps growing and the *37th* moving up into its forward trench, and he had guessed the Drum would be the place. He'd wanted to see it for himself. If the Third was going to come at him across open ground, as it had trained to for six hundred years, then he wanted to be there when it did.

He'd come down, and he'd sat in the dark for five cycles while the thralls of the *11th* Cohort went mad around him.

Some of them did. He'd expected it. They were forge-workers who had held a rifle for the first time the year before, and they'd never been under a bombardment, and nobody who hadn't been under one could imagine it. After the first cycle they stopped talking. After the second, some of them started to scream, and their officers had to hold them down. On the third, a thrall of the *2nd* Maniple got up from his place on the floor, walked to the foot of the stair, and started to climb, and kept climbing, and nobody stopped him, and nobody ever saw him again. On the fourth, two of them fought with knives over a water bottle, and the Sworn shot both. On the fifth, they sat in silence with their hands over their ears and their eyes shut, rocking.

None of them broke, which surprised him. They went mad, and some of them died, but none of them broke, and none of them tried to leave the shelter but the one, and when the officers told them to check their rifles, they checked their rifles. He'd been watching them for five cycles. He'd thought they would break. He had not, he realised, understood what it meant to have nowhere to go. The ceiling would never take them back. If they ran, the Lictors would shoot them. If they surrendered, the Third would hand them back to the Hephaestean orders, who would put them in the casting lines for the rest of their short lives. There was the Drum, and the shelter under it, and the guns on the crest, and that was all.

At the end of the fifth cycle, without warning, the noise stopped.

---

It didn't stop entirely. It rolled. He felt it go over his head, the weight of it lifting off the chamber roof and moving away, back over the crest of the Drum toward the second line, and he was on his feet before he knew it.

"Up!" he roared. "Up, up! The guns are lifting! Man the nest! Up the shaft! Go!"

They went. Four hundred thralls of the *11th* Imperator's Cohort went up the spiral stair of the shaft at a run, shoving, stumbling, deaf, filthy, half-mad, and it took them a little under a minute to reach the top. Ostorian went up with them. He came out of the head of the shaft into the gun chamber of Nest Gamma 7, which was a low concrete room six metres under the rubble of the Drum with a single long embrasure looking out over the ground in front of it, and the light coming through the embrasure was grey and thin, the first light of the watch, and the thralls were throwing themselves onto the guns.

Gamma 7 had four guns. Three of them had survived five cycles of bombardment with nothing worse than dust in their works. The fourth had been knocked off its mount when a Phaethon shell came down on the slope above the nest on the second cycle, and four thralls were trying to heave it back up. Ostorian left them to it. He went to the embrasure and put his eye to it.

The ground in front of the Drum was boiled ash and wire, smoking, empty. Beyond it, twenty-six hundred metres off, was the forward trench of the *37th* Trench Regiment, and on the ash in front of it were the soldiers of the Third.

They were walking toward him. They were walking in lines, at an even pace, with their rifles at the port.

"Gods," Ostorian said softly. "Gods. They're actually doing it."

He'd read the manuals. He'd known that they would. He'd still thought, right up to the moment he put his eye to the embrasure, that there would be something else, some trick, some feint, smoke at least. There was nothing. There were the soldiers of the Third coming toward Gamma 7 across open ground at the walk, as they had been trained to for six hundred years, and they were a little over twenty metres out from their own wire.

"Fire," he said.

---

He stayed in Gamma 7 for six hours. He watched the first wave of the *37th* destroyed in front of him, and he watched the second wave come over at the sixth bell, the great grey breachers in their heavy plate, and walk into the same fire across the same ground and be destroyed in the same way, and he watched the attack halted at the end of the seventh watch, and he didn't feel anything that he could put a name to.

He watched what happened in the forward trench of the outer belt, too, where a few sections of the Third had crawled through the wire where the guns had thinned it and come down into the fire bays. He watched the thralls fight them hand to hand in the bays, with bayonets and entrenching tools and their bare hands, and he watched the thralls die, a great many of them. They had no implants. A thrall killed on the Drum was gone for good. They knew that, and they held their bays to the last anyway, and when the bays fell, Ostorian did what he had been told to do.

"Mortar line," he said into the field-net. "Gamma 7. Bays four through nine of the forward trench, below the nest. Enemy in the bays. Fire on them."

There was a pause on the net. The mortar centurion on the far side of the Drum was an old garrison man named Pellas, who had served with Ostorian for twenty years.

"Prefect," he said. "There'll be thralls in those bays. Some of ours'll still be alive."

"I know, Pellas. Fire on them."

The mortars fired on them. When the smoke cleared there was nothing alive in bays four through nine of the forward trench below Gamma 7, Third or thrall, and the Third didn't come into them again.

**THE INNER WARD, THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 84**

On the evening of the eighty-fourth cycle, after the third wave of the *37th* had gone in at the fourth bell and been destroyed on the same ground as the first two, Ostorian went up the Pilgrim Stair to the Archflamen's table and asked for permission to counter-attack.

He did it standing, in his filthy armour, with the dust of Gamma 7 still in his hair, because he hadn't had time to wash and didn't intend to make time. The Archflamen listened, as he always did, with his kind tired face turned toward him and his hands open on the table. At his right hand, Dace listened too, and smiled.

"They're bled white, Holiness," Ostorian said. "The *37th*. I watched them through the embrasure for three cycles. They've put three waves across the Drum and lost the best part of their infantry doing it. Their forward trench is held by survivors and walking wounded. If I put nine thousand of the garrison across the ash behind a barrage and a screen of Hoplons, I can take that trench by the end of the watch, and push the Third back two kilometres along the whole of the Drum front, and they won't be able to stop me."

"Nine thousand," the Archflamen said.

"Nine thousand, Holiness. The best of the garrison."

"And how many of the nine thousand will come home?"

Ostorian hesitated.

"Most of them," he said. "If it goes the way I think it will."

The Archflamen looked at him for a long time.

"Then go," he said. "With my blessing. And Prefect." His voice was gentle. "Please count them for me, when they come home. I'd like to say a prayer for every one."

---

The counter-attack went in at the third bell of the eighty-fifth cycle, behind a barrage from the Arsenal's divine mortars and a screen of forty Hoplon carriers from the vaults, and in the first minute, Ostorian knew he'd made a mistake.

He'd been right about the *37th*'s infantry. The forward trench of the regiment was held by survivors and walking wounded, and when the garrison came across the ash behind its barrage, the infantry in the forward trench fired on them for a few minutes and then fell back down the communication trenches. He had been right about that. What he hadn't considered, and should have, and would remember not considering for the rest of the siege, were the *37th*'s heavy weapons.

They had never been committed to the assault. They had sat in their pits behind the forward trench for three cycles while the infantry went over, the heavy mortars and the quad-launchers and the heavy rifles of the second line, dug in where they had been placed when the *37th* came forward, with the same crews and their ammunition stacked beside them, and they had watched the three waves fail. They'd had nothing to do for three cycles. They were eager to do something now.

The garrison went into a crossing fire exactly like the one that had destroyed the *37th* on the Drum. Ostorian watched it from the forward observation post of the outer belt, through a periscope, and he saw the first ranks of the garrison come up level with the *37th*'s abandoned forward trench, and he saw the heavy rifles of the second line open up on them from both flanks at once, and he saw the garrison soldiers go down in a long ripple from left to right, folding over at the waist and falling into the ash with their rifles at the port, exactly as he'd seen the Third's soldiers fall in front of Gamma 7. And within the minute, the heavy mortars and quad-launchers of the *37th*'s second line lifted their fire onto the ground behind them, and the Hoplon carriers began to burn.

"Back," he said into the field-net. "Back. All cohorts, back. Withdraw to the outer belt. Now."

It took them until the end of the watch to get back. Many of them didn't. Of the nine thousand of the garrison who had gone across the ash, fewer than four thousand came back to the outer belt, and those who didn't come back lay on the ash in front of the *37th*'s trench on top of the *37th*'s own dead from the three waves, in a long low drift of grey armour that looked the same from both sides.

The Third's dead would be back in a fortnight. The garrison's would not.

---

That night he climbed the Stair again, and stood before the Archflamen's table, and reported.

"Five thousand and forty, Holiness," he said. "That's how many didn't come home. I've counted them. I've got all their names." He held out a slate. "You said you'd say a prayer for every one."

The Archflamen took the slate. He looked at it for a while. Then he looked up at Ostorian, and his eyes were wet.

"Thank you, Prefect," he said softly. "I will."

Beside him, Dace was looking at the slate too, upside down, with his head slightly on one side, as if the names were a column of figures he was adding up in his head.

---

Ostorian drew his lessons from the Drum, as a good soldier should. He wrote them down that night in his own hand, in a field book, and then he went down onto the Flats the next morning and began to put them into practice.

He learned that the Third would always attack behind a barrage, and that its soldiers would always advance in line, at the walk, the moment the barrage lifted. He learned that the Third's breachers would always follow its first wave onto the same ground, whatever had happened to the first wave. He learned that he could not trust the Third's spotters to tell the Third what they were seeing, and that the Third would believe its own overlay over its own periscopes. He reorganised the defence of the outer belt on that basis, thinning his forward trenches to a picket and putting more of his soldiers into the deep shelters, with orders to come up only when the Third's guns lifted and not a moment before.

He learned the price of his own counter-attack, too. He had sent nine thousand of the garrison across the ash and fewer than four thousand had come back, and the five thousand and forty who didn't would never come back from any vat. He had seventy cohorts of garrison when the siege began, and they were the only soldiers on the spur who could hold a line without the Lictors behind them, and he had thrown away eight of them in a single watch to win back two kilometres of ash he couldn't have held. He wrote that down too. For the rest of the siege, he counter-attacked only where his guns could cover the ground and where he could expect to hold what he took, and the Third's officers, who had expected the garrison to waste itself in such attacks, found that it seldom did.

On the spur, the first assault was received as a deliverance. The Archflamen's preachers told the thralls at every shift change that the Imperator had turned back the Empire at the Drum, that the ceiling had sent its soldiers across the ash in their hundreds of thousands and they had broken on the outer belt. The casting lines, which had been working at half their rate since the revolt, were restored to full production within a month. Ostorian heard the preachers and didn't correct them.

He didn't tell them about the five thousand and forty. Dace had already told them that those had gone to the Imperator.

**COMMAND POST OF THE 37TH TRENCH REGIMENT. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 90**

The Répertoire's report on the first assault on the outer belt arrived on the ninetieth cycle, at the end of the second watch, and Petra Anselm read it at her map table alone, at Corbel's suggestion, with the door of the command post shut.

It was long. It was thorough. It was, she thought, eminently fair. It found that the bombardment had been adequate in weight and inadequate in effect. It found that the depth of the Archflamen's shelters had been underestimated by about half. It found that the first wave had been sent forward at the moment the barrage lifted, giving the defenders the time they needed to man their guns. It found that the breachers of the second wave had been committed onto the same ground as the first, in the same manner, after the first had failed.

It recommended that future barrages lift in stages, with feints, so that the defenders could not tell when the infantry were coming. It recommended that the breachers be held back until a lodgement had been made. It recommended an urgent survey of the remaining nests on the outer belt by ground patrol, in addition to periscope. It recommended a great many other things.

It assigned no fault to the commanders. Colonel Anselm retained the *37th*.

She read that line three times, and then she put her face in her hands for a while.

When she looked up, the figures were still there, at the bottom of the report, where the Répertoire always put the figures. The first assault on the outer belt had cost the Third one hundred and eighty-four thousand transfers in seven cycles, against an estimate of ninety thousand. The *37th* had taken a hundred and eleven thousand of them. The *37th* would be rebuilt from the vats within two months. And no soldier of the *37th* had reached the enemy wire on Leaf 22-14 in the whole of the first cycle, and the breach the Répertoire had planned for had never come, and the *16th* Armoured Regiment had never left its reserve.

She thought about the report she'd written on the seventy-eighth. *I do not expect my first wave to meet much resistance.* She'd read the Tactica on it. She'd read the manuals. She'd looked at the boiled surface of the Drum through the periscopes for five cycles, and she'd believed what she saw, and the Archflamen's thralls had been sitting twenty metres underneath it the whole time with their guns, waiting for the whistle.

There was a knock at the door. It was Corbel.

"Ma'am," he said, and came in, and shut the door behind him. He had a slate in his hand, and he wasn't smiling. "There's something else. It's not in the report. The Répertoire's technical office sent it separately."

"What is it?"

"Nulls." He put the slate on the map table. "Two hundred and six of them. From the first assault. All from the *37th*, the *22nd* and the *33rd*, all from Leaf 22-14 and the two leaves either side." He hesitated. "The soldiers' implants fired, ma'am. Their patterns entered the noosphere. But the noosphere didn't deliver them to the Watchpoint. They came up empty."

Anselm looked at the slate. It was a list of names.

"How many of ours?"

"Ninety-one."

She ran her finger down the list. Halfway down the second page, she stopped.

*BRASK, TEO. TROOPER. 14TH COHORT, 2ND MANIPLE, 4TH SECTION. TRANSFER: NULL.*

"What does the technical office say?"

"Jammers," Corbel said. "The Archflamen's jammers have been broadcasting on the noosphere bands since the first barrage, and the pylons in the forward area took a beating in the counter-battery fire. And the Phlegethon, of course. The river's always taken a few uploads at Depth 40. Two hundred and six out of a hundred and eighty-four thousand is about a tenth of a percent. It's well under the ceiling."

"Is that what you think?"

Corbel was quiet for a moment.

"I think it's two hundred and six people, ma'am," he said.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 90, FOURTH WATCH**

Leda Corvis drafted the covering note to the Command herself, at the end of the fourth watch, and took it to the Lord-General's quarters on a slate rather than sending it down the corridor on the pane, which was a habit of hers when she wanted him to read something twice.

Castorre was at his desk with the Répertoire's report open in front of him and his reading lamp pulled down close over it. He had read the whole of the report, she knew, every page, and the technical office's list of nulls with it. He looked up when she came in and held out his hand for the slate without saying anything.

She watched him read it. It was four sentences long.

*The 3rd Siege Army has tested the outer belt of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal on Leaf 22-14 and found it stronger than estimated. The enemy's counter-attack of cycle 85 was repulsed with heavy loss to his garrison. The Répertoire's recommendations on barrage and the employment of breachers have been adopted. The plan will proceed with adjustments.*

"It's accurate," Castorre said.

"Every word, sir."

"It doesn't say that four hundred and sixty thousand soldiers failed to reach the wire of an unfinished trench belt."

"No, sir."

"It doesn't say that their gunners found Battery *26* at thirty-one kilometres in four hours and have been finding our batteries at that range every cycle since." He set the slate down on the report. "Or that the *37th*'s colonel read her own periscopes and believed them, and so did I."

Corvis didn't answer. She had written the note to leave those things out, and he knew she had, and she knew he would sign it anyway, and they had both been Athenians long enough not to pretend otherwise. A covering note to the Command was a document with a purpose. Its purpose was to keep the Command from taking the siege away from the Third before the Third had learned how to fight it, and every sentence that told the truth about the Drum was a sentence some Legate-Superior would read aloud at a table on the Watchpoint.

"The nulls," Castorre said.

"The technical office says jammers and the Phlegethon. It's a tenth of a percent, sir. Well under the ceiling."

"Varros?"

"Has added a page to his file."

Castorre nodded slowly. He picked up his stylus, and signed the note at the bottom in his small neat hand, and dated it, and gave it back to her.

"Send it," he said. "And Leda, move the College's liturgy of thanksgiving back a rotation. Tell the Flamen-Prior it's a matter of scheduling."

"It's in Rotation Four now, sir."

"Then put it in Rotation Five," Castorre said, and pulled the report back under his lamp, and went on reading it.

**THE FORWARD DEPOT, LANDING SHELF KAPPA. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 93**

Kel Imber was at the vat-halls at Kappa when the bodies of the *14th* Cohort's transfers came up from the Watchpoint, and he waited at the door of the halls for Teo Brask for three days.

They came in batches, the transfers, on the relay barges down from the low geometry, in long white crates with the soldier's name and number on the lid, and the orderlies at Kappa opened the crates and brought the soldiers out still groggy from the vats, wrapped in blankets, and walked them across the hall to the issue depot where they'd get new plate and new rifles and go back down the Stair. Imber stood by the door of the hall and watched every one of them come through. Some of them he knew. They saw him standing there and nodded, or said *fucking Drum*, or didn't say anything. Centurion Voss came through on the second day. She stopped when she saw him, and looked at him for a moment, and put her hand on his shoulder, and walked on without speaking.

On the third day, an orderly came over to him.

"You're the one waiting for Brask," she said.

"Yes."

"You'd better come with me."

She took him to the back of the hall, where there was a row of crates on trestles that hadn't been opened yet, and opened one of them, and stood back.

Brask was lying in it. He looked exactly as he always had, the same face, the same nineteen years, a little cleaner than Imber had ever seen him, naked under a white sheet. His eyes were open. He was breathing. His chest went up and down, slow and steady.

He wasn't there.

Imber could see it at once. He didn't know how. There was nothing wrong with the body. It was a good body, the vats made good bodies, and its eyes were open and it was breathing. But there was nobody behind the eyes. They looked up at the ceiling of the hall without moving and without seeing, and when Imber leaned over the crate and said, "Teo," they didn't move, and when he said it again, louder, they still didn't.

"Null," the orderly said gently. "I'm sorry. The pattern didn't come through. The body grew, but there was nothing to put in it. It happens."

"How often?"

"Not often." She looked down at Brask. "More than it used to."

"What happens to him now?"

The orderly didn't answer for a moment.

"He'll go to the Flamens," she said at last. "They'll say the rite over him. And then the Watchpoint will take the body back for the nectar." She put her hand on Imber's arm. "He's not in there, Trooper. I'm sorry. He went somewhere else."

"Where?"

"Nobody knows," the orderly said. "The river, they say."

**THE PALACE TERRACE, THE ACROPOLIS. ROTATION ONE, CYCLES 79 TO 86**

Mirren heard them go over the seven cycles of the first assault, most of them while she sat at the Archflamen's table on the terrace of the palace with a plate of bread in front of her and Dace beside her.

On the first night she'd been hearing the birds going up from the Flats since the fourth bell. There had been so many of them that she couldn't count them anymore, thousands and thousands of them, going up off the Drum in long rising cries and away north and up toward the ceiling, toward wherever the birds went. It had been like standing under a waterfall. She'd had to put her hands over her ears, though it didn't help, and in the end Dace had taken her hands down off her ears and held them, gently, and asked her to listen.

"Not to all of them," he said. "To the ones that don't go home."

So she'd listened. And among the thousands of birds going up off the Drum and away toward the ceiling, she'd heard the others.

They started to rise like the rest. Then something caught them, low down over the Flats, a long way short of the ceiling, and held them. She could feel them beating their wings against it, the same way the big bright bird near the bridge had done on the night of the fighting, and then she felt them go quiet. They didn't go home. They didn't go anywhere. They hung there in the air over the Flats, quiet, as the bird near the bridge still hung, waiting.

She went on hearing them for seven cycles, a few at a time, among the thousands that went home, and on the seventh night, when the shells had stopped and the Drum was quiet, Dace asked her.

"How many?"

She didn't want to tell him. She didn't know why. She'd told him everything else, every night, for months.

"Mirren," he said, very softly. "How many?"

"Two hundred and six," she said.

Dace squeezed her hands, and let them go, and took out the small grey vial, and tipped three drops onto the back of his hand. Then he smiled, and wrote the number down in his small black book, under a column of other numbers, and closed it.
