## Chapter 18: The Demus Rim

*"Hold until relieved."*

*Order of the Eighth Line Phalanx to the* 54th *Trench Regiment, Rotation Eight, Cycle 360*

---

**LEAF 61-29, THE RIM OF THE DEMUS GORGE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 357**

Tank Centurion Lio Barca had commanded the *3rd* Company of the *63rd* Armoured Regiment for six years, and in all that time he had never once taken it into a battle where he couldn't see the enemy's trenches before he started.

He couldn't see anything now. He stood on the rear deck of his Thureos on the rim of the Demus Gorge, in the last light of the three hundred and fifty-seventh cycle, with his field-glass to his eyes, and looked south across fifty kilometres of empty ash toward the place where the drowned host was supposed to be, and saw nothing but the ash and the haze and, far off, the black line of the old second belt where it came round the head of the Gorge.

"Anything?" his driver asked from the hatch below him. Her name was Petrona Sabb, and she had driven for him for four of the six years, and she had a voice like gravel in a bucket.

"Nothing."

"Recon?"

"No recon," Barca said. He lowered the glass. "There wasn't time. The Phalanx says."

Sabb said nothing for a moment. Then she said something under her breath that he chose not to hear.

The Sixth Assault Phalanx had begun to move on the three hundred and nineteenth cycle. It had been in reserve behind the Ninth Line Phalanx when the outriders reported the *Lament of Ixion*'s holds open, nineteen cycles before, and it had been ordered out of the line at once to meet what had come out of them, and to take whatever extra tanks it could from the Fourth Assault Phalanx on the way. It had taken twenty-two. It had taken them, and the tank crews from the Fourth who came with them, and the infantry of three Trench Regiments, and the *37th* from the Seventh Line Phalanx, lent for the purpose, and its field guns on their tractors, and all of it had come south across the Flats in nineteen cycles to the rim of the Demus Gorge, to Leaf 61-29, and stopped.

And in those nineteen cycles the drowned host had come fifty kilometres north and occupied the old second belt as it came.

Barca had heard the briefing that morning. Everyone had. The drowned host was on the old second belt behind the Eighth and Ninth Line Phalanxes, and the Archflamen's army was attacking the Third's front, and if the drowned host came on another thirty kilometres north it would cut the Eighth and the Ninth off from the rest of the Third. The only thing in its way was the Sixth Assault Phalanx, on the Demus Rim. If the Sixth failed, the Third would pay heavily for it. The Phalanx-General had said so in exactly those words.

"How many of them?" Sabb said.

"The outriders saw nine Behemoths come out of the hulk. And a host. They didn't count the host."

"Nine Behemoths."

"Drowned ones."

"What do we kill Behemoths with?"

Barca pointed back along the rim, to where the Phalanx's few Behemoth-hunters sat in a long row in the dusk behind the tank companies: four of them, long low Colossus hulls each carrying a single enormous lance, longer than the hull itself, the Gigantomachy lances that the Forge had built centuries ago to kill engines of the kind they would meet tomorrow. Beside them sat a company of ordinary Colossi, twelve of them, the great slab-sided super-heavies with their twin guns.

"Those," he said.

Sabb looked at them.

"Four," she said.

"Four."

"For nine."

Barca didn't answer. He climbed down off the rear deck and went round to the front of the Thureos and stood there for a while in the dusk, with his hand on the cold armour of the glacis, looking south.

It was a good tank. The Thureos was the Third's main battle tank and had been for three hundred years, a heavy, low, broad machine with a long gun in a squat turret and armour that would turn anything the Arsenal had on the second belt. It had carried him through the outer belt and the Drum and the Big Push. It had never failed him. He had never once doubted it.

He had never taken it against a Behemoth.

---

The Phalanx-General gave the orders at the dark bell. The tanks would go first, by companies, in line abreast, at a walking pace, in parade order, with the Behemoth-hunters and the Colossi among them. Behind the tanks would come the infantry of the Sixth's Trench Regiments on foot, the *37th* among them. Behind the infantry, the field guns, still on their tractors, to come into action wherever the tanks found the enemy. There were no heavy guns. There had been no time to bring the Phaethons forward, or their shells.

"Parade order," Sabb said, when he told her. "Line abreast. Walking pace."

"That's the order."

"Against a trench line. That's how we go against a trench line."

"I know."

"They haven't got a trench line, Lio. They've got nine Behemoths."

"I know," Barca said. "Go to sleep, Petrona."

She went. He didn't. He sat on the rear deck of the Thureos with his back against the turret and looked up at the dark, at the ash cloud and the low geometry above it, where the drowned ships were, and thought about what he had heard from the regiments that had been on the Flats on the two hundred and seventy-eighth cycle, when the landing craft came down out of the storm.

They had come from above. That was the thing every soldier who had been there said first. They had come from above, out of the low geometry, and nothing the Third had could fight them there.

He looked at the long gun of the Thureos, pointing south, level with the ground. It could traverse a full circle. It could elevate eighteen degrees.

---

**LEAF 61-29 TO LEAF 63-31, THE DEMUS RIM. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 358**

The battle began at the first bell, and for the first hour it went as the briefing had said it would.

The Sixth went forward across the ash in long straight lines, company beside company, at a walking pace, exactly as the Third's tanks had gone forward against the Arsenal's trenches for eight rotations and against every trench line on every front for three hundred years. Barca's company was in the second line, in the centre, with the *2nd* Company on his left and the *4th* on his right, and the first line two hundred metres ahead of him. Behind him, through his periscope, he could see the infantry of the *37th* coming on in their own long lines, walking, with their rifles across their chests. Behind them, the field guns, on their tractors. The ash rose up behind every tank in a long grey plume and hung in the air, and the whole of the Sixth Assault Phalanx went south across the Demus Rim under a single great grey cloud of its own making, which anything watching from above could have seen from a hundred kilometres away.

Something was watching from above.

The first blow fell at the end of the first hour.

Barca heard it before he saw it. A sound came down out of the ash cloud, high up, a long thin shrieking, thousands of voices together, metallic, the shrieking of metal birds, and it came down out of the dark over the Sixth, and he looked up through the commander's vision block and saw them.

Stymphalians. He had seen them only in the recognition books, and in the old paintings in the halls of the Academy: the bronze birds of the deep, the size of a man, with feathers of bronze that they could throw. They came down out of the river cloud in their thousands, a great dark wheeling swarm, so many that they blotted out the faint red glow of the ash cloud, and as they came down they threw their feathers.

The feathers came down on the Sixth like rain.

Barca heard them strike the turret. A long hard rattling clatter of iron on iron, and then a deeper crack as a bigger one struck. Through the vision block he saw them hit the infantry of the *37th* behind him, walking in their long lines in the open, and saw the lines break up. He saw them strike the tank on his left, the commander of which had been standing up in his hatch to look, and the commander was gone.

"Close up," he said on the company net. "All hatches. Close up."

Then the Lanterns came.

They came down out of the river cloud behind the swarm, the drowned ships' old Lanterns, crusted and patched and flown by crews who had died a long time ago, and they came in low over the Sixth's lines in pairs, and they fired.

Barca watched a Thureos of the first line, two hundred metres ahead of him, take a rocket through its engine deck from directly above, where its armour was thinnest, and burn.

Then another.

Then another.

"Traverse," he said. "Traverse and elevate. All guns. Engage the Lanterns."

"Can't, sir," his gunner said. His gunner was a boy named Tiberio Fenn who had been with him eight months. His voice was high and tight. "Eighteen degrees. That's the limit. They're above that. I can't reach them."

Barca looked out of the vision block at the Lanterns wheeling over the Sixth, firing down on the tanks one after another, and the swarm above them, throwing its feathers. The Sixth's tanks had no weapons that could engage them. Nobody had thought they would need any. Nothing had attacked the Third from the air over the Flats since the first rotation, when the ceiling-lances had cleared the Stymphalians out of the dark as fast as they came. Now the Archflamen held the lances, and the drowned held the low geometry, and the Sixth Assault Phalanx was crossing the Demus Rim in parade order under an enemy sky.

The formations broke. He watched them break, all along the line. Companies that had been advancing in long straight lines scattered under the attack, tanks swerving and turning and trying to find cover that wasn't there on the open ash, and as they scattered the Lanterns picked them off one at a time, and tank after tank was set burning.

Within an hour the Third had lost control of the air over the whole of the southern Flats.

---

The leading companies pressed on.

Barca never knew afterward whether anybody had ordered it, or whether the commanders of the first line had simply gone on, told to go on and never told to stop. He followed them. He didn't know what else to do. The *3rd* Company had lost two tanks to the Lanterns, and the *2nd* on his left had lost five, and the infantry of the *37th* behind him had gone to ground under the feathers and were coming on again in scattered groups, crouching, running. The first line went on south across the ash under the swarm, and he went on after it.

They reached the drowned host's first line at the end of the second hour.

It was dug in. The Forsworn had dug it, along the old second belt, using the Third's own abandoned trenches and the Archflamen's old ones, and they had dug it well. The first line of the Sixth came up to it and the guns of the drowned host opened fire on it from the trenches, and the first line went into the trenches anyway, with the field guns coming into action on their tractors behind it and firing over its head. Barca saw a Thureos of the first line roll over the parapet of the drowned host's forward trench and crush it flat, and the drowned soldiers in it, in their rotted armour of a dozen centuries, go under its tracks. He saw another. He saw the infantry of the *37th* come up behind them and go into the trench with the bayonet.

For a few minutes, in the middle of the second hour, it seemed that the first positions had been overrun.

Then the Behemoths came over the old second belt.

---

He saw the first of them through the vision block, and for a long moment his mind would not accept what his eyes were telling it.

It was too big. It was simply too big. It came up over the line of the old second belt two kilometres away, a walking tower sixty metres high, on two great jointed legs that each took a long time to lift and a long time to put down, and every time one of them came down Barca felt it through the tracks and the hull and the seat under him. Its body was a fortress. Its plating was black, a deep dull black that drank the light, and it was crusted with the river bed and patched with the white ice like everything that came up out of the river, and along its flanks and on its shoulders and its great blunt head there were guns, more guns than he could count.

And from somewhere in its body, a sound came. Long and low and rising and falling, a dirge, played on horns so huge that the sound of them went through the Thureos's armour and into his chest and made his ribs hum.

Behind it, over the second belt, came the second. Then the third.

Nine.

"Gods below," Fenn said, quietly, in the gunner's seat.

The tallest of them fired.

---

Barca would try many times afterward to describe the Phlegethon-cannon, to the board of inquiry and to the Sixth's surviving officers and once, late at night, to his sister, and he would never succeed.

It threw the river. That was all he could say. A great gun on the Behemoth's shoulder opened, and what came out of it was the Phlegethon, or a piece of it, a long bright arc of orange-white fire that was also liquid, that splashed and poured and ran. It came down on the first line of the Sixth, on the tanks in the drowned host's forward trench, and where it came down the tanks melted. He saw a Thureos take the edge of it across its turret, and the turret sagged and ran and fell in on itself, molten, and the crew inside didn't come out. The fire ran on along the trench, pouring and splashing, and the infantry of the *37th* in the trench were in it.

The other Behemoths fired too. Phlegethon-cannon, and others: great hammer-guns, the Forge's own pattern, drowned versions of the guns the Forge's own Behemoths carried, that threw shells the size of a Thureos. The first line of the Sixth came apart. Barca watched it come apart through the vision block, tank after tank, one after another, along the whole front.

"Fire," he said. "All guns. On the nearest Behemoth. Fire."

The *3rd* Company fired. Every gun in it, on the nearest of the drowned engines, at two kilometres. He watched the shells go across the ash and strike its black plating.

They didn't strike it. A metre from the plating, every one of them, the air shimmered, and there was a flash, white-blue, and the shell burst in the air short of the hull and fell away in burning pieces.

"Wards," Fenn said. "Aegis-wards, sir. Forge pattern. They're Forge-built. They've got Aegis-wards."

Barca knew what Aegis-wards were. Every officer of the Third knew. They were the Forge of Hephaestus's own protection for its engines, laid in the plating by the Forge's keyers when the engine was built, and they would turn anything the Third possessed. The Forge had built its Behemoths to walk the deep holds and crush what rose from the rivers, and it had built them so that nothing that rose from the rivers could hurt them.

The river had them now. And the wards still worked.

---

The Gigantomachy lances were the only weapons the Third possessed that had been built to kill a Behemoth, and the Sixth had committed them piecemeal in the first wave, and Barca saw the last of them die.

It found a firing position on a cinder bank to his left, half a kilometre away, a low hummock of black slag where it could hull-down and see the tallest of the Behemoths. He watched it climb the bank, grinding, its long lance swinging round. He watched it stop and settle and aim.

It fired. The beam was white, so white that it left a long purple line burned across his vision, and it went across the ash in a single straight line and struck the tallest Behemoth full in the chest.

The wards took it. He saw them flare, white-blue, a great shimmering shield round the Behemoth's chest, and the beam struck the shield and spread across it and went out.

It fired again. The wards took it again.

It fired a third time. The wards took it a third time, and the Behemoth turned, slowly, its great head swinging round, and looked at the cinder bank.

Then the Behemoth fired, and the cinder bank and the Gigantomachy lance on it were gone.

---

On the left, the drowned host's own armour came into the fight.

Barca saw it through the vision block, coming out of the smoke beyond the old second belt: tanks. Thureos, and Hoplons behind them, but of a pattern he had never seen, older, heavier, crusted and patched like everything from the river. A pattern the Forge had not built in a thousand years. They came into the fight on the left of the Sixth's line with the Forsworn commanding them, and they went into the Sixth's stalled companies on the flank, and the Sixth's attack began to come apart.

"Centurion." It was the regiment's net, crackling. The colonel's voice. "All companies. Withdraw. Withdraw to the Demus Rim. Reserve company is coming forward to cover you. Withdraw."

"Petrona," Barca said. "Back."

The Thureos lurched. Sabb brought it round, hard, in a tight turn on the ash, and began to take it back north, toward the rim, and Barca turned in his seat and looked through the rear vision block at what was behind them.

The reserve company was coming forward. Sixteen Thureos of the *63rd*'s *7th* Company, in a long line abreast, at a walking pace, in parade order, coming south across the ash toward the drowned host to cover the withdrawal. Barca knew their commander. Centurion Aulo Grisk. They had been at the Academy on Callistus together. They had shared a room for a year.

He watched Grisk's company go past him, going south as he went north, and on into the smoke toward the Behemoths.

None of them came back.

---

**THE DEMUS RIM. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 358, DARK WATCH**

The Thureos died three kilometres short of the rim.

Barca never knew what hit it. Something from above, he thought afterward. It struck the engine deck with a great flat crash and the whole tank jumped and the lights went out and the cabin filled with smoke, and Sabb was shouting, and Fenn was shouting, and the loader, a big slow girl named Annis who had never said anything in eight months, was screaming.

"Out," Barca said. "Out. Everyone. Out."

They got out. He didn't remember how. He remembered being on the ash beside the tank in the dark, with the tank burning behind him and lighting the ash red for twenty metres around, and Sabb on one side of him and Fenn on the other, and Annis lying on the ash a little way off with her hands over her face, and the sound of the swarm still shrieking somewhere overhead.

"Walk," he said. "North. Keep low. Walk."

They walked.

It took them all night. The ground on the Demus Rim was covered with the wreckage of the Sixth, burning hulls and dead soldiers and the abandoned field guns on their overturned tractors, and with wounded, and with tank crews like themselves, in twos and threes, trying to find their own lines in the dark. Twice they lay flat in the ash while something went past them, once a Lantern low overhead, once a long column of the drowned host moving north in the dark, a hundred metres off, in rotted armour, silent. Barca lay on his face in the ash and watched them go past, and saw what they were doing. They were dragging the Sixth's damaged tanks away. Every one they could salvage. Long lines of the drowned, harnessed to the hulls with chains, hauling them north across the ash toward the old second belt, toward the river.

"What do they want them for?" Fenn whispered.

Barca thought about the drowned host's own armour, coming out of the smoke on the left: the Thureos of a pattern the Forge had not built in a thousand years.

"The same thing they want everything for," he said.

At the third bell of the dark watch they came on a group of infantry sitting in a shell-hole on the rim, and a voice challenged them out of the dark.

"Who's that?"

"*63rd* Armoured," Barca said. "*3rd* Company. Four of us. Who's that?"

"*37th*," the voice said. "*14th* Cohort."

They climbed down into the shell-hole. There were nine soldiers in it, sitting with their backs to the walls, with their rifles across their knees, and an optio, a thin grey woman with one arm in a sling and a face blank with exhaustion.

"Where's the rest of your cohort, Optio?" Barca said.

The optio looked at him for a long moment.

"This is the rest," she said. "And two more we lost on the way back. We went in with two hundred, Centurion." She looked at the nine soldiers round her in the dark. "We're eleven."

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 359**

The Sixth Assault Phalanx lost seventy-four tanks on the Demus Rim, twelve of them Colossi, and twenty-nine field guns. Its infantry losses came to thirty-one thousand transfers, four thousand of them null.

None of the drowned Behemoths had fallen.

Corvis wrote that last line on its own at the bottom of the slate, and took it in to the Lord-General, and stood in front of his desk while he read it.

He read it twice. Then he put it down.

"The worst armoured defeat in the Third's history," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Tell me why."

She had known he would ask. She had spent the night working it out, from the reports of the Sixth's surviving officers and the Répertoire's reconstruction and her own map, and she told him plainly.

"We sent them against an enemy nobody had measured, sir. Without reconnaissance. Without heavy guns. Without any means of fighting what came down on them from the river cloud. They went across open ground in parade order, line abreast, the Third's drill against a trench line, and against Behemoths it was the worst formation they could have chosen. And we committed the Gigantomachy lances piecemeal in the first wave, and lost them in the first hour, and they were the only weapons on the Flats built to kill a Behemoth." She paused. "We didn't underestimate the drowned host, sir. We didn't estimate it at all. We told the Sixth to meet it, and the Sixth did what it was told."

Castorre sat with his hands flat on the desk for a long time.

"And now?"

"The drowned host is on the old second belt behind the Eighth and the Ninth Line Phalanxes, sir. There's nothing between it and the Ninth's rear. We have no armoured reserve that can stop it." She paused. "The Behemoths can't be killed with anything we have on the Flats. The only engines that could meet them are the Empire's own Behemoths."

"And the Forge has never sent a cohort of them below Depth 30."

"No, sir."

Castorre looked at the map table through the open door of his office, at the little brown lights of the drowned host on the old second belt in the south, and the little grey lights of the Eighth and the Ninth in front of them, between them and the inner belt.

"Then we give up the south," he said.

---

**HEADQUARTERS OF THE EIGHTH LINE PHALANX. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 360**

The order went out on the three hundred and sixtieth cycle. The Eighth and Ninth Line Phalanxes were to abandon their positions on the southern face of the inner belt and swing west, out of the path of the drowned host, into new lines alongside the Seventh. The southern half of the Third's ring around the inner belt would be given up.

And one regiment would stay behind.

Legate Ashcombe read that part of the order three times in his headquarters dugout. Then he put it down and looked at it for a long while.

The rearguard would hold the southern face of the line at the Lethe Cut, which could not be crossed, while the rest of the Eighth and the Ninth withdrew past it. It would hold as a pocket, with the Cut at its back, and it would hold the drowned host for as long as it survived, while the other regiments turned to meet it. Once it was cut off, it couldn't be supplied. The drowned host would overrun it before long. Sooner, if it brought up the Behemoths.

The order said none of that. Every officer in the Eighth who read it knew what it meant.

Ashcombe chose the *54th*.

He told his chief of staff afterward that he had chosen it as the Eighth's freshest regiment, raised from the ration rolls of the Lethe Hold in Rotation Two, and had been on the Flats for less than a rotation and hadn't been worn down by the Big Push. Because it was already on the left of the Eighth's line, against the Lethe Cut, where the pocket would have to be. Because it was the only regiment of the Eighth that could be spared without breaking the line that would be left.

His chief of staff, who had served with him for nine years, said nothing at all.

**THE COMMAND DUGOUT OF THE *54TH*, LEAF 54-49, AGAINST THE LETHE CUT. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 360**

Colonel Corin Vadas read the order in his dugout at the second bell of the morning watch, standing at his table with his reading glasses on the end of his nose and a cup of tea going cold beside him, and when he got to the end of it he read it again from the beginning.

He was fifty-five. He had been an administrator in the Lethe Hold's ration office for twenty years before the shells stopped coming in Rotation Two, and when the Hold had raised a regiment off the ration rolls to send to the Third, nobody else had wanted it, and the Directorate had given it to him with a colonel's grade attached. Somebody had to have it. He had never commanded anything larger than a ration office in his life. He had never been in a battle. He had come down the Stair onto the Flats with the *54th* at the start of Rotation Seven with fourteen thousand bakers and clerks and ore-lift hands and dockhands behind him, and he had told them every day since then to dig in deep and keep their heads down and watch the wire, and nothing could touch them.

He put the order down on the table and took off his reading glasses and polished them on his sleeve for a long time.

Then he picked up the handset of the relay set on his table and called the Eighth Line Phalanx's headquarters, and asked for the Legate's chief of staff, and waited.

"Colonel Vadas," the chief of staff said, after a while.

"Thank you for speaking to me," Vadas said. "I've read the order. I understand it. I want you to know that I understand it, and so will my officers, and so will my soldiers, and we'll do what it says." He paused. "I've only one question. How long are we to hold?"

There was a silence on the line.

"Until relieved, Colonel," the chief of staff said.

"Yes," Vadas said. "I see. Thank you." He paused again. "And when might relief be expected?"

The silence on the line went on for a long time. It went on so long that Vadas thought the line had been cut, and he was about to put the handset down when he heard, faintly, at the other end, somebody breathing.

Then the line clicked, and went dead.

Vadas stood at his table with the handset in his hand for a while. Then he put it back in its cradle, carefully, and put his reading glasses back on, and picked up the order again, and read it a third time, and then he went up the dugout stair into the grey light to tell his officers.

---

**THE LINES OF THE *54TH*, LEAF 54-49. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 363**

The *52nd* and the *50th* came through his lines on the three hundred and sixty-third cycle, going north-west toward the Seventh, and Vadas stood at the junction of the communication trenches on Leaf 54-49 and watched them go past.

They went past for nine hours. Two regiments, with their guns on tractors and their wounded on stretchers and their stores on their backs, in long grey columns down the communication trenches and across the open ash behind them, out of the southern face of the inner belt where they had fought since the Big Push, north-west toward the new line. They didn't hurry. They went steadily, in good order, with their officers walking beside them. Some of the soldiers looked at the *54th* in its trenches as they went past. Most of them didn't.

Colonel Lowe came through at the end of the *52nd*'s column, on foot, with her optio beside her. She stopped when she saw him.

"Colonel Vadas."

"Colonel Lowe." He smiled at her, the round cheerful smile that he had worn all his life and found he could still put on. "I hear you took a ridge."

"For an hour." She looked at him for a long moment. Her grey eyes were steady. "I heard about the order."

"Yes," Vadas said. "Well. Somebody has to, I suppose. And we're the freshest. And we're already here." He looked out over the parapet, south, toward the Lethe Cut, which he couldn't see from the trench but which he knew was there, three kilometres off, a long black gash in the ash with a river running at the bottom of it that nobody in the *54th* had ever seen. "And the Legate says the Cut will protect our backs."

Lowe said nothing for a while.

"Dig deep," she said at last. "Deeper than you think you need to. Then deeper again. Roof everything you can with steel. Put your sentries facing the ceiling as well as the front." She paused. "And when they come, Colonel, don't let your soldiers freeze when the flares go up. Tell them to keep moving. Whatever they're doing, keep moving. The ones that freeze are the ones that get seen."

Vadas looked at her. Then he took out the little notebook he carried in his breast pocket, in which he wrote down everything, as he had written everything down all his life in the ration office, and wrote it down. *Dig deep. Then deeper. Steel. Sentries to ceiling. Don't freeze at flares. Keep moving.*

"Thank you," he said. "I'll tell them."

She held out her hand. He took it. Her hand was hard and thin and cold.

"Good luck, Colonel," she said.

"And you," Vadas said.

She went on, after her regiment, north-west, and her optio went with her, and Vadas stood at the junction of the communication trenches and watched her go until she was out of sight.

Then he went back to his dugout and sat down at his table and opened his notebook and began to work out, carefully, as he had worked out the ration allocations of the Lethe Hold for twenty years, exactly how long fourteen thousand soldiers could live on what the departing regiments were leaving behind them.

---

**THE NEW LINE, ORE HEAD TO THE SEVENTH. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 363 TO 375**

The withdrawal was carried out with more skill than the battles that had made it necessary.

Corvis watched it on the map table for twelve cycles, and she was proud of it, and she was never able to say so to anyone without sounding mad.

Hundreds of thousands of soldiers were on the move across the Flats at once, with their tanks and their guns, digging new lines as fast as they could while the drowned host advanced toward them. The Seventh Line Phalanx fell back to a new line anchored on the ruins of Ore Head, with its front along the old ore road to the south-east, and the remains of both Assault Phalanxes were placed behind it as the Third's last reserve. The Eighth and the Ninth came in beside it, from the south, swinging west round the end of the inner belt under the guns of the Archflamen's gunners and the raids of the drowned. The Third's regiments, which had faced a single front for eight rotations, now faced every direction at once. The southern Flats were in pieces.

The Third had spent six hundred years learning to dig, and it dug. The regiments dug the new line from Ore Head to the Seventh's old positions in eleven cycles, under the guns of the inner belt, while the *54th* held the Lethe Cut behind them. The Eighth and the Ninth brought out most of their guns and nearly all their soldiers. They abandoned their stores on the southern Flats, two rotations of shells and rations, and the Third's engineers set charges in the dumps before they left.

On the three hundred and seventy-fifth cycle, the drowned host came up to the abandoned works on the southern face of the inner belt and found them empty.

The charges in the dumps went off as the first of the drowned reached them. Corvis saw it on the map table, a long line of white flashes along the southern Flats, one after another, eleven of them, and heard the reports come in from the outriders watching from the old second belt: the dumps going up in great columns of fire and smoke that lit the underside of the ash cloud for a hundred kilometres. It killed a great many of the drowned, and the rest came on.

And then, for the first time since the Tabularium fell, the Archflamen's cohorts came out of the inner belt to meet the drowned host on the open ash, and the Arsenal's army and its allies from the river stood together on the Cinder Flats.

Corvis looked at it on the map table for a long time: the brown lights of the Archflamen's cohorts and the brown lights of the drowned host, side by side, on the southern face of the inner belt, where the Eighth and the Ninth had stood.

Any chance of taking the Arsenal within the Répertoire's twelve rotations was gone. She knew it, and the Lord-General knew it, and the Command at Watchpoint Hades knew it. It would take everything the Empire could send merely to keep the siege of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal from ending in defeat.

---

**THE POCKET ON THE LETHE CUT, LEAF 54-49. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 375 TO 380**

The drowned host didn't attack the *54th*.

Vadas waited for it every watch. He had dug in, as Colonel Lowe had told him: deep, then deeper, with steel on everything he could roof, and sentries facing the ceiling as well as the front. The pocket was nine kilometres wide with the Cut at its back. It was held by fourteen thousand soldiers who had been ration clerks and ore-lift hands two rotations before, under officers who had never commanded in battle, with the stores the departing regiments couldn't carry, a battery of divine mortars without keyers to key them, and the promise of relief. He had told his soldiers about the flares. He had walked the whole of the pocket every night, with his notebook and a lantern, and talked to every section, and told them not to freeze.

The drowned host passed them by.

He watched it from the parapet on the three hundred and seventy-sixth cycle, through a field-glass he had borrowed from the *52nd*'s quartermaster and never given back: a long dark column of the drowned, moving north across the ash on the far side of the abandoned works, a kilometre off, in their rotted armour. It went past the pocket without turning, north, toward the new line and the Third, and left behind it, on a low rise of ash a kilometre from the *54th*'s wire, a picket. Twenty of the drowned, and a single tall figure in black among them who stood quite still at the top of the rise, facing the pocket, and never moved.

It was there the next cycle, and the next.

"What's it doing, Colonel?" his adjutant asked him. She was a ration clerk from the Lethe Hold named Ottilie Crane, twenty-two, who had worked in the office next to his for three years and had been made a lieutenant as the only person in the *54th* who knew how the forms worked.

"Watching us," Vadas said.

"Why doesn't it attack?"

He thought about it.

"I don't think it needs to," he said.

---

The mist began on the three hundred and seventy-sixth cycle.

It came up out of the Lethe Cut in the dark watch. Vadas saw it first from the parapet at the south end of the pocket, where the trench ran along the lip of the Cut and you could look down into it, if you dared, into the long black gash in the ash with the river at the bottom that nobody in the *54th* had ever seen. He looked down into it on his rounds at the third bell, as he did every night, and saw the mist coming up.

It was a soft pale white, unlike the grey of the ash or the brown of the seep-storms, and it came up out of the Cut slowly, in long curling tendrils, and spread out along the bottom of the trench at the south end of the pocket, a few centimetres deep, and lay there, quite still. It was cold. He put his hand down into it and felt the cold go into his fingers.

It was gone by the first bell.

It came back the next dark watch, thicker. Ankle-deep along the south trench. The next, it was knee-deep, and it had spread north along the communication trenches into the pocket, and the sentries at the south end said that when it was at its thickest they couldn't see their own boots. The next, it reached the command dugout.

Vadas reported it to the Eighth Line Phalanx. He wrote the report himself, carefully, in his small neat ration-office hand, every dark watch, and sent it up the relay. *Mist from the Lethe Cut in the dark watch, thicker than the previous night. Cold. Disperses by the first bell. No enemy activity. Picket on the rise unchanged. Request instructions.* He wrote it on the three hundred and seventy-sixth cycle, and the three hundred and seventy-seventh, and every cycle after.

The Eighth logged the reports, and sent no reply.

It had nothing to send.

On the three hundred and eightieth cycle, at the third bell of the dark watch, Vadas stood at the south end of the pocket on the lip of the Lethe Cut with the mist up to his waist, white and cold and perfectly still, and looked down into the Cut, and for the first time since the *54th* had come to the Flats he thought he could hear the river at the bottom of it.

It was faint, and a long way down. It sounded, he thought, standing there in the cold white mist with his notebook in his hand, a great deal like somebody singing.

---

**WATCHPOINT HADES. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 380**

The Command's alarm at the events of Rotation Eight was matched by its confusion about what to do.

The Third had been the Empire's anchor at Depth 40 for six hundred years, and in a single rotation it had broken the second belt, lost its fleet, lost its reserve and been driven from half its ring by an enemy that nobody in the Command had believed existed. The Pontifical College demanded that the Archflamen be destroyed whatever it cost, since an Archflamen allied to the drowned dead of the Phlegethon was now a matter discussed in every chapel of the theatre. The Directorate demanded an accounting of twenty-three million transfers. The Répertoire reported that the drowned host's advance on the southern Flats, if nothing checked it, would reach the relay pylons at Landing Shelf Kappa within a rotation, after which the Third could neither be supplied nor reinforced, and every soldier it lost would be lost for good.

It also reported that the null rate on the Flats had risen to one transfer in eighteen.

The Command didn't need the Répertoire to tell it what that meant for the Third's soldiers.

It asked the Répertoire what would be needed to save the siege. The Répertoire's answer was delivered on the three hundred and eightieth cycle, and it was short. A new commander for the Third. And behind him, the Behemoths of the Forge of Hephaestus.

The Command began to look for both.

---

On Quarterdeck Nineteen, the staff counted what remained.

Corvis did most of the counting. The Third had lost the southern half of its ring, its fleet over the Flats and its reserve at Segment Twelve. Its supply depended on cables it could no longer protect. It still had more than two million soldiers on the Flats, nine months of shells at the rationed rate and a line that held. It had no plan for winning the siege.

The Command at Watchpoint Hades had begun to draw one up.

Castorre was not consulted.

Corvis found that out by accident, on the three hundred and eighty-second cycle, from a clerk of the Directorate who called her on the relay to ask for a figure and mentioned in passing, taking it for common knowledge, that the Command's planning staff had been working on the new plan for the Flats since the three hundred and seventieth cycle and would need the Third's shell returns by the end of the rotation.

She put the handset down and sat at her desk for a long time.

Then she got up and went to the Lord-General's office, and stood in the open doorway, and looked at him.

He was at his desk, with his reading lamp on and a slate in front of him that he wasn't reading. He looked up at her.

"They're writing a plan, sir," she said. "The Command. For the Flats. They've been writing it for twelve cycles."

Castorre looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked down at the slate in front of him.

"Yes," he said. "I thought they might be."

"They haven't asked you."

"No."

"Sir..."

"It's all right, Leda." He put the slate down, and took off his reading glasses, and rubbed his eyes. "I've had eight rotations. I've had twenty-three million transfers. I've broken two belts and lost a fleet and a reserve and half the ring, and the drowned are on the Flats and the river's coming up and the null rate is one in eighteen." He put the glasses back on. "If I were the Command, I wouldn't ask me either."

Corvis said nothing.

"Get me the shell returns," Castorre said. "All of them. Up to the minute. They'll need them, whoever has them." He looked back down at the slate. "We'll give them everything we've got. That's all we can do now."

"Yes, sir."

She went back to her desk and began to gather the shell returns. Outside her window, far below, the Flats lay grey and smoking under the ash cloud, and in the south, at the Lethe Cut, where she couldn't see it, a white mist was beginning to rise.
