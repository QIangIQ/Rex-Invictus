## Chapter 8: Saturation

*"All artillery, rotary, and missile batteries, continuous fire saturation ahead of advancing infantry."*

*Répertoire du Savoir Athénien, general verdict, Rotation Four, cycle 3*

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 3, SECOND WATCH**

Leda Corvis was halfway through a sentence about boot-soles when the sky to the north caught fire.

She was in the conference hall with the logistics staff, standing at the long basalt table with a slate in her hand, listening to the deputy quartermaster of the Third explain for the eleventh time why the consignment of boot-soles that had been missing since Rotation Minus Two had now been found, in a depot at Kappa, under a consignment of tent-pegs, and why the boot-soles were the wrong size. She'd been listening to this story, in instalments, for six rotations. It was the closest thing the Quarterdeck had to entertainment.

"...and the depot master says they were logged in as size nine," the deputy quartermaster was saying, "and the Répertoire says they're size nine, and I've measured them myself, ma'am, and they're..."

The crystal floor went white.

It didn't flash. It lit up, slowly, from the northern end of the hall, a white-gold light that came up through the smoked crystal under their feet and climbed the walls and the long basalt table and their faces, getting brighter, until Corvis had to put her hand over her eyes. Then the sound came. It came up through the chains of the Quarterdeck, through four basalt links each the size of a Hoplon carrier, and through the iron of the hull, and through the soles of her boots, a long, deep, grinding roar that she felt in her chest and her teeth and the bones of her skull, and the whole of Quarterdeck Nineteen swung on its chains, slowly, a metre to the south, and back again.

Somebody screamed. The deputy quartermaster dropped his slate.

Corvis went to the observation gallery at a run. The whole of the logistics staff went with her, and the night watch from the relay vault, and everyone else on the Quarterdeck who was awake, crowding along the crystal wall of the gallery and looking north.

Two hundred kilometres away, over the northern skyline, beyond the Mora Cut and the upper bend of the Phlegethon, a column of fire was standing up out of the deep.

It went up into the dark of Tartarus kilometres high, white at its root and gold above and red at its crown, where it spread out against the underside of the ceiling in a great slow boiling mushroom of flame. Around its base the deep was burning for kilometres in every direction. And down through the column, along its whole height, still falling, she could see the long burning streak of the thing that had made it, a slanting line of fire from the ceiling to the deep, as if somebody had drawn it with a ruler.

"Gods," somebody said. "Gods, what is it? What is that?"

Corvis didn't answer. She was looking at the slant of the line. It came down out of the ceiling far to the north-east, on a long shallow angle, and it went into the deep at the upper bend of the Phlegethon at Depth 38, two hundred kilometres north of the Cinder Flats, and it had been falling for a long time before it hit. Something had come down through the low geometry from a great height, slowly, under control, and put itself into the deep on that exact spot.

The column stood for seven minutes. She timed it. Then it began to sink, slowly, folding back into itself, and the glow at its base spread out and settled into a long low line of fire on the northern horizon, and the Quarterdeck stopped swinging.

The panes along the wall of the gallery chimed, every one of them at once.

---

*RÉPERTOIRE DU SAVOIR ATHÉNIEN. FLEET REGISTRY. EXTRACT.*

*AC-09-1642. THE EYE OF LIGHT. ANNCHASE-CLASS BATTLESHIP, 1,642ND OF ITS PATTERN.*

*BUILT: FORGE OF HEPHAESTUS. ASSIGNED: 9TH OLYMPIAN REACH, LOCAL DEFENCE FORCE, UNDER THE HOUSE OF APOLLO.*

*STATUS: LOST WITH ALL HANDS. CAPTURED BY THE ORDER OF THE HOLLOW CRESCENT, THIRTY-TWO YEARS PRIOR TO THIS ENTRY. RECOVERED AT LUNA ON THE HERETICS' WITHDRAWAL. REQUISITIONED FROM SCHEDULED INSPECTION AT THE FORGE OF HEPHAESTUS BY AN ALPHA-CLASS ENTITY, IDENTIFIED: MINERVA.*

*TERMINAL EVENT: IMPACT, TARTARUS, DEPTH 38, GRID DELTA, SECTOR NINETEEN-F. VESSEL EXPENDED AS A WAYPOINT.*

*ESTIMATED EFFECT: NEUTRALISATION OF 0.09 PERCENT OF ACTIVE TARTARUS MACROFORMS IN THEATRE.*

---

Corvis read it twice. Around her the staff of the Third were reading it too, on their own panes, and nobody said anything.

"A battleship," the deputy quartermaster said at last, in a small voice. "Somebody crashed a battleship into the deep."

"Somebody flew a battleship into the deep," Corvis said. "On purpose. As a waypoint."

"A waypoint for what?"

She didn't get to answer. The panes chimed again, all of them, and this time they weren't the only thing that spoke.

**THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE 44TH TRENCH REGIMENT. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 3**

Phalanx-General Oren Callix was walking his line when the Répertoire spoke to his soldiers, and it was the most frightening thing he'd heard in forty-eight years.

He'd been in the forward trench of the *44th* when the column went up in the north, inspecting the hinge that Marrow's cohort had dug on Leaf 59-30 and turned into a proper trench over two rotations. He'd felt the impact through his boots and seen the light of it on the underside of the ceiling and heard every soldier on the fire-steps around him start shouting at once. He'd been standing in Bay 1 with Marrow, both of them staring north at the column, when every relay set in the trench crackled at once.

Every soldier in the Third carried a relay set. Most of them never used it. It hung on a soldier's chest rig on its strap, a small grey box with a speaker grille, and it carried the regimental net and the section net and, in theory, the voice of anybody who had the authority to speak on all bands. Nobody did. The Third's soldiers had heard the Répertoire's verdicts all their lives, read out to them at stand-to by their officers from the panes. Some of them had heard their officers argue with it over the relay. None of them had ever heard it speak.

It spoke now, from every relay set in the Third, at once. It spoke from the boxes on the soldiers' chests and from the panes in the dugouts and from the speakers on the gun lines, all along the hundred and nine kilometres of the arc, in a voice that Callix would never be able to describe afterward. It belonged to neither a man nor a woman, and it was quiet and clear and calm, and it seemed to come from close by, as if it were standing in the bay beside him, and it went into his ears and down into his chest.

*"Verdict. Accepted. Warzone Re-evaluation, CODE 78E. Every and all mortal combat assets initiate total assault. Penetrate the Tartarus front at Grid Delta, Sector Nineteen-F. Utilize Battleship AC-09-1642 as waypoint. All artillery, rotary, and missile batteries, continuous fire saturation ahead of advancing infantry. Full authorization granted for Heavy Armor brigades, breakthrough formation, bypass all secondary contacts. Ave Imperator."*

A pause. Callix realised he was holding his breath.

*"Clarification. This will establish a permanent territorial wedge for the first time since the Second Grand Offensive. All auxiliary garrisons will reinforce from rear sectors. ETA: 15 hours. Good Luck."*

The relay sets went quiet.

In the bay around him, nobody moved. The soldiers of the *6th* Cohort stood on the fire-step with their rifles in their hands and their faces turned toward their own chests, toward the small grey boxes on their rigs, as if they expected them to say something else. Callix looked at Marrow. She was standing absolutely still with her hand on the relay set on her chest, and her face had gone white.

"Good luck," a trooper said, somewhere down the bay. "It said good luck. Did you hear it? It said good fucking luck."

"I've never heard it," someone else said. "I've been in the Third twenty years. I've never heard it say anything."

"It sounded..." The first trooper stopped. "I don't know what it sounded like."

Callix did. It had sounded like somebody who had been listening to every word they'd ever said, for six hundred years, and had decided, at last, to answer.

"Centurion," he said quietly. "Your cohort."

Marrow came back to herself. She took her hand off the relay set.

"Right," she said, and her voice was almost steady. "Right. *6th* Cohort. Back to your bays. Nothing's changed. Rifles up, eyes front. Whatever's happening at Grid Delta is two hundred kilometres away and it's not ours." She looked at Callix. "Is it, sir?"

Callix looked north at the column of fire, which was sinking now, slowly, into a long low line of flame on the horizon. He thought about the phrase *every and all mortal combat assets*. He thought about *all auxiliary garrisons will reinforce from rear sectors*.

"I don't know, Centurion," he said. "I think we're about to find out."

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN, THE LORD-GENERAL'S QUARTERS. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 3, THIRD WATCH**

The second leaf arrived at the Lord-General's desk an hour after the broadcast, and Aurelian Castorre read it with Corvis standing beside him and did not, for some time, say anything at all.

It wasn't addressed to the soldiers of the line. It was addressed to the command of the *3rd* Siege Army, and it was short, and it said exactly what Corvis had known it would say from the moment she heard the phrase *auxiliary garrisons*.

*CODE 78E. ANNEX. TO: 3RD SIEGE ARMY, COMMAND. 22ND AND 33RD TRENCH REGIMENTS, AND THE RESERVE OF THE 4TH ASSAULT PHALANX, TO PROCEED BY CEILING RELAY TO GRID DELTA, SECTOR NINETEEN-F, FORTHWITH, TO REINFORCE FROM THE REAR. REMAINDER OF THE 3RD SIEGE ARMY TO MAKE GENERAL ATTACK ON THE SECOND BELT OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL ALONG ITS WHOLE FRONT, UNDER CONTINUOUS SATURATION FIRE, COMMENCING CYCLE 5, SO THAT NO FORCE ON THE CINDER FLATS ANSWERING TO THE ARCHFLAMEN, IN THE TRENCHES OR IN THE RIVER, IS FREE TO MOVE NORTH WHILE THE WEDGE IS DRIVEN IN. AVE IMPERATOR.*

Castorre put the leaf down on his desk, squared it to the edge, and looked at it.

"In the trenches or in the river," he said.

"It thinks the Archflamen might send something north, sir. Up the Phlegethon. To hit the Wedge in the flank."

"Does he have anything to send?"

"Not that we know of." Corvis hesitated. "But the Répertoire knows more than we do. It always has."

Castorre nodded slowly.

"The general attack," he said. "Tell me about the general attack, Leda."

She had the figures ready. She'd had them ready since the broadcast. She laid them out on his desk on her slate, one after another, in the order he would want them, as she had done every day for nineteen years.

"Sir, the Third isn't ready to attack the second belt. The plan requires a rotation of sapping before the first assault. We've reached the belt on twelve leaves. On the rest of the arc we're still two kilometres short of it, and the ground between is the most heavily ranged ground on the Flats. We have no fire plan. The verdict requires continuous saturation fire, which means every gun firing at the belt without pause and without targets, for as long as the attack lasts. The second belt's nests are older and deeper than the outer belt's, and we haven't located half of them. A saturation barrage won't reach them. It'll churn up the wire and the ground in front of the wire and leave the nests exactly where they are." She tapped the slate. "And the Répertoire's own figure for the attack is six regiments. That's what the Third can spare, after the *22nd* and the *33rd* go north. Six regiments, sir. Against a hundred and nine kilometres of the second belt."

Castorre looked at the slate for a long time.

"Is there anything in that," he said, "that the Répertoire doesn't know?"

Corvis was silent.

"No," she said at last. "No, sir. It knows all of it."

"Then it has decided that six regiments of the Third, attacking a belt they can't break, is a price worth paying to keep the Archflamen from moving north while the Wedge goes in." He picked up his stylus. "It's probably right."

"Sir..."

"Leda." He looked up at her. His face was calm. "In six hundred years, no Lord-General of the Third who disputed a CODE 78 has kept his command for a rotation afterward. Not one. If I dispute this, the Command will relieve me, and the Répertoire will appoint my successor, and my successor will sign it. The only thing my refusal would change is who signs." He pulled the leaf toward him. "And I'd rather it was me. At least I'll read the casualty lists."

He signed it, in his small neat hand, and dated it.

Then he sat back and looked at the black window behind her, where the glow of the impact still lay along the northern horizon.

"Who crashed the ship?" he said.

"Sir?"

"The *Eye of Light*. Somebody flew it into the deep at Grid Delta and put it exactly where the Répertoire needed a waypoint for a wedge it's been waiting thirty years to drive. The registry says an Alpha-Class entity. Minerva." He frowned. "I've never heard of an Alpha-Class entity called Minerva. Have you?"

"No, sir." Corvis hesitated. "The registry's all I have. The Wedge command at Grid Delta belongs to Lord Commander Varric von Romhardt. He'd be the officer most likely to know."

"Get me a line to Varric's base."

She got him one. It took eleven minutes. When it came through, it came through to an empty room. The signals officer of the base, a young lieutenant who had stayed behind to keep the relay open, apologised to the Lord-General of the *3rd* Siege Army at some length and explained that there was nobody to speak to, since every officer and runner in the base had gone forward with the assault.

"All of them?" Castorre asked.

"*All of them, my lord. The Lord Commander went first. He took the base guard with him. He said...*" The lieutenant hesitated. "*He said he'd waited thirty years for this, and he wasn't going to watch it on a pane.*"

Castorre thanked him, and closed the line, and sat for a while in silence.

"Varros," he said. "Ask Varros. If anyone on this Quarterdeck knows what an Alpha-Class entity is, he will."

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN, THE ARCHMAGISTER'S OFFICE. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 3, FOURTH WATCH**

Varros knew.

He told them in his office at the stern of the Quarterdeck, sitting in his cognition chair with a glass of the dreadful ceiling brandy in his hand and Lector Pell on the floor with her panes, while Castorre stood at the door and Corvis sat on the edge of the cot that nobody ever used.

"It's a classification," he said. "Not a rank. The Répertoire uses it for entities whose effective power exceeds that of a fleet. There are very few of them. The Olympians, of course. A handful of the elder powers. A few of the Primordials, on the days they're awake." He drank. "And, quite recently, someone called Minerva, who appears in the registry for the first time attached to events the Répertoire has classified at a level I can't read." Varros shrugged. "I don't know any more than that. I know that the Répertoire's fleet registry lists her as having requisitioned a battleship from the Forge of Hephaestus without asking anyone, flown it down through the whole of the ceiling geometry of Tartarus, and put it into the deep at Grid Delta precisely where a wedge needed to go. I know that the Répertoire has been waiting thirty years for a waypoint at Grid Delta that it could drive a wedge through. And I know that the Répertoire's verdict on what she did was *Accepted*." He looked at his brandy. "I have read eleven thousand verdicts in my life, Lord-General. I have never seen it accept anything. It renders. It doesn't accept. Someone did something it hadn't planned for, and it decided, on reflection, that it would like the result."

Castorre was silent for a while.

"Is she on our side?" he asked.

"I don't know," Varros said. "I'm not sure she has one."

**THE CINDER STAIR. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 3, DARK WATCH**

That night the *22nd* and the *33rd* Trench Regiments went up the Cinder Stair with the reserve of the *4th* Assault Phalanx, one hundred and eighty thousand soldiers and two hundred tanks, and Callix watched them go.

He stood at the foot of the Stair in the dark with his staff around him and watched the column climb past him, one cohort wide, up the forty-one switchbacks of the shelf wall toward the relay at Kappa. The soldiers didn't look at the Arsenal as they went. They'd been looking at it for four rotations. They looked north instead, at the long low glow of the fire on the horizon at Grid Delta, two hundred kilometres away, where they were going.

The *22nd* had been the first regiment of the Third to come down the Stair, in Rotation Zero. It had been on the Flats for four rotations, had fought on the Drum and in the outer belt and the roll-up, and its soldiers had died and come back and died again, and now it was going up the Stair to fight somewhere else for somebody else, and it would be back, the Répertoire said, within the rotation.

The *22nd* did come back, eventually.

The *33rd* never did. It went into the Wedge at Sector Nineteen-F with Varric's assault and was still there four rotations later, holding a section of the line that the Répertoire had declared permanent, and which proved to be so. Its soldiers fought and died and came back and fought again in the Wedge for the rest of the war, and the *3rd* Siege Army never saw it again. Callix would write its name at the top of a fresh page of his book that night, and under it, over the following rotations, he would write the names of its nulls, as they came to him from the Répertoire's lists. It was no longer his regiment. He counted it anyway.

At the end of the dark watch, when the last cohort of the *33rd* had gone up past Switchback 19 and out of sight, he went back to his headquarters and sat down at his desk and wrote a letter to the Répertoire.

He wrote it by hand, as he wrote everything that mattered. He protested that a general attack on an unreduced belt without a fire plan would cost the Third a rotation of its timetable and give the Archflamen a victory he had not earned. He set out, in figures, the depth of the second belt's nests and the extent of the ground the Third had not yet reached. He asked that the *2nd* Line Phalanx be allowed to make its share of the attack by sapping, as the Third's plan had always intended, so that it would hold the Archflamen's soldiers in their trenches as the verdict required, without throwing his regiments onto wire that his guns had not cut.

He signed it, and sent it up the relay to the Quarterdeck, and from there to the Watchpoint.

The reply came back eleven seconds later.

*Query received. Ref. 78E-3SA-0004. Verdict restated. General attack will proceed along the whole front as ordered. Ave Imperator.*

**THE GUN LINES, NORTHERN FLATS. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 5**

The Saturation began at the first bell of the fifth cycle, and Nessa Carrow laid Gun Three of Battery *26* for nine hours without a single target.

She'd been laying Gun Three since the night Gun Four went up, three rotations ago. Gun Four had never been replaced. The waiting list for divine mortars had got longer, not shorter, and Battery *26* had five guns now instead of six, and she and Aster Lenn had been transferred onto Gun Three, where the old layer had been glad to give her the seat.

At the first bell the order came down from the Quarterdeck as a single amber line on her pane.

*All batteries. Saturation. Continuous fire. Second belt, your sector, all leaves. No fire plan. Rate: maximum sustainable.*

She looked at it for a while.

"No fire plan," she said.

"What does that mean?" Lenn asked, beside her. He'd been keying shells for four rotations now. The nervous laugh had gone some time in the second rotation, along with most of the others.

"It means there aren't any targets." She brought up the overlay of the second belt on her pane, the long red line of it across the Flats eleven kilometres away, and the leaves of the sector Battery *26* was laid on. "It means we lay on the belt, and we fire, and we keep firing, and we move the gun a few mils every round so we cover the whole of our sector, and we don't stop until somebody tells us to." She traversed the wheel. "It means we're not trying to hit anything, Aster. We're trying to hit everything."

"Is that possible?"

"No," Carrow said, and laid the gun.

---

It went on for nine hours. Every gun on the Flats fired without pause, from the trench mortars of the forward regiments to the Phaethons of the *10th* eighty kilometres back, and the Third's gunners afterward called it the Saturation, and never called it anything else.

Carrow fired Gun Three four hundred and eleven times in those nine hours, and not one of her shells went at anything she could name. She laid them across the leaves of the sector in a long slow sweep from left to right and back again, a few mils at a time, and Lenn keyed them, and they went out over the Flats and came down on the second belt, and she never knew what they hit. The wire, probably. The ground in front of the wire. The ground behind it. The slag heaps and the old craters and the empty spaces between the nests, where there was nothing at all.

By the fourth hour the barrel of Gun Three was glowing red, and they were pouring condenser water over it from canvas buckets, and it was the first night on the Flats all over again, the steam and the deafness and the endless thud of the breech. She didn't feel happy this time. She felt sick.

At the seventh hour, four kilometres to the south, a Phaethon split its barrel.

She heard it go. Everybody on the gun line heard it. It made a sound she'd never heard before and never heard again, a single enormous cracking shriek that went on far longer than any gun should, and when she looked south, a long gout of red fire was going up into the dark from the Phaethon battery behind the *14th* Ordnance Regiment, from a gun that had been firing every four minutes for seven hours until its barrel couldn't take any more. A second Phaethon went an hour later, on the western Flats. Neither of them was replaced for the rest of the siege.

At the ninth hour, the guns lifted.

The silence afterward was so deep that Carrow thought she'd gone deaf for good. Then, slowly, through it, she heard a sound coming from the north, from the *2nd* Line Phalanx's trenches, faint and far away. It was whistles.

**THE FORWARD OBSERVATION POST OF THE 2ND LINE PHALANX. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLES 5 TO 9**

Callix watched the general attack from the forward observation post of the *2nd* Line Phalanx, and he watched it for six cycles, and it went exactly as he had said it would.

Six regiments attacked the second belt along the line from the Mora Cut to the Lethe Cut. Two of them were his. They went over at the end of the Saturation, as the guns lifted, and for a moment he let himself hope; the second belt in front of them had been hammered for nine hours by every gun the Third possessed, eleven million shells, and from the observation post it looked like the surface of the moon.

The wire was still there.

It had been laid over two rotations. It was deeper than the outer belt's, three belts of razor-wire, then a mined gap, then three more. The Saturation had fallen on it for nine hours, and some of it had been cut, in places, and most of it hadn't, and the parts that had been cut had been thrown up by the shells into tangled heaps that were harder to cross than the wire had been. And behind the wire, the nests were waiting.

They were older than the outer belt's nests. They had been built as permanent works, with galleries beneath them linking one to the next, and they were harder, and deeper, and most of them had not been touched. The Saturation hadn't been aimed at them. It hadn't been aimed at anything.

In most places, the attack was stopped at the wire.

He watched the soldiers of the *2nd* Line Phalanx go down in front of it in long rows, caught in the wire under the guns of the nests, as the *37th* had gone down on the Drum, as the *41st* had gone down on Leaf 58-31, as soldiers of the Third had been going down in front of the Archflamen's guns for four rotations. And then he watched them come again, because the verdict said continuous, and the regiments' colonels had their orders, and the next wave went over into the same fire across the same ground. The Third's dead lay in heaps in front of the second belt.

In a few places, the attack broke through.

It broke into the forward trenches and the support trenches behind them, on Leaf 61 and on Leaf 64 and on a stretch of the *44th*'s front on Leaf 59, and each time it did, Callix watched the Archflamen's gunners do what Ostorian had taught them. They had ranged on their own forward trenches for a rotation, waiting for a day like this one. The moment a cohort of the Third came down into a trench of the second belt, the guns of the inner belt came down on the trench, and the ground on both sides of it, and the communication trenches leading back to the Third's line. Every cohort that broke in was cut off in its new position, alone, unsupported, unable to go forward or back. Then the garrison cohorts came up out of their deep shelters, fresh, untouched by nine hours of Saturation, and drove them out before they could be reinforced.

It went on for six cycles. Each wave was repulsed and followed by the next, the soldiers of the Third going in and coming back out and going in again, and each cycle the attack was widened to a neighbouring leaf, to suppress the flanking fire from the nests on either side, and failed there too.

On the third cycle, Marrow's *6th* Cohort broke into the second belt on Leaf 59 and held a stretch of the forward trench for eleven hours before the garrison drove them out. Callix watched it on the overlay. When the green pip of the *6th* Cohort finally went back across the wire, he found that his hands were shaking, and he put them in his pockets so that his staff wouldn't see.

On the ninth cycle, the Répertoire lifted CODE 78E for the Cinder Flats.

**THE DEEP SHELTER UNDER NEST BETA 4, THE SECOND BELT. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 5**

Galba Ostorian spent the Saturation exactly where he had spent the bombardment of the Drum, twenty metres under a nest, and this time he brought a book.

He had learned that much. He'd learned a great many things since the Drum, and written all of them down in his field book in his own square hand, and one of them was that a man who sat in a deep shelter for nine hours with nothing to do but listen would come out of it with his nerves in rags. So he brought a book, a soldier's edition of the *Annals of the First Descent* that the Archflamen had given him in the year before the revolt, and he sat on an upturned crate at the foot of the shaft under Nest Beta 4 with a tin lamp beside him and read it, while the whole of the *3rd* Siege Army's artillery fell on the rubble overhead and the chamber shook and the dust came down in a slow endless rain onto the pages.

The thralls of the *23rd* Imperator's Cohort sat around him along the walls with their hands over their ears and watched him read. That was the other thing he'd learned. They watched the officers. If the officers were afraid, the thralls were afraid. If the Prefect of the Imperator's legion sat at the foot of the shaft turning the pages of a book while the Empire tried to bring the ceiling down on his head, then perhaps it wasn't so bad, after all.

He didn't take in a word of it. He'd read the same page eleven times by the fourth hour.

It was a strange bombardment. He noticed that early. He'd been under the Third's guns on the Drum and in the outer belt and he knew how the Third fired, methodically, by fire plan, target by target, walking its shells from one nest to the next and lifting them in stages as the Répertoire had recommended after the Drum. This was different. This came down everywhere at once and kept coming, without lifting, without pattern, without any sign that it was aimed at anything in particular. It fell on the wire and the ground in front of the wire and the ground behind it, and it fell on the nests too, now and then, by accident, and it didn't stop.

At the fourth hour, Dace came down the shaft.

He came down alone, in his grey coat, with a lamp, picking his way down the spiral stair through the falling dust as calmly as if he were coming down the steps of the palace, and the thralls along the walls shrank away from him as he passed. He sat down on a second crate beside Ostorian and looked at the book.

"The *Annals*," he said. "I gave the Archflamen that edition. Do you like it?"

"I haven't read a word." Ostorian closed it. "What are you doing here, Haruspex? You told me the Prefect of the Imperator's legion was too valuable to bury under a hill. Surely the Haruspex of the Imperator's Hold is worth more."

"I wanted to see it," Dace said. "Same as you did, on the Drum." He looked up at the shaking roof of the chamber with an expression of mild interest. "What do you make of it, Prefect?"

"It isn't aimed." Ostorian had been thinking about it for four hours. "They're throwing everything they have at the whole length of the belt, without a plan. I've never seen the Third do that. It's not how they fight." He frowned. "Somebody above Castorre has told them to. Somebody wants us kept in our holes, every one of us, along the whole of the line, and doesn't care what it costs to keep us there."

"Yes." Dace seemed pleased. "There was a light in the north, three cycles ago. Did you see it?"

"Everybody on the spur saw it."

"Something came down out of the ceiling at Grid Delta. Something large. And the Empire is driving a wedge into the deep behind it, two hundred kilometres north of here, and it is afraid." He tipped his head toward the roof. "It's afraid of us, Prefect. Of the Imperator's Hold. It's afraid we'll send something up the river to strike its wedge in the side, and so it's throwing the whole of the Third at our wire to keep us from moving."

Ostorian stared at him.

"Up the river," he said. "We don't have anything in the river. We don't have a boat on the Phlegethon bigger than an ore-barge."

"No," Dace agreed. "Not yet."

He took out the grey vial and tipped three drops onto the back of his hand, and the cold smell of the winter river came up off his skin in the heat of the chamber, and he licked it clean.

"The ceiling thinks we have friends in the river," he said. "It's wrong, of course. For now. But I find it interesting that it thinks so. It means somebody up there has been looking at the river and wondering, and that means the river is worth looking at." He put the vial away and stood up, brushing dust off his coat. "Enjoy your book, Prefect. I'll see you at the Archflamen's table, when this is over."

He went back up the shaft into the bombardment, unhurried, and Ostorian sat at the foot of it with the book closed on his knee and watched him go, and thought about the Sworn parties that went down the Styx road to Depth 44 and came back with river-water on their boots and fewer men than they'd left with, and about the cold-smelling things the thralls had seen moving on the Phlegethon at night, below the Number Four docks, and about the river being the Haruspex's business and the Flats being his.

He opened the book again. He read the same page a twelfth time.

At the ninth hour the guns lifted, and he was on his feet and roaring at the thralls of the *23rd* to man the nest before the sound of it had finished rolling away overhead, and he forgot about the river for six cycles.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 9**

The message came to the Lord-General's desk as a single amber leaf, and Corvis brought it in herself.

*CODE 78E LIFTED, CINDER FLATS. TO: 3RD SIEGE ARMY, COMMAND. THE WEDGE AT GRID DELTA HAS HELD. THE ARCHFLAMEN'S FORCES HAVE BEEN FIXED IN PLACE AS REQUIRED. THE GENERAL ATTACK HAS ACHIEVED ITS OBJECT. AVE IMPERATOR.*

Castorre read it. Then he held out his hand for the other slate, the one she was carrying under her arm, and she gave it to him.

It was her own summary. She had written it in the last watch, from the regimental returns, and she'd checked it three times, and it was correct in every particular.

The general attack on the second belt had gained three hundred metres on two leaves, Leaf 61 and Leaf 64, at a cost of three hundred and twelve thousand transfers. Eleven thousand of the transfers were null. It was a null rate of three and a half percent of all transfers, the first time in the siege the rate had exceeded the ceiling set in the order of assignment, and the Répertoire's technical officers had already reported it to the Command, which had already asked them to account for it, and they had already attributed it to the Archflamen's jammers and to the density of the fire, which had destroyed more of the Third's relay pylons in six cycles than in the whole of the preceding rotation.

The Third had fired more than eleven million shells in nine hours of preparation and six cycles of attack. It was a quarter of its stocks on the Flats. Much of it had fallen on ground that held nothing. The keyed ammunition the Third had drawn from the Arsenal's lists before the revolt would not be replaced. From the next cycle, for the first time in the siege, the Third's divine batteries would be on a ration.

At the bottom of the slate was the Arsenal's figure for its own losses, from the Third's intelligence section, taken from the interrogation of prisoners. Sixty thousand. Most of them in the forward trenches Ostorian had always expected to lose. The garrison cohorts had gone forward on the eighth cycle and reoccupied every metre of the second belt but the six hundred the Third had kept, and the thralls had begun repairing the wire the same night.

Castorre read the slate to the end. Then he put it down, on top of the amber leaf, and squared them both to the edge of the desk.

"*The general attack has achieved its object*," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"It has, of course. Its object was never the second belt." He was quiet for a moment. "Its object was to keep a man on a hill nineteen kilometres away from sending something up a river two hundred kilometres to the north, which we don't know he had, to strike a wedge we weren't told about until the ship had already come down. And it did that. I'm sure it did." He looked up at her. "Three hundred and twelve thousand transfers, Leda. And eleven thousand who didn't come back."

Corvis said nothing.

"Was it worth it?"

"I don't know, sir. The Wedge held."

"The Wedge held." He nodded. "Varric will have his permanent territorial wedge, and the Répertoire will have its waypoint, and somebody called Minerva will be wherever she's gone. And the *3rd* Siege Army will have three hundred metres on two leaves, and a ration on its divine batteries, and eleven thousand names." He picked up his stylus, and then put it down again. "Do you know what the centurions are saying?"

She did. She'd heard it from the provost-marshal that morning.

"They're saying you ordered it, sir."

"I did order it."

"They don't know about the verdict, sir. Not the second leaf. They heard the broadcast, they heard *all auxiliary garrisons*, and then two days later they were going over the top into the second belt without a fire plan. They think it was yours." She hesitated. "And Callix's letter."

"What about it?"

"It's gone along the line, sir. His protest to the Répertoire. Somebody on his staff made a copy, and somebody copied the copy. It's in every regiment of the Third by now. The centurions are reading it out at stand-to. The provost-marshal has asked for authority to confiscate it."

Castorre looked at her for a long moment.

"Give him the authority," he said. "And then bring me a copy."

---

She brought it to him at the end of the watch. It was a crumpled sheet of field-message paper, in a cramped trooper's hand, copied from a copy of a copy, with a stain of something that might have been coffee in one corner. The provosts had taken it off a breacher of the *46th* in a dugout on Leaf 61.

Castorre read it slowly, under his lamp. It was Callix's letter, word for word, with all its figures. At the bottom, under Callix's signature, the trooper who had copied it had added a line of his own.

*Old Callix knew. The Lord-General signed it anyway.*

Castorre read the line several times. Then he folded the sheet carefully along its creases and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, beside the Archflamen's letter from Rotation Zero, which he had been carrying there for four rotations, and which he had never told anyone about.

"Leda," he said.

"Sir."

"The Command. Have they said anything?"

"Nothing in writing, sir." She chose her words carefully. "The Legate-Superior's office has asked for the null figures again. Twice. And for your assessment of when the Third expects to break the second belt."

"What did you tell them?"

"That the Third would return to sapping, sir, and that you'd send your assessment within the rotation."

"Good." He nodded. "Good. That's exactly right."

He sat for a long while looking at the black window. The glow at Grid Delta had faded at last, and the northern horizon was dark again, and below them, nineteen kilometres away on its spur, the Arsenal burned as it always did, small and black and lit by its river.

"No one has ever relieved a Lord-General of the Third during a campaign," he said at last, to the window, or to her. "In six hundred years. I looked it up."

"No, sir."

"I don't think they're ready to be the first." He picked up his stylus again, and pulled the Répertoire's plan for the second belt toward him, and opened it to the first page. "Not yet."

**THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE 44TH. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 12**

Three cycles after the Saturation, Ilsa Marrow found a trooper of her *4th* Maniple kneeling in the back of a fire bay in the dark watch, with his relay set in his hands, praying to it.

She stopped at the traverse and watched him for a while. He was young, eighteen or nineteen, one of the new drafts from the ceiling who'd come down the Stair to replace the *6th* Cohort's losses in the Saturation, and he was kneeling on the duckboards with the little grey box held up in front of his face in both hands, and he was talking to it softly, in the voice people used for the vat-rite.

"...and keep us, and bring us home," he was saying. "And let us come back. Please. Let us all come back. Ave Imperator. Ave. Please."

He finished, and kissed the speaker grille, and put the set back on his rig.

"Trooper," Marrow said.

He jumped, and turned, and saw her, and went red to the roots of his hair.

"Centurion. I wasn't... I didn't..."

"What were you doing?"

He was silent for a moment.

"It spoke," he said at last. "On the third. It spoke to us. All of us. It said good luck, Centurion. Nobody's ever said good luck to me. Not in the vats, not on the Stair, not anywhere." He looked down at the set on his chest. "And it can hear us. The relay goes to the Quarterdeck and the Quarterdeck goes to the Watchpoint. It hears everything we say. Everybody knows that. So I thought..." He stopped. "I thought, if it can hear us, and it's the one that decides who comes back..."

Marrow looked at him for a long time.

The College had already issued a letter about it. She'd had it read out at stand-to two days ago, as ordered, in which the Flamen-Prior of the Tartarean theatre reminded the soldiers of the *3rd* Siege Army that prayer was owed to the gods and to the Lord Imperator alone, and that the Répertoire du Savoir Athénien was a servant of the Empire and not an object of devotion. The Flamens of the Third reported that attendance at the vat-rites had risen by a third since the broadcast. The provosts reported that a number of soldiers in the forward cohorts had begun to say their prayers to their relay sets. The Command had chosen not to notice.

"It doesn't decide who comes back," she said.

"Centurion?"

"It doesn't decide. It counts." She came down off the traverse into the bay. "If it decided, the nulls wouldn't happen. Nobody would decide to let eleven thousand of us not come back. It just counts us, after." She sat down on the fire-step beside him, heavily. She was tired. She'd been tired since the Saturation. "Go on. Say your prayers. I won't tell the Flamens. But don't say them to that. Say them to something that can hear you."

He looked at her uncertainly.

"Like what, Centurion?"

Marrow thought about it. She looked out over the parapet, at the dark ash of Leaf 59 and the wire of the second belt beyond it, and the heaps in front of the wire where the *6th* Cohort's dead had lain until the vats took them back, and the Arsenal beyond that, small and black and burning on its spur.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I used to think it was the vats."
