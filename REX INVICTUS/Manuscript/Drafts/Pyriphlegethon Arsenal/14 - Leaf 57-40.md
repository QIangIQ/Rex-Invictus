## Chapter 14: Leaf 57-40

*"A regiment that freezes at every flare crosses no-man's land twice. Mine will cross it once."*

*Colonel Damaris Lowe, to the Legate of the Eighth Line Phalanx, Rotation Eight*

---

**LEAF 57-40, THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE *52ND*. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 104**

By the fourth cycle of the Big Push, the *52nd* Trench Regiment had attacked the second belt three times, and Optio Bram Keddy had stopped counting the dead.

He had counted them on the first cycle. He had stood in the fire bay on Leaf 57-40 with the colonel's slate in his hands and logged the transfers as the relay operators called them in, one after another, name and cohort and maniple. The colonel had told him to log them all, since she wanted to know every one. By the dark bell he had logged two thousand, one hundred and six. On the second cycle he logged nine hundred more. On the third cycle the *52nd* went over again, on the Phalanx's orders, against the same stretch of trench on the same leaf, and the Arsenal's guns cut down the first wave in the same craters as the first two times, and when the relay operators began to call the transfers in Keddy found that his hands wouldn't write them down. He gave the slate to the relay operator and told her to do it.

He was twenty-nine. He had been in the *52nd* for eleven years and had been Damaris Lowe's optio for four of them, since before she was colonel, on the Acheron shelves, and he had transferred six times and had never once been afraid of it. He'd been afraid of other things. Fire. Drowning. The pale things that came up out of the Acheron at night and stood at the edge of the lamplight. But never transfer. You went up and you came back. Everyone knew that.

Now, standing in the wrecked fire bay at the end of the third cycle, with the dead lying on the trench floor and the colonel's slate in the relay operator's hands, he found that he was thinking about the medicus at Watchpoint Hades, and the Wall, and the pylon two hundred metres behind them that he could see from where he stood, with its little red lamp blinking through the haze.

He made himself stop.

The colonel was in her dugout. He found her there at the dark bell, sitting at the map table with a single lamp, alone, with her helmet on the table beside the map and her hands flat on either side of it. She had been on the fire-step of the forward bay for all three attacks. She had a cut over her left eye that she hadn't had looked at and a splash of somebody else's blood dried brown down the front of her field coat. She didn't look up when he came down the steps.

"How many?" she said.

"Three thousand, eight hundred and forty transferred, Colonel. In three cycles." He swallowed. "The relay operators are still logging the third. And sixty-one null."

"Sixty-one."

"Yes, Colonel. The medici say most of them were near the pylon."

She said nothing for a while. She was looking at the map. Keddy had seen it many times before. It was her own map, hand-drawn, of Leaf 57-40 and the second belt in front of it, from the *52nd*'s forward trench to the support trench of the belt and beyond. She had drawn it herself, over three months, from the forward bays and the saps and the listening posts, at night. Every crater was on it. Every gap in the wire. Every shaft the thralls used to come up from their shelters to their guns, and the time it took them to come up, measured by the colonel herself with a stopwatch from a listening post sixty metres from the wire.

"We can't do it like this, Bram," she said.

"No, Colonel."

"Three times. Three times we've gone over at the first bell, in daylight, at a walk, in a line, after a barrage they've sat out thirty metres down, and three times they've come up their shafts and been at their guns before we've got halfway across." She touched the map, the line of the second belt's forward trench, the little marks of the shafts. "They know exactly when we're coming. They know exactly where. We walk out into the open and they wait for us to come into range. It's the same every time. It's been the same for eight rotations, and the Phalanx wants us to do it again tomorrow."

"Yes, Colonel."

She looked up at him, finally. Her grey eyes were red-rimmed and steady.

"I'm going to ask them to let us do it differently," she said. "I'm going to ask them to let us do it at night."

Keddy felt something turn over in his stomach, and recognised it, after a moment, as hope.

"The crawl," he said.

"The crawl."

---

They had practised it for two months.

The colonel had begun in the last weeks of Rotation Seven, while the Eighth was still digging in, before anybody had heard of Antilochus. She had taken the *4th* Cohort out first, at night, into the old shell-churned ground behind the *52nd*'s support line, where the Arsenal's sentries couldn't see them, and she had made them crawl.

They crawled on their bellies, in the ash, in silence, for a kilometre, and then back.

The *4th* had thought she was mad. Keddy had thought so too, a little. The Third had taught its regiments to advance at a walk in parade order for six hundred years. It was in the Regulations. It was in the drill books. It was in the bones of every soldier who had ever gone over a parapet, the long line walking forward into the smoke with their rifles across their chests, and nobody in the *52nd* had ever crawled across anything in their lives except under wire.

"Why?" Centurion Livia Marsh of the *4th* had asked her, on the first night, standing in the dark with the ash to her knees. Marsh was twenty-six and had transferred three times and was not afraid of the colonel, which the colonel liked. "Why crawl, Colonel? It takes three times as long."

"It takes three times as long to cross," the colonel had said. "It takes a tenth as long to be seen. Look." She had pointed out across the dark toward the second belt, where a flare was going up, a single pale light rising slowly into the ash cloud and hanging there. "That's what they're watching for. Movement. When a flare goes up, every soldier in the Third who's out on the ash freezes where they are. The Regulations say to. A regiment that freezes at every flare takes twice as long to cross, and a regiment that's frozen in the open is a regiment the sentries can count." The flare had gone out. "They don't look for things lying still, Centurion. They look for things that move and then stop. Movement that stops. That's what catches the eye. If you're on your belly in the ash and you keep crawling, slowly, steadily, through the flare, through the light, the sentry's eye goes straight over you. You're part of the ground."

"And if they see us?"

"Then we're on our bellies already," the colonel had said, "and it's a great deal harder to hit something lying flat than something walking upright in a line."

They had crawled. Every night for two months, every cohort of the *52nd* in turn, out into the old ground behind the support line, a kilometre out and a kilometre back on their bellies in the ash, and every night the colonel had crawled with them. She had sent up flares over them herself, from the support trench, and lain in the ash with a night-glass and watched them, and every time a section froze in the flare she had crawled over to them in the dark and told them quietly to keep going. By the end of the first month they had stopped freezing. By the end of the second, Keddy had lain in the support trench with a night-glass of his own and watched the whole *4th* Cohort crawl past him under three flares at forty metres, and hadn't been able to see a single one of them.

And while they crawled, the colonel had sat in the forward bays and the listening posts at night with a stopwatch and timed the thralls.

She had timed everything. How long it took the Arsenal's barrage to start after a flare went up over movement. How long it took the thralls in the forward trench to come up their shafts to their guns when the Third's barrage lifted. How long the Third's barrage could fall on one line before it had to lift to the next. She had written it all down in her small neat hand in a notebook she kept in the breast pocket of her field coat, and at night in her dugout she had sat over the map and the notebook with a lamp and worked it out, minute by minute.

"Ninety seconds," she'd told Keddy one night in Rotation Seven, looking up from the notebook with her eyes bright. "Ninety seconds, Bram. That's how long it takes the thralls in the forward trench to come up their shafts after a barrage lifts. Every time. I've timed it forty times. If we can be on their parapet when the barrage lifts, we've got ninety seconds before they're at their guns. And if the barrage comes down on the trench line itself, not the wire, at the right minute, and lifts at the right minute, and we're lying in the craters thirty metres out when it does..."

"That's close, Colonel. Thirty metres. Our own shells."

"Yes," she'd said. "It's close."

---

**HEADQUARTERS OF THE EIGHTH LINE PHALANX, LEAF 55-36. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 105**

The Legate of the Eighth Line Phalanx was a heavy, careful, white-haired man named Severin Ashcombe, who had commanded the Eighth for twelve years and the *50th* before that, and he heard Lowe out in his headquarters dugout on the morning of the hundred and fifth cycle without interrupting her once. Keddy stood behind her against the wall and watched his face, and saw nothing in it at all.

She told him everything. The crawl, and the two months of practice. The flares, and the sentries' eyes. The ninety seconds. The barrage, from every gun the *52nd* had and every battery she could borrow from the Phalanx, falling on the second belt behind the trench line, then dropping onto the trench line itself at the planned minute while the regiment lay in the craters thirty metres out, then lifting and rolling forward, and the *52nd* rising out of the craters and going over the thralls' parapet in the ninety seconds before the thralls reached their guns. The whole regiment at once, eleven cohorts, on the regiment's whole front, all of it in one blow, at night, in place of a cohort here and a cohort there.

When she had finished, Ashcombe sat back in his chair and looked at her for a long time.

"No," he said.

Lowe said nothing.

"Colonel," Ashcombe said. "I've read your plan. I've listened to you. It's clever, and I can see you've worked at it, and I admire the work. But I can't allow it." He leaned forward. "If it fails, your regiment is caught in the open in front of the second belt, at night, in the craters, thirty metres from the wire, under its own shells. It'll be stranded there. The Arsenal's guns will find it at first light and destroy it. I'll lose the *52nd*. All of it. In one night. And the main effort of the Phalanx with it."

"Sir," Lowe said. "With respect. You'll lose the *52nd* anyway. You'll lose it a cohort at a time, at the first bell, every cycle, for the rest of the offensive. We've lost three thousand, eight hundred and forty in three cycles, sir, and sixty-one of them won't come back, and we've taken nothing of the second belt, not a metre. And the Phalanx wants us to go again tomorrow, at the first bell, at a walk, in a line, the same as the last three times."

"The Phalanx's plan..."

"The Phalanx's plan is the Third's plan, sir, and the Third's plan has been the same for six hundred years, and the Arsenal knows it better than we do." She put her notebook on his desk, open. "If the guns are strong enough, sir, they can keep the Arsenal's guns quiet even if the attack fails. I've worked it out. If we're stopped in the craters, the barrage keeps falling on the trench line and the guns behind it, and the regiment crawls back under it, the same way it crawled out. A regiment brought back under the cover of its own guns won't cost much. Less than a cycle of what we're doing now."

Ashcombe looked at the notebook. He didn't pick it up. He looked at it for a long time, at the columns of figures in her small neat hand.

"How many guns?" he said at last.

"Every gun the *52nd* has, sir. Every battery of the Phalanx you'll lend me. And as many of the Tenth Ordnance as the Lord-General will let me borrow."

"You want the Tenth Ordnance."

"Yes, sir."

He sat back. He looked at her for a long time again.

Then he sighed, heavily, and pulled the notebook toward him and turned it round, and began to read it properly.

---

It took the rest of the cycle. Ashcombe's staff argued, and the colonel argued back, and the Phalanx's chief gunner came down from his own headquarters and sat over the notebook with her for three hours, checking every figure, and found two mistakes, which she corrected on the spot. In the afternoon watch Ashcombe signalled Quarterdeck Nineteen, and an hour later a signal came back under the Lord-General's own seal, which Keddy saw Ashcombe read twice before he passed it across to the colonel without a word.

She read it, and handed it to Keddy.

*From the Lord-General commanding the 3rd Siege Army to Colonel Lowe, 52nd Trench Regiment. You may try. Give it everything. Castorre.*

Keddy looked up at her. She was looking at the signal in his hand with an odd, small, tight expression that he had never seen on her face before.

"He remembered," she said.

"Colonel?"

"Nothing, Bram." She took the signal back and folded it and put it in her breast pocket, behind the notebook. "Go and tell the centurions. Tonight. The dark watch of the hundred and sixth."

---

The batteries came in through the afternoon and evening. The *52nd*'s own guns, and the divine batteries of the Eighth Line Phalanx, and three batteries of the *3rd* Ordnance Regiment of the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx that the Lord-General had sent down from the northern Flats on his own authority, with their Phaethons on tractors and their keyers riding on the ammunition limbers. Keddy watched them come up the communication trenches behind Leaf 57-40 and into their pits, and the colonel went round every one of them in the dark watch with her notebook and her map, and sat with every battery commander and every gun captain and went through the fire plan with them, minute by minute, until they had it by heart.

One of them was a gunner of Battery *26*, a stocky, weather-beaten woman of forty with a voice wrecked by eight rotations of shouting over Phaethons, whose name was Nessa Carrow and who had commanded Gun Four since the start of the siege. Keddy stood behind the colonel while she went through the plan with Carrow, and watched Carrow's face go still when she heard the range.

"Thirty metres, Colonel?" Carrow said. "In front of your own lads?"

"Thirty metres, Gunner."

Carrow looked at the map for a long time. Then she looked at the colonel.

"Right," she said. "Then we'd better not be late."

---

**NO-MAN'S LAND, LEAF 57-40. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 106, DARK WATCH**

They went out at the second bell of the dark watch, eleven cohorts of the *52nd* Trench Regiment, over the parapet on their bellies, and the colonel went first.

Keddy went beside her. That was his place. He went over the parapet a hand's breadth behind her left shoulder and down onto the ash on his belly, and felt it give under him, soft and cold, and smelt it, the old dry stink of the Flats, burnt stone and sulphur and the dead. Then he began to crawl.

There was no light. The ash cloud was low that night and there was nothing in the sky but its dull red underside, glowing faintly from the river. He could see the colonel's boots in front of him, just, a dark shape in the dark, moving. He could hear nothing at all except his own breath and the soft whisper of the ash under his body as he pulled himself forward. All along the regiment's front, on either side of him, eleven cohorts were doing the same, four thousand soldiers on their bellies in the dark, crawling, and he couldn't hear one of them.

That was the first thing that frightened him. He had been on attacks before. He had been on a dozen attacks with the *52nd* and a good many more before that, and he had never been on one where he couldn't hear the soldiers around him. There was always noise. Boots and equipment and breath and somebody swearing. The long line walking forward into the smoke made a sound you could hear a kilometre away. Tonight there was nothing. Four thousand soldiers and nothing. He might have been alone on the Flats.

He crawled.

The first flare went up at the end of the first hour.

He heard it before he saw it, a soft pop from somewhere ahead and to the right, from the second belt, and then the light came, white and pale and terrible, rising slowly into the ash cloud and hanging there, and the whole of no-man's land lit up around him in a flat grey glare. He saw everything. He saw the craters and the wire and the old bones in the ash, and the colonel's boots in front of him, and the backs of the soldiers on either side of him, on their bellies, in a long ragged line across the ash, for a hundred metres in either direction. Four thousand soldiers lying in the open under a flare, sixty metres from the Arsenal's wire.

Every muscle in his body told him to freeze. Six hundred years of the Third told him to freeze. He had been taught to freeze since the first week of his training. When a flare goes up, you freeze.

The colonel's boots went on moving in front of him. Slowly. Steadily. Without stopping.

He made himself follow.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done. Harder than going over the top at the first bell. Harder than the Acheron. He pulled himself forward on his elbows under the flare, a hand's breadth at a time, slowly, steadily, with the white light on his back and the wire in front of him and the dark shapes of the sentries on the parapet of the second belt beyond it, and every moment he waited for the shout, and the rattle of the guns. All along the line, on either side of him, the soldiers of the *52nd* did the same. Nobody froze. Nobody stopped. Two months of nights in the old ground behind the support line, with the colonel sending up flares over them and crawling over in the dark to tell them quietly to keep going.

The flare went out.

No shout came. No guns. Nothing.

Keddy lay in the ash in the dark with his face pressed into it and his heart hammering, and found that he was crying, silently, and didn't know why. Then he wiped his face on his sleeve and began to crawl again.

Three more flares went up in the next hour. Nobody froze for any of them.

---

They reached the craters at the end of the second hour.

The colonel had marked them on her map, a line of old shell-holes thirty metres in front of the second belt's wire, deep and wide and half full of ash, the remains of the Saturation two rotations ago. The *52nd* came into them in the dark and lay in them, on their bellies, against the forward lips, and Keddy lay in the colonel's crater beside her with his face in the ash and looked up over the lip at the wire.

It was close enough to touch. He could see it in the faint red glow from the ash cloud: the long dark tangled mass of it, three strands deep, with the gaps the barrage had cut in it over eleven cycles and the stakes leaning at odd angles. Beyond it, the parapet of the second belt's forward trench, a long low black line. On the parapet, every twenty metres, a sentry. He could see their heads. He could hear them. One of them was coughing, a dry hacking cough, over and over. Another was humming something tuneless under his breath.

Thirty metres.

The colonel took out her watch and held it close to her face. Keddy could just see the faint glow of its hands.

"Two minutes," she breathed.

He put his face back in the ash and waited.

He thought about the barrage. He had been through barrages before, a great many of them, but always behind them, walking forward into the smoke where the shells had already fallen. He had never lain thirty metres in front of where they were going to fall. He thought about Carrow's face going still when she heard the range. *Then we'd better not be late.* He thought about a Phaethon shell, which weighed as much as a small tractor and would make a crater ten metres across, landing thirty metres in front of him.

He thought about the pylon two hundred metres behind the *52nd*'s forward trench, with its little red lamp.

"One minute," the colonel breathed.

He made himself stop thinking.

The barrage came down.

---

It came down on the second belt behind the trench line first, as the colonel had planned. Every gun the *52nd* had, every battery the Phalanx had lent, the three batteries of the *3rd* Ordnance, all of them at once, on the support trench of the second belt and the communication trenches and the shafts and the gun pits behind them, four hundred metres beyond the wire, in one enormous blow. The night went white. The ground under Keddy heaved and kept heaving. The noise was beyond anything he had ever heard, beyond the eleven cycles of the bombardment, beyond the Acheron in flood, a single tremendous roar that went on and on and seemed to come from everywhere at once. He lay in the crater with his face in the ash and his hands over his head and felt the shock of every shell come up through the ground into his chest.

On the parapet of the forward trench, thirty metres in front of him, the sentries had gone. He saw them go, in the white light of the barrage. They had dropped down into the trench and were running for their shafts, to go down into the deep shelters thirty metres under the ground and sit out the barrage there, as they always did. He saw them disappear down into the dark, one after another.

The colonel had her watch to her face.

"Now," she breathed. "Now, now, now..."

The barrage came back.

It came back from the support trench to the trench line itself in a single movement, every gun lifting its range by four hundred metres at the same second, and fell on the forward trench of the second belt thirty metres in front of the *52nd*'s craters, and Keddy felt the world end.

He had no words for it afterward. He never tried to find any. It was beyond noise. It was a solid thing, a wall of fire and earth and iron going up thirty metres in front of his face, so close that he felt the heat of it on his skin and the ash lift off the ground around him and the lip of the crater shake loose and pour down on him. Splinters went tearing over his head. Something heavy hit the lip of the crater a metre from his face and bounced away. All along the line of craters he heard soldiers of the *52nd* screaming, and he didn't know whether they were hurt or only frightened, and he couldn't do anything for them either way.

The colonel lay beside him with her face in the ash and her watch in her hand, counting.

He would never forget that. Afterward, when he told the story, as he told it a great many times to a great many people over the rest of his life, it was always that he came back to, over the noise and the fire and the screaming: the colonel, lying in the crater beside him in the middle of it, with her watch, counting the seconds under her breath, without a tremor in her voice.

"Twenty," she said. "Nineteen. Eighteen..."

The barrage on the trench line went on for a minute and a half. Ninety seconds. The same as the time it took the thralls to come up their shafts.

"Three," the colonel said. "Two. One."

The barrage lifted.

It went forward, over the trench line and the support trench and on, rolling back toward the inner belt in a long slow wall of fire, and in the sudden terrible silence where it had been the colonel came up out of the crater onto her feet with her sword in her hand and her pistol in the other, and turned to the *52nd* lying in the craters behind her, and shouted.

"*Up! Up! Fifty-Second, up!*"

They came up.

---

**THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE SECOND BELT, LEAF 57-40. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 106, DARK WATCH**

They came up out of the craters all along the line, four thousand of them, in the red light of the burning wire, and went over the thirty metres to the parapet of the second belt at a dead run, shouting. Keddy ran beside the colonel. He was through the wire before he knew it, through a gap the barrage had blown in it, and up the slope of the parapet with the ash sliding under his boots, and over the top.

The trench was empty.

It was wrecked, broken open, its revetments smashed and its fire-steps buried and its floor full of earth and splintered timber and the bodies of the sentries who hadn't reached their shafts in time. But nobody in it was alive. The guns were unmanned. The thralls were still coming up.

He heard them. He heard them in the shafts, under his feet, all along the trench, the scrape of their boots on the iron rungs as they climbed up from the deep shelters thirty metres below, to come to their guns now that the barrage had lifted, as they always did. They didn't know the *52nd* was there. They couldn't. They had heard the barrage fall on the trench line and lift, and they were coming up to their guns to meet the long line walking forward into the smoke.

The *52nd* was already on their parapet.

"*Shafts!*" the colonel shouted. "*Shafts! Take the shafts!*"

The soldiers of the *52nd* went down into the trench and along it to the mouths of the shafts. There was a shaft every forty metres, a square black hole in the trench floor with an iron ladder going down into the dark, and they stood around them with their rifles and their bayonets and their grenades and waited, and the thralls came up the ladders into them one at a time.

Keddy never forgot that either. He stood at the mouth of a shaft with Centurion Marsh and three soldiers of the *4th*, and the first thrall came up the ladder out of the dark, a big pale man with a blank face and an Arsenal rifle slung across his back, climbing fast, and his head came up out of the hole into the red light, and he saw the four of them standing over him, and for a single moment his blank face changed. Then Marsh put her bayonet into him and he went back down the ladder into the dark, and the next one came up behind him, and the next.

A few of them reached their guns, here and there along the trench, where a shaft came up too far from the nearest soldier of the *52nd*, and opened fire, and soldiers of the *52nd* fell. Most of them didn't. They came up their shafts one at a time into the bayonets, and the regiment went along the trench line over them, bay by bay, and the thralls below kept coming up into it.

Keddy lost the colonel in the fighting.

He saw her last at the end of the trench, by the entrance to the communication trench that ran back to the support trench of the second belt. She was standing on the fire-step with her sword in her hand, shouting to the *2nd* Cohort to come on, pointing down the communication trench, and her face in the red light was alight with something he had never seen in it before, a fierce bright joy. He was thirty metres away. He started toward her.

The Laistrygon came up out of the communication trench.

It came up out of the dark at a run, from the support trench, three metres tall, grey and enormous and hairless, with its great jaw open and a club of iron in its hand as long as a man. Keddy had seen them before, at a distance, in the purges of Rotation Zero, from the far end of the Flats. He had never seen one close. It came out of the communication trench onto the fire-step where the colonel was standing, and she turned to face it, with her sword up, and it hit her.

It hit her once. She went down, and did not move again.

Keddy screamed something. He never knew what. He ran along the fire-step toward her, firing his rifle into the Laistrygon from the hip, and the soldiers of the *2nd* Cohort came up behind him and fired too, and the great grey thing turned toward them and came on, and then a breacher of the *2nd* put a flame lance into its face from five metres, and it went down, burning, over the colonel's body.

He reached her. He dragged her out from under it.

She was dead. He had known she would be. He had seen it hit her.

He knelt beside her in the trench with her head in his lap, and the smoke from the burning Laistrygon going up around them, and the noise of the fighting all along the trench, and looked down at her face. It was quite calm. Her eyes were open, grey and steady, looking up past him at the dull red underside of the ash cloud.

He listened.

He had never listened for it before Rotation Seven. Nobody had. Now everyone did. He bent his head close to hers and listened, and after a moment, faintly, under the noise of the fighting, he heard it: the small soft tick of her implant firing.

He looked for the flicker. It was there. The tiny blue spark at the edge of his visor display that meant an upload going up, rising and fading.

He knelt there for a long time, watching where it had been.

Then Centurion Marsh was beside him, with her hand on his shoulder.

"Optio," she said. "Optio. The regiment."

He looked up at her. Her face was black with smoke and her helmet was gone and there was blood running down her neck from a cut behind her ear.

"She's gone," he said.

"I know. I saw. She'll be back." Marsh's hand tightened on his shoulder. "But she isn't here, Bram, and you are, and the second wave's coming through, and somebody's got to take them on. She'd want you to."

He looked down at the colonel's face once more. Then he laid her head down gently on the trench floor, and took the notebook out of her breast pocket, and the folded signal behind it, and put them in his own, and stood up.

---

At the first bell of the hundred and seventh cycle, the second wave of the *52nd* passed through the first in the forward trench of the second belt and went on, into the communication trenches and the support trench behind them, and beyond, behind the rolling wall of the barrage, into the space behind the second belt where no soldier of the Third had stood since the siege began.

The second belt had been broken.

---

**IN TRANSIT. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 106**

There was the Laistrygon, and then there was nothing, and then Damaris Lowe was going up.

She had been told that you didn't remember. Everyone said so. She had transferred twenty-two times and had never remembered any of them. You died and you woke in the vat and there was nothing in between, and she had always been grateful for it.

This time she remembered.

It was dark, and it was fast, and there was a pull, upward, toward something. She knew where she was going. She could feel it above her, far off, the ceiling and the relay chain and the watchpoint and the vats, home. She was going home. And she was not alone.

There was somebody else in the dark.

She felt it the moment it noticed her. It was cold, colder than anything she had ever felt, colder than the Acheron in flood. It was close and it was vast, and it was looking at her.

She knew what it was. She had read the rumours, the same as everyone on the Flats. The Wall. The nine thousand. The seven seconds. She had sat in the dugout on Leaf 58 with the words scratched on the steel joist above her head. *IT LISTENS.*

It was listening now. It was listening to her.

She waited for it to ask her name.

It didn't. It looked at her for a long moment, in the dark, with that vast cold attention, and she felt it turn her over, and look at what she was, and find... she didn't know what it found. Something it didn't want. Or something it wasn't looking for. Its attention went past her, downward, toward something far below her in the dark, something that it wanted a great deal more, and for a moment, as it turned away, she felt the shape of what it wanted, huge and old and hungry and far down, at the bottom of everything.

Then it let her go.

She went up.

**WATCHPOINT HADES. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 114**

She woke in the vat eight cycles later, coughing, with the fluid in her mouth and her nose and the pale lamps of the vat hall above her blurred and swimming, and the vat-masters lifted her out and wrapped her in a grey blanket and sat her on a bench, and she sat there shaking for a long time.

Her upload had been logged arriving at Watchpoint Hades ninety seconds after her death. Whole. The vat-master told her so, in a flat, tired voice, reading it off her slate. Arrived whole. Pattern intact. Nothing missing.

"Ninety seconds," Lowe said, when she could speak. "It's supposed to be seven."

The vat-master looked at her slate again.

"Ninety seconds," she agreed. "It's in the log. It happens sometimes, now." She hesitated. "You're lucky, Colonel. Most of the late ones come in empty."

Lowe sat on the bench in her grey blanket and looked at the floor.

She thought about the cold thing in the dark, and how it had looked at her and looked away. She thought about the shape of what it had wanted, far down, at the bottom of everything.

"My regiment," she said. "The *52nd*. What happened?"

The vat-master looked at her for a moment. Then she smiled, for the first time, a small tired smile.

"You don't know?" she said. "Colonel, they broke the second belt."

**THE SECOND BELT, LEAF 57-40. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 107 TO 160**

News of the *52nd*'s success reached Quarterdeck Nineteen within the watch, and reinforcements were moving to widen the breach within two.

Keddy saw it all, from the forward trench of the second belt on Leaf 57-40, where the *52nd* had dug itself in. The first to arrive was a battery of quad-launchers, towed across no-man's land behind tractors in the half-light of the first bell, lurching and bouncing over the craters, and it came into action just beyond the second belt's forward trench and broke up the Arsenal's last attempts at a counter-attack with salvo after salvo of rockets, the long white trails of them going overhead in fours. Behind it came the tanks and the Hoplons and the heavier guns. Behind them came the Third's outrider squadrons on their armoured hounds, great lean grey beasts in plate with riders crouched on their backs, which Keddy had never seen before. They came through the gap in a long loping stream, hundreds of them, and wheeled east and west along the inside of the second belt and went racing away along it, behind the Archflamen's cohorts, to take them from the rear.

And the second belt collapsed.

It went just as the outer belt had gone six rotations before. Keddy had heard the veterans tell it, the soldiers who had been on the Flats since the beginning: how the outer belt had held for rotation after rotation, and then broken in one place, and then gone all at once, every nest and every trench along its whole length emptying in a few cycles as the Archflamen's commanders pulled their soldiers back before they could be cut off. Now it happened again. The Archflamen's commanders left rearguards to die in the nests and withdrew the rest of their army behind the inner belt, and one by one the Third's regiments advanced into the second belt, in places without a shot fired against them. Any of the Archflamen's cohorts that failed to escape before the ring closed were surrounded and destroyed.

The *52nd* didn't take part in most of it. There weren't enough of the *52nd* left to take part in anything. They held Leaf 57-40, the first gap, and dug in, and watched the rest of the Third go past them.

Keddy commanded them. Nobody had told him to. The *52nd*'s second-in-command had transferred on the first cycle of the Big Push and its three senior centurions in the night attack, and Keddy was the colonel's optio, and the centurions who were left looked at him, and he found himself giving orders. He gave them as he thought she would have given them. He walked the regiment's front every night with her notebook in his breast pocket, and her map, and a lantern.

He found the dugout on Leaf 57-41 on one of those nights, in the second belt's old support trench, where the Arsenal's soldiers had lived. It was a deep dugout, much deeper than the Third's, with a long iron stair going down and a room at the bottom with bunks and a table. On the steel joist above the table, somebody had scratched something with the point of a knife, in neat small capitals.

It was in the Arsenal's script, which Keddy couldn't read. He copied it into the colonel's notebook, carefully, and later, when she was back, he showed it to her.

She looked at it for a long time.

"Do you know what it says, Colonel?" he asked.

"No," she said. "But I can guess."

---

She came back to the regiment on the hundred and twentieth cycle, down the Stair from Kappa, and Keddy met her at the foot of the communication trench on Leaf 57-40 with the notebook and the signal in his hand.

She took them from him and looked at them. She opened the notebook and turned the pages, slowly, the columns of figures in her small neat hand, the times and the ranges and the ninety seconds. She unfolded the signal and read it again. *You may try. Give it everything.* Then she folded it and put it back in her breast pocket, behind the notebook, where it had been.

"How many, Bram?" she said.

He had known she would ask. He had the slate ready.

"The night attack, Colonel. Eleven hundred and four transferred. Thirty-eight null." He swallowed. "And the Big Push. The whole of it. For the *52nd*. Six thousand, two hundred transferred, and a hundred and ninety null."

She was quiet for a long time.

"And the Third?"

"They're still counting, Colonel. They say..." He stopped.

"Tell me."

"Two million, nine hundred thousand transfers, Colonel. In sixty cycles. A hundred and eighty thousand null." He looked down at the slate. "They say the Third has transferred twenty-three million soldiers on the Flats since the siege began. And six hundred and eighty thousand haven't come back."

She stood at the foot of the communication trench with the notebook in her hand and looked out over the parapet at the second belt, at the wrecked trenches and the craters and the long smoking line of the inner belt beyond it.

"It keeps what you give it," she said quietly.

"Colonel?"

"Nothing, Bram." She put the notebook away. "Show me the regiment."

**LEAF 57-40, THE OLD SECOND BELT. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 162**

On the hundred and sixty-second cycle of Rotation Eight, a Phaethon of Battery *26*, *3rd* Ordnance Regiment, Tenth Ordnance Phalanx, fired from a new pit on the old second belt, and Colonel Lowe stood on the parados of the *52nd*'s trench on Leaf 57-40 and watched it go.

She had heard it was coming. Everyone had. The gun pits had been going in along the old second belt for twenty cycles, as fast as the engineers could dig them, deep and wide and lined with steel, and the Phaethons had come up the old communication trenches behind their tractors one at a time to fill them. The Tenth Ordnance had worked it out on the first day. From the old second belt, for the first time, the Third's heaviest guns could reach the spur.

The Phaethon fired at the first bell. Lowe heard the sound of it, a single enormous flat crash from somewhere to her left along the old second belt, and saw the shell go up, a faint red spark climbing into the ash cloud on a long high arc toward the spur. She watched it rise. She watched it reach the top of its arc, and turn, and begin to fall, toward the dark mass of the spur and the Cinder Wall and the inner belt, and beyond them, rising up in tiers against the red glow of the ash cloud, the terraces of the Acropolis.

Forty seconds.

The shell landed on the lower terraces of the Acropolis. She saw the flash, small and far away, a single bright point of light on the dark face of the spur, and then, a long moment afterward, heard the sound of it come back across the Flats, faint and dull, a single thud.

All along the old second belt, the soldiers of the Third were standing on their parapets, watching, and when the sound came back they began to cheer. Lowe heard it spread along the trenches, from the *52nd* to the *50th* and the *54th* and on, along the whole sixty-one kilometres of the new front, a long ragged roar of voices in the half-light.

For the first time in the war, the Third's guns could reach the Archflamen.

Keddy was standing beside her on the parados. He wasn't cheering. He was looking at the place on the face of the spur where the shell had landed.

"Will it make any difference, Colonel?" he said.

Lowe thought about the cold thing in the dark. She thought about how it had looked at her and looked away, down, toward the shape of what it wanted at the bottom of everything.

"I don't know," she said. "I don't think he's up there to be reached."

"The Archflamen?"

"No," Lowe said. She was looking past the Acropolis now, past the spur and the Cinder Wall, at the thin black needle of the Echo Mast standing up against the red glow of the ash cloud, and at the dull red light beyond it, down at the bottom of the spur, where the Phlegethon ran. "I don't think it's the Archflamen we should be trying to reach."
