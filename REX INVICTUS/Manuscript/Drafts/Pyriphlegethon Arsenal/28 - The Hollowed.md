## Chapter 28: The Hollowed

*"To fight the thing that eats the soul and come away whole, you keep your purpose narrow. Each of us conquers herself first. Then the rest."*

*Hunt-Elder Melaina Sarn, to the Hunters of the Third, before the cleansing of the Arsenal*

---

**THE *SELENE'S ARROW*, THE HIGH GEOMETRY OVER THE CINDER FLATS. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 101**

Shield-Captain Antheia Roe of the Sixth Moonwall stood on the bridge of the moon-ship *Selene's Arrow* with her helmet under her arm and watched the navigator take them down through the deep geometry by feel, and tried, as she had tried on every one of the eleven cycles out from Luna, not to watch the navigator's hands.

She failed. Everybody did. The navigator was a girl of perhaps sixteen, from one of the Hunt's own houses of navigators on the far side of Luna, bound to the Director by an oath her family had sworn before the Empire had a name, and she sat cross-legged in the navigator's cradle at the front of the bridge with her eyes shut and her bare hands resting lightly on the two silver spheres of the helm, and the ship went where her hands told it. She didn't look at the augurs. She didn't look at anything. She hummed, now and then, under her breath, and turned her hands a fraction on the spheres, and the *Selene's Arrow* slid down through the dark between the levels of the deep on routes no pilot of the Fleet would ever have attempted, through gaps in the geometry that the Fleet's charts showed as solid rock.

Roe had flown with the Hunt's navigators for sixty years. She still couldn't watch it without her stomach turning over.

"Depth 38," said the ship's master, beside her, quietly. "Depth 39. We're over the Flats, Captain."

The navigator opened her eyes.

Below the bridge's forward ports, the ash cloud over the Cinder Flats rolled away from horizon to horizon, brown and red and endless, lit from beneath by the guns. And out of it, twenty kilometres off, the dark cone of the Acropolis rose, with the faint gold shimmer of its wards about its summit, and the Mast above it.

Roe looked at it for a long time.

"Is that it?" asked her second, Lochia Venn, at her shoulder. Venn had never been in Tartarus before. Most of the company hadn't.

"That's it."

"It's smaller than I thought."

"It'll get bigger," Roe said.

The *Selene's Arrow* came down through the high geometry over the Flats on the hundred and first cycle of Rotation Sixteen. She was among the fastest vessels in the Empire, long and slender and silver, heavily armed for her size, and her translation chambers could put several squads on the ground at once. Only the Hunt flew ships of her kind. With them the Hunt could place a strike force anywhere in the Empire faster than anyone else, and the first company of the Sixth Moonwall, two hundred shieldmaidens and flame hunters, the oldest fighting company of the Hunt, was aboard her. They had been trained for nothing in their lives but fighting the things that came out of the pit.

Roe went down to the Flats by translation that afternoon, alone, to report to the Lieutenant.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 101**

She found Thalia Grace in the Marshal's office on Quarterdeck Nineteen, behind a desk that was far too big for her, with a lump of slag on it for a paperweight and a map of the nulls on the wall behind her, eating a ration biscuit and reading a slate of shell figures with an expression of deep loathing.

"Captain Roe," Thalia said, without looking up. "Sit down. Have a biscuit. They're terrible."

Roe sat. She didn't take a biscuit.

"Lieutenant."

"I've been here sixty cycles," Thalia said, still reading. "Do you know what I've learned in sixty cycles, Captain? I've learned that a Phaethon round weighs two tonnes and a Phaethon barrel lasts four hundred rounds before the rifling's gone and the Quiet Ones can make eleven barrels a rotation. I've learned that the Third needs nine hundred thousand shells a rotation to hold the line and the Directorate's giving it five hundred and forty. I've learned that a regiment that's been through the vats eight times on average can't attack across more than six hundred metres of open ground before the transfers get too slow to keep the momentum." She put the slate down. "And I've learned that every single person on this deck has been doing this for fifteen rotations and is far better at it than me."

Roe said nothing.

"So I've kept them," Thalia said. "Kastellan's staff, all of them. Dray's my chief of staff. The Phalanx-Generals keep their commands. I've put my Hunters over them as a second chain, and the second chain can overrule the first, and so far it hasn't had to." She looked at Roe at last. "That's going to change. Sooner or later something's going to come up out of that river that the first chain can't handle, and then the second chain's going to have to. Which is where you come in."

"Lieutenant."

"You'll stay on the *Arrow*. You and your company. You're my reserve. You answer to me and nobody else. When something comes up that the Third can't fight, you go." Thalia paused. "I don't know when that'll be. I don't know what it'll be. Kestrel doesn't either. She says it's soon."

Roe looked at her for a moment.

"Lieutenant," she said. "May I speak plainly?"

"Please. Everyone else on this deck calls me *Lieutenant* in a voice that means *child*."

"The whole Hunt knows why you're here."

Thalia's face didn't change.

"Yes," she said. "It does."

"The Moonwall doesn't care," Roe said. "I want you to know that. We've been fighting the things that come out of the pit for longer than you've been in the Hunt, and we've served under Lieutenants who were sent down here as rewards and Lieutenants who were sent down here as punishments, and in the end it's never made a difference. The pit doesn't care either." She stood. "We're yours, Lieutenant. Send us when you need us."

Thalia looked at her for a long time. Then she picked up another biscuit.

"Thank you," she said. "Get out before I embarrass us both."

**THE *35th*'S COMMAND DUGOUT, LEAF 57-44. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 120**

She went to see Kastellan on the hundred and twentieth cycle.

She went to see him before every major operation, from the first, and she never told anyone why. Dray thought she was being polite. Celyn thought she was being careful. Kastellan, who sat across the camp table from her in the *35th*'s command dugout on Leaf 57-44 in his greatcoat with a mug of tea in his big hands, probably knew exactly why, and never said.

The relationship between them stayed correct, and cool. She'd expected that. Kastellan regarded the Hunt as an instrument of the Director's displeasure, and Thalia regarded herself in much the same light. Both of them knew it. Neither of them let it get in the way.

"The east," she said, and spread the map on the table between them.

Kastellan looked at it.

"Yes," he said. "I wondered when you'd get to the east."

It was the problem that had occupied him for most of a rotation before the Conclave. The siege couldn't be finished while the east stayed open. The ring round the Acropolis had a gap in it, out on the open Flats beyond the old southern ring that had been given up after the Demus Rim, and while the gap was there the Archflamen had a line of retreat. Even if the Third took the cone, the war might go on somewhere else, if his people could slip out of it through the gap and fight on from the deep holds. And the open flank tied down the *5th* Line Phalanx, which had to guard the rear of the *1st* against attack from the east, so that four good regiments and their guns sat on the defensive when they were needed somewhere else.

"I'm going to close it," Thalia said.

"How?"

She showed him. She put her finger on the map at the north end of the *5th* Line Phalanx's front, and drew it round: south, and then south-west, in a long curving sweep across sixty kilometres of open Flats, to Leaf 57-44, where the *35th* stood. Kastellan's regiment. The southernmost regiment on the *7th* Line Phalanx's front.

"Like a door," she said. "On a hinge. The *1st* Line Phalanx's positions are the hinge. The *5th* swings round them, regiment by regiment, in echelon from north to south. The *23rd* first, then the *24th* and the *26th*, and the *28th* last. They come round and shut the gap and join hands with you here." She tapped the *35th*'s position. "And the Archflamen's inside, and the door's shut."

Kastellan looked at the map for a long time.

"Sixty kilometres," he said.

"Yes."

"Open ground. Held by the Unremembered and the Lethe-blooded cohorts the whole way. The outriders' reports say the whole of it."

"Yes."

"And the Grey." He put his finger on the map, on the pale wash along the Lethe Cut, which had spread north over three rotations until it lay across a third of the ground she meant the *5th* to cross. "You'll be going through the Grey, Lieutenant."

"I know."

"The *5th* will be going through the Grey."

"I know that too." She met his eyes. "I'm giving it the *16th* and *20th* Armoured for a fist. They're sealed against the Grey. The tanks can go where the infantry can't. And there'll be Hunters with every regiment. And Roe's company on the *Arrow* if it goes wrong." She paused. "Colonel. Tell me it's a bad plan."

Kastellan was quiet a long time.

"It's not a bad plan," he said at last. "It's the plan I'd have made, if I'd stayed. I'd have made it a rotation ago." He looked down at the map. "I didn't. I kept waiting for a better one."

"There isn't one."

"No," Kastellan said. "There isn't." He put down his tea. "When?"

"The hundred and fiftieth."

"Then tell me the price, Lieutenant," he said. "Dray will have given it to you. Tell me."

She told him. He listened to the whole of it without moving, and at the end of it he nodded, once.

"Thank you," he said. "Now go and do it."

**LEAF 60-38, THE *26th* TRENCH REGIMENT. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 150**

The *23rd* Trench Regiment went first, at the first bell of the hundred and fiftieth cycle, behind a rolling barrage mixed with smoke, with a seep-storm coming in over the Lethe Cut. Trooper Bryn Askew of the *26th* watched it go from the parapet of the *26th*'s forward trench on Leaf 60-38, eight kilometres to the south, through a periscope, and thought he was watching the end of the world.

The ground looked more graveyard than front. The stretch in front of the *5th* Line Phalanx had been fought over for rotations, back and forth, by both sides, and nobody had ever cleared it. It was covered from end to end in the wreckage of that fighting, as far as Askew could see through the periscope: old tanks and guns half buried in the ash or tipped nose-down into shell holes, the broken remains of trenches and pillboxes, stretches of wire that had once guarded something and now guarded nothing at all, rusted and sagging in the brown light. Under the ash, everyone knew, there were mines nobody had mapped and shells that had never gone off, and either could go off without warning after rotations in the ground. And over all of it, to the south, lay the Grey.

Askew had never been in the Grey. Nobody in the *26th* had, who'd come back to talk about it.

The *23rd*'s leading cohorts went forward behind the barrage, each with a company of the *16th* Armoured joined to it as a battle group, tanks and infantry together. For the first hours it went well. Askew watched the *23rd*'s cohorts move out across the wreck-strewn ash with the tanks among them, and saw them reach the first of the old trench lines and go over it and on.

Then the Archflamen's counter-barrage came down, and among the smoke of its bursts there was a paler and heavier cloud that didn't rise.

Askew saw it. It came down out of the bursts of the counter-barrage along the *23rd*'s front in long slow grey drifts, heavier than smoke, and lay on the ground, and spread, and the *23rd*'s cohorts walked into it.

"That's the Grey," said Centurion Holm, beside him, very quietly. "That's Grey Seventh."

The Third had known the distillate since the *54th*'s last stand on the Lethe Cut, seven rotations before. It still had no idea what it was, beyond the fact that soldiers who breathed it began to forget, and soldiers who died in it reached Watchpoint Hades blank, or didn't reach it at all. The cloud lay across the *23rd*'s front. And the *23rd*'s attack fell apart in it.

Askew watched it fall apart. He watched the cohorts lose their way in the smoke, turning, milling, going in the wrong direction. He watched sections walk into the thickest of the Grey and simply stop, and stand in the ash, and not move, and he understood without being told that they no longer knew where they were or what they'd come to do. The relay at the *23rd*'s command bunker logged one in three of the uploads from the dead in the cloud as empty. Askew heard that afterward. At the time he only saw the soldiers standing still.

The tanks went on. They were sealed against the Grey. They became the point of the attack, grinding forward through the cloud with the infantry stumbling behind them or not at all, and visibility inside the cloud was a few metres, and they drove into unseen shell holes and old tank ditches and stuck, and several of them struck mines. Askew saw a Thureos go up on a mine in the middle of the cloud with a long dull orange flash and a column of black smoke that rose out of the grey and leaned over in the wind. He saw Arsenal tanks come out of the cloud at point-blank range on a squadron of the *16th* and burn the whole of it in a minute, and go back in.

But the attack's weight kept it moving. Wherever the Grey thinned, the *23rd* pressed on against scattered resistance. By the end of the first cycle it had gained ten kilometres. On the second cycle the *24th* joined the attack to its south. On the fourth, the Archflamen's commanders organised a proper defence, and the Unremembered came up out of the Lethe Cut in force with their own armour, and behind the Unremembered came things the Third hadn't met on the Flats before.

Laistrygons who had been in the Grey too long, and no longer knew what they were. Blind drakon-spawn from the deep river channels, pale and huge and eyeless, that went for the tanks by the heat of their engines. Other things, without names, that came out of the mist.

On the fifth cycle, the *26th* went in.

**THE GREY. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 155, DARK WATCH**

The *26th* had gone eleven kilometres by the dark watch of the fifth cycle, and dug in for the night in a line of old Arsenal trenches on the edge of the Grey, and Askew was on sentry on the fire-step of the *2nd* Cohort's bay at the third hour when the soldiers came out of the mist.

He saw them at a hundred metres. Shapes in the grey dark, coming toward the trench across the ash. Many of them. A long loose crowd, not in step, not in any order, walking.

"Stand to," he said into the band. "Stand to. Movement. Front. A hundred metres."

The *26th*'s *2nd* Cohort stood to along the trench in the dark, quietly, and Centurion Holm came along the bay at a crouch and stood beside him on the fire-step and looked out.

"What is it?"

"I don't know, Centurion. Infantry. A lot of them."

Holm lifted his periscope, and looked through it for a long time, and Askew heard him draw in his breath.

"Those are ours," Holm said.

Askew looked.

They were coming out of the Grey in the Third's grey coats. Hundreds of them. In the Third's field uniforms, filthy and torn and stained, and walking toward the *26th*'s trench in a long loose crowd. Without helmets. Without rifles. With their hands hanging empty at their sides, and their faces turned toward the trench, and no expression on their faces at all.

"Halt," Holm called. His voice cracked. "Halt! Who goes there? Halt or we fire!"

The soldiers in the grey coats kept walking. None of them answered. None of them stopped.

"Halt!"

They kept walking. Fifty metres. Forty. Askew could see them clearly now, in the faint red light of the cone behind them. Young men and women. Old ones. Thin and stooped and broad and short, all sorts, the sort of soldiers you'd see in any bay on the Flats. Some of them had wounds. Terrible wounds. One of them, near the front, walking steadily toward the trench with the rest, had no left arm below the elbow, and the stump wasn't bleeding. And on the shoulders of their coats, on most of them, Askew saw a regimental flash. He couldn't read it in the dark.

"Fire," Holm said.

Nobody fired.

Askew had his rifle at his shoulder. He had a soldier in its sights, a young woman, walking toward him with her empty hands at her sides. She was wearing the Third's grey, and she had his sister's face, and he couldn't pull the trigger.

Nobody in the *2nd* Cohort could.

"Fire!" Holm shouted. "Fire, damn you, fire, that's an order..."

The first of them reached the parapet.

He was a big man, heavy-shouldered, in a torn grey coat with no helmet, and he came over the parapet into the bay without slowing, without a sound, and took the trooper on the fire-step three metres from Askew by the throat with both hands and bore him down into the bottom of the bay, and Askew heard the trooper scream and then stop screaming. And then they were all coming over the parapet. All along the trench. Into the bays. Without rifles, without bayonets, without anything, with their hands, and their teeth, and the soldiers of the *26th* who had held their fire for the Third's grey went down under them in the dark.

The Hollowed didn't hesitate.

Askew fired. He fired at last, point-blank, into the big man kneeling in the bottom of the bay over the trooper he'd strangled, and the big man jerked and fell sideways and lay still, and Askew turned and fired at the next, and the next. All along the trench, the *2nd* Cohort was firing now. Too late, for some of them. Askew fired until his magazine was empty and reloaded and fired again, and the grey-coated soldiers came on over the parapet into the fire without stopping, without ducking, without making a sound. They fell in heaps on the parapet and in the bays. They kept coming.

The big man he'd shot got up.

Askew saw it. He saw the big man stir in the bottom of the bay, where he'd fallen, and put his hands under him, and push himself up onto his knees, with a hole in his chest that Askew had put there at a range of two metres. And stand. And turn toward him.

Askew shot him again. And again. In the head, the third time. The big man fell, and this time didn't get up.

It went on until the end of the dark watch. The Hollowed came out of the Grey all night, in hundreds, and the *26th* killed them in hundreds, and they kept coming regardless of their losses. Some of those that were cut down rose again and had to be destroyed again, and again. Those that reached the parapet fought with their hands and their teeth. At the first bell they stopped coming, and the *26th* stood in its trenches in the grey light among the heaps of dead in the Third's grey coats, and nobody said anything for a long time.

The *26th* called them the Hollowed. Somebody in the *2nd* Cohort said it first, that morning, standing in the bay looking down at the dead, and it went down the trench and up the line to the *5th* Line Phalanx and to Quarterdeck Nineteen within the cycle, and nobody ever called them anything else.

---

Askew went through the pockets of the young woman he hadn't been able to shoot, at the first bell.

He didn't know why. He'd never searched a body in his life. She was lying on her back in the ash at the foot of the parapet with her eyes open and her hands at her sides, where somebody else had shot her in the dark, and she had his sister's face, and he climbed down over the parapet and knelt beside her and looked at her for a long time.

She had the flash of the *54th* Trench Regiment on her shoulder. He could read it now in the light. The *54th*, that had been raised from the ration rolls of the Lethe Hold and sent to the Lethe Cut in Rotation Eight and lost there, every soldier of it, in Rotation Nine. Null and absent. Everyone on the Flats had heard of the *54th*. Most of the dead on the parapet wore the same flash.

In the inside pocket of her coat he found a notebook.

It was a thick black notebook with a cracked cover, warped by damp and heat, and it was full. Every page of it, from the first to the last, in two different hands, one small and neat and one larger and rounder, was covered in names. Columns of them. Hundreds of names to a page. Thousands. Under each name, a cohort number, and a century, and a home hold. And at the end of the last column on the last page, in the small neat hand, a line on its own.

*Fourteen thousand one hundred. C. Vadas, Colonel.*

And under that, in pencil, in the larger round hand gone thin and shaky, a single word, written over and over, down the whole of the last page's margin, so many times that the pencil had worn through the paper in places.

*Counting. Counting. Counting. Counting.*

Askew knelt in the ash and held it.

He looked at the young woman's face. Then he looked for a name tag, at her collar, and found it, and read it.

*Pike, J. Trooper. 54th Trench Regiment.*

He put the notebook inside his own coat. He took it up to Centurion Holm, and Holm looked at it and took it to the *26th*'s colonel, and the colonel looked at it for a long time and then sent it, by runner, under seal, to Quarterdeck Nineteen.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 156**

Thalia read the notebook at her desk on the hundred and fifty-sixth cycle, with Dray standing beside her, and when she'd turned the last page she sat for a long time looking at the pencil in the margin.

"Tell me," she said.

Dray told her. The *54th* Trench Regiment, raised from the ration rolls of the Lethe Hold in Rotation Eight and sent to the Flats as a whole regiment, bakers and clerks and dockhands, none of whom had ever been to Depth 40, none of whom had ever transferred. Colonel Corin Vadas, who had run the ration office of the Lethe Hold for twenty years and was the best-natured man anyone on the Flats had ever met. The Lethe Cut, on the left of the *52nd*, at the edge of the Grey. The mist. The Grey Seventh. Vadas and his adjutant copying fourteen thousand one hundred names into two notebooks so that somebody would know them. Vadas going null at the Cut on the fifteenth cycle of Rotation Nine. Ten thousand nine hundred null. Three thousand two hundred listed absent, a category the Répertoire had invented for the purpose, having no other word for soldiers whose implants had stopped reporting and whose bodies were never found.

"And this is one of the notebooks," Thalia said.

"Yes, Lieutenant."

"Where's the other?"

"Nobody knows. Vadas's adjutant had it. Ottilie Crane. She's listed absent too."

Thalia looked at the pencil in the margin again. *Counting. Counting. Counting.*

"Trooper Pike," she said. "The one who had this. She was listed absent."

"Yes, Lieutenant. The Répertoire listed her absent on the fifteenth cycle of Rotation Nine." Dray paused. "The *26th* reports that she came out of the Grey last night with the rest of them. In her coat. With this in her pocket. Walking." He paused again. "Lieutenant, they're the absent of the *54th*. The three thousand two hundred. Most of the Hollowed on the *26th*'s front last night wore the *54th*'s flash. And some of them had died in the Grey since. The *23rd*'s dead. The ones that reached Watchpoint Hades blank. They stood up again in the ash." He looked at her. "The Third's dead are fighting for the Archflamen."

Thalia closed the notebook.

"Get me Sarn," she said.

---

Hunt-Elder Sarn went out to the *26th*'s front on the hundred and fifty-seventh cycle, on her silver legs, and spent a day among the Hollowed.

She examined them where they lay. The dead ones, in heaps on the *26th*'s parapet and in no-man's land in front of it, and the ones the *26th* had taken alive in the dark watch, three of them, tied hand and foot in a dugout in the support line, who lay on the floor with their faces turned toward the Wall and said nothing and didn't struggle. Sarn knelt beside each of them in turn with her silver hand on their foreheads and her moonstone eye glowing, and stayed there for an hour at a time, and the *26th*'s soldiers watched from the dugout door and didn't come in.

She reported to Thalia that night.

"Lethe-work," she said.

Thalia waited.

"The Grey takes the soldier out and leaves the body," Sarn said. "That's what Grey Seventh does, Lieutenant. That's all it does. It's the Lethe, distilled. It's forgetting. It takes everything a soldier is and washes it away, the name and the memories and the self, until there's nothing left in the body but the body. That's the null. That's the absent. And then the Unremembered put something else in." She was quiet a moment. "I don't know what. I've been in Tartarus since before the Third was raised and I don't know what. It's not the soldier. It's not a soul. It's something of the river's. It wears them."

"Can they be brought back?"

"No."

"Can it be stopped?"

"I don't know yet," Sarn said. "I'll find out."

Thalia's note on Sarn's report went into the Hunt's record that night, and was quoted afterward in every history of the siege. It was three sentences.

*If the Third's dead fight for the Archflamen, he will have as many soldiers as the Third gives him. No army can fight a war of attrition against an enemy that grows with every loss it inflicts. This changes everything.*

Within two cycles, the *24th* and the *23rd* were reporting Hollowed on their fronts as well.

**THE GREY, LEAF 59-45. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 158**

After eight cycles of fighting, the *5th* Line Phalanx's hook had stuck in the Grey.

The ground taken in the first cycles was held. But the *23rd*'s long sweep had reached only halfway to its objective, and the enemy in front of the *5th* was now nearly equal to it in numbers, and more Hollowed came out of the Grey every watch. The *5th* was killing them in thousands. They kept coming. Every soldier of the *5th* who died in the Grey, every one whose upload reached Watchpoint Hades blank, might be standing up in the ash in the dark watch with the rest of them, walking toward his own regiment's parapet.

Hunter Celyn was with the *5th*. She'd been with it since the first bell of the hook, in the *23rd*'s forward command bunker, with a squad of Psiloi and a sister-augur, and on the hundred and fifty-eighth cycle she came on the band to Quarterdeck Nineteen.

"Lieutenant," she said. "The Styx-salt."

Thalia had known she would ask. She'd known since the first night.

The Third had kept a reserve of Styx-salt shells since the Second Grand Offensive, rotations before the siege. Nobody on the Flats was supposed to know about it. It was sealed in a deep magazine at Kappa and listed in the Répertoire's books as destroyed, and the Hunt had found it in the first week of Thalia's command, going through Vasska's shell list line by line, where Vasska had written in the margin beside it, in her small precise hand, *Not destroyed. Lying.*

Styx-salt burned the Lethe out of anything it touched. The oath-river against the forgetting-river. It was the one thing in the Empire's arsenals that the Hunt had ever found would undo Lethe-work.

It also poisoned the ground it fell on, and the air above it, for centuries.

"How much ground?" Thalia asked.

"Two leaves," Celyn said. "Leaf 59-45 and Leaf 60-45. That's where they're coming out. That's the heart of the Grey on our front, Lieutenant, that's where the Unremembered are putting them in. If we drown those two leaves, the hook can close."

"And the leaves?"

"The Répertoire says four hundred years." Celyn's voice was steady. "Before anyone can stand on them."

Thalia sat at the desk with the band in her hand and looked at the notebook, which was still lying on the desk in front of her where she'd left it two cycles before. *Counting. Counting. Counting.*

"Do it," she said.

---

They fired it on the hundred and fifty-ninth cycle.

Roe's company came down from the *Selene's Arrow* by translation for it, at the first bell, to stand with the *23rd* and the *26th* on the edge of the Grey in case the Hollowed came out under the bombardment, and Roe stood on the parapet of the *26th*'s forward trench on Leaf 60-38 beside Trooper Bryn Askew and watched the shells go over.

They were silver. She'd known they would be. Every shieldmaiden of the Moonwall had been taught about Styx-salt. She had never seen it fired. They came over from the batteries behind the line in long glittering arcs and came down on Leaf 59-45 and Leaf 60-45 two kilometres ahead, in the heart of the Grey, and burst, and where they burst the Grey burned.

It burned silver. A cold white silver fire that ran along the ground through the grey vapour and ate it, and the vapour writhed under it and boiled away, and the ground under it went black, and glittered, and steamed. The salt went down into the ash and the ground and the rock beneath it, and Roe could feel it from two kilometres away, a hard bitter cold taste on her tongue and at the back of her throat, the taste of an oath sworn and broken.

The Hollowed came out of the Grey under it.

They came out walking, as they always did, in their hundreds, in the Third's grey coats, toward the *26th*'s parapet, and the silver fire ran over them. And where it touched them, they stopped.

Roe saw it. She saw a grey-coated figure walk through the silver fire at a kilometre's range and stop in the middle of it, quite still, and stand there for a moment with the fire running over it. Then it lay down. It lay down in the ash, slowly, as a tired soldier lies down at the end of a march, and didn't get up. All across the two leaves, the Hollowed were lying down in the silver fire. Hundreds of them. Thousands, Askew thought afterward. Lying down in the burning ash and not getting up.

Askew stood on the parapet beside her and watched them, and Roe saw that he was weeping.

"Trooper," she said.

"Captain."

"You know some of them."

"No, Captain." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "No. I don't know any of them. That's the thing." He watched them lie down. "They were ours, Captain. They were the *54th*. They were bakers and clerks and dockhands, and nobody ever knew their names, and the last of them was walking about in the Grey for seven rotations with a notebook in her pocket, counting." He swallowed. "And now they're lying down."

Roe said nothing for a long time.

"They're at rest," she said, at last. "I think. If anything is."

"Do you know that, Captain?"

"No," Roe said. "I don't know it."

Leaves 59-45 and 60-45 were drowned in the salt that cycle. The Répertoire estimated that they wouldn't be fit to stand on for four hundred years. The hook closed on the hundred and sixty-second cycle, when the *28th* Trench Regiment's leading cohorts came round the southern edge of the drowned ground and joined hands with the *35th* on Leaf 57-44, and Colonel Kastellan of the *35th* walked out into no-man's land to meet the *28th*'s colonel in person and shook his hand in front of both regiments.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 170**

The hook had closed the ring. The cost of closing it was measured in more than transfers.

The *5th* Line Phalanx had lost two hundred and forty thousand soldiers in eight cycles. The Répertoire logged sixty-one thousand of them as null and nine thousand as absent, the first time the Third had used the second category in a report since the Lethe Cut. Thalia read the figure and asked Dray what it meant, and Dray told her that it meant nine thousand of the *5th*'s soldiers were somewhere in the Grey, walking, and that the Styx-salt hadn't reached them.

The decision to use Styx-salt was a political one as well, and Thalia had known it would be. The Pontifical College protested to the Council of the Twelve that the Hunt had poisoned ground within sight of the Katabasion. The Dionysian Logistics Directorate complained that two leaves of the Flats had been removed from any future use.

Thalia answered both in a single paragraph, which Dray sent up the cables on the hundred and seventieth cycle.

*The leaves in question were in use by the enemy. The Third's dead are no longer available to fight for him. Grace.*

The protests were noted. They were also remembered, and the College would come back to them before the rotation was out.

The appearance of the Hollowed changed the Répertoire's arithmetic for the rest of the siege. Until Rotation Sixteen, a soldier lost on the Flats had been lost to the Third and nothing worse. From the hook onward, a soldier lost in the Grey might be added to the enemy's strength, and the Répertoire's estimates of the Archflamen's army, which had fallen every rotation since the inner belt broke, began to rise again.

The Third issued new orders on the handling of its dead. Bodies left in the Grey were to be burned where possible. And regiments advancing through ground where the Grey had fallen were to treat any figure in the Third's colours that didn't answer a challenge as hostile.

The order was obeyed. It did nothing for the morale of the regiments obliged to carry it out.

Thalia kept the notebook. She kept it in the drawer of the Marshal's desk, where Kastellan had kept Corvis's list, and where Castorre before him had kept the Archflamen's letter. On the hundred and seventy-first cycle she sent a copy of every page of it, made by the Hunt's own clerks, up the cables to Commander Thaleia at Lethe Hold, with a note in her own small quick untidy hand.

*The Lethe Hold's own. Found on the Flats, Rotation Sixteen. Trooper J. Pike of the 54th was still counting. T. Grace, Lieutenant of the Hunt of the Director. (Full name and seal, as requested.)*

---

**HEIGHT 202, THE NORTHERN SLOPES. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 230**

The *16th* and *20th* Armoured needed a complete refit after the hook, and the *1st* Line Phalanx postponed its offensive to wait for them. So on the two hundred and thirtieth cycle, the *11th* Line Phalanx attacked alone, along a front of fifteen kilometres, toward the Wall on the north side.

It was the least experienced of the Third's phalanxes. Its three regiments had been on the Flats for only six rotations.

The *68th* Trench Regiment had the hardest task. Its sector took it up the northern slopes of a range of old cinder vents, gone cold before the Arsenal was built, that ran south across the Flats toward the cone and ended at its foot. Its first objective was Height 187. After that it had to take three hardened ceiling-lance silos, built to survive a direct hit from anything the Third could fire, before the final climb to Height 202, from whose crest it would look down on the Cinder Wall. The *70th* had further to go over easier ground, with two lines of trenches to storm before it reached the Wall. The *10th* Ordnance Phalanx had three hundred and fifty tanks ready to support the attack, with thirty Colossi among them, and the Behemoths of Talos stood by. Sappers in every regiment's sector had been at work for a rotation under the Archflamen's works, and the battle opened with their mines going up along the slopes and their saps breaking out behind the strongpoints they'd marked.

In a seep-storm, the leading cohorts of the *68th* began the climb.

Within a few hours the advance had been halted along the whole line. The strongpoints on the slopes had been dug deep, with clear fields of fire downhill, and the cohorts coming up the slopes behind the first wave were cut to pieces by guns sheltered behind the Wall. To bring the tanks forward, the *109th* Sapper Company had been ordered to build a road up the slope. Its sappers worked under fire through the whole of the cycle, blasting rock and clearing a track for the armour waiting at the foot of the slope. The work went slowly. Without tanks to destroy the gun pits, the soldiers on the slope could do little but endure.

On the left of the *11th*'s attack, the *70th* opened with a charge by four hundred and fifty outriders on ash-bikes along the old ore road.

There was no barrage before it. The staff believed the guns would do little against the dug-in cohorts and would only warn them the attack was coming. The charge depended on surprise, and nothing else. Outrider-Optio Hedda Brakk rode in its third squadron, on a battered ash-bike with *FAUST* painted on the fuel tank in white letters, for a trooper she'd watched the *Lament of Ixion* open with in Rotation Eight, on the Demus Rim, who'd been null since.

At first it worked. The outriders came down the old ore road in the seep-storm at a hundred kilometres an hour, four hundred and fifty of them, engines screaming, in a long dark stream of bikes through the brown rain, and the Archflamen's cohorts in the trenches along the road never saw them coming until they were past. Some of the leading squadrons broke through as far as Leaf 58-45, two kilometres from the Wall.

Then the guns on the Wall found them.

Brakk saw the squadron in front of her go down. All of it, in a few seconds, in a long rolling burst of fire along the road. Bikes going end over end. Riders thrown into the ash. She went through the wreckage of them at full throttle with fragments rattling off her bike's fairing, and came out the other side, and turned off the road into a gully, and lay there under her bike with the rain falling on her, and waited for the dark.

About sixty answered the roll at the end of the cycle. She was one of them.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLES 240 TO 261**

The *11th*'s offensive went on slowly, at the usual cost in soldiers and tanks, and the *1st* Line Phalanx's delay made that cost far higher.

Thalia knew it. Everybody on Quarterdeck Nineteen knew it. With the *1st*'s front quiet, the Archflamen could shift soldiers from it to meet the *11th*, and the attrition needed to wear down the defenders on the slopes would take much longer. The *11th*'s commanders asked her to set the *1st* moving and take the pressure off them. They asked on the two hundred and fortieth cycle. They asked again on the two hundred and forty-fourth, and the two hundred and forty-eighth, and the two hundred and fifty-second.

She refused every time.

"The *1st* goes when every tank is in place," she told the *11th*'s Phalanx-General on the band, on the fourth request. "Not before. If I send it now, without the *16th* and *20th*, it'll go into the Wall the way the *5th* went into the Grey, and I'll lose it, and you'll still be on the slopes."

"Lieutenant, I'm losing a regiment a rotation on these slopes."

"I know."

"Lieutenant..."

"I know," Thalia said. "Eight cycles. The *1st* attacks in eight cycles. I'll give you that in writing."

She put down the band. Dray was watching her from the door.

"You could have sent them," he said. "Kastellan would have."

"Would he?"

Dray thought about it.

"No," he admitted. "He'd have done what you just did."

"Then stop looking at me like that," Thalia said, "and get me the *16th*'s refit figures."

For more than a week the *11th* bled alone. On the two hundred and sixty-first cycle, the *1st* Line Phalanx attacked, as she'd promised. Its three regiments struck south-west across fifteen kilometres toward the Cinder Wall, with massed tanks and Colossi at their head, and the armoured spearheads were soon breaking into the Archflamen's lines, where they met the survivors of the cohorts the *5th*'s hook had driven back into the pocket. The Unremembered fought fanatically there, and fired more of the Grey. But the Wrath wings now flew every watch, and the Archflamen's swarms and drowned Lanterns came up less and less often, until the Third seemed at last to have won the air beneath the ceiling. The heavy Wraths bombed the Cinder Wall on Leaf 58-45 and carpeted the ground behind it. Halloran flew top cover for every raid. The combined weight of all the attacks began, slowly, to grind the defenders down.

---

**GIBBET HILL. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 330**

On the *11th*'s front, the progress had been slow too. The *109th* Sapper Company's road had held back the whole attack. Cohorts had clung to the slopes waiting for tank support that couldn't reach them, while hundreds of sappers died under the shelling building the road. But even without the tanks, the ceiling-lance silos on the slopes fell one by one, in hard fighting at close quarters, and from the last of them, on the three hundred and twentieth cycle, the *68th* could see the crest of Height 202.

They could see what the Archflamen's soldiers had done there.

They'd built gallows on the crest. A long line of them, black against the red glow of the cone, a dozen or more, and from them hung prisoners of the Third. Soldiers of the *68th* and the *70th* who'd been taken on the slopes, hanging in their grey coats, turning slowly in the hot wind. As a warning. And before they'd hanged them, the Archflamen's soldiers had cut out their implants, so that every soldier on the gallows had died slowly, with nothing to carry them back to Watchpoint Hades.

Every one of them had gone null.

The Third's soldiers called the place Gibbet Hill.

---

On the three hundred and thirtieth cycle, the *68th*'s *15th* Cohort was close enough to try the crest.

There were a hundred and nine of them left, out of four hundred and eighty at the start of the offensive, its centuries down to twenty soldiers or fewer. Three times that cycle it went up the last slope behind a mortar barrage, and three times it was thrown back in the fighting at the top. On the third attempt its commander, Centurion Ilse Marrick, was shot through the body on the slope twenty metres below the crest, and lay there for several hours out of reach of a relay, with the cohort pinned in the rocks below her, until a relay team of the *68th*'s signals section crawled up the slope to her in the dark with a portable set on a sledge.

Her upload went through. She woke at Watchpoint Hades in Rotation Seventeen and asked who had the cohort.

The cohort went up a fourth time under cover of the dark watch, without a barrage, in silence, and after a confused fight in the dark among the gallows on the crest, took Gibbet Hill. There was a single optio left to command its defence. His name was Teodor Rask. He was twenty-three. He put the *15th*'s forty-one survivors into the Archflamen's trenches on the crest, facing south, toward the Wall, and waited for the counter-attack.

It never came. It was a further sign that the Archflamen's army was weakening.

At the first bell, the *68th* cut the bodies down from the gallows. Thirty-seven of them. Rask had them laid out on the crest in a row, and covered with the coats of the *15th*'s own dead, and then he and the survivors of the cohort carried them down the hill, one at a time, through the morning, to the *68th*'s lines. And they planted the regiment's standard on the crest.

Below it lay the Cinder Wall. Beyond the Wall, close now, the cone of the Acropolis, and the Mast on its summit, humming.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 335**

The capture of Gibbet Hill completed the second of the operations Thalia had planned to follow the hook.

The *11th* Line Phalanx had taken a hundred cycles to advance fifteen kilometres and had lost a hundred and ninety thousand soldiers doing it, more than its whole strength at the start of the offensive. Its regiments ended the operation largely made up of soldiers who'd been transferred during it. Its performance had exceeded the expectations of every officer on Quarterdeck Nineteen who'd doubted it, and Thalia wrote to its Phalanx-General to tell him so, and to apologise, in writing, for the eight cycles.

With the *1st* Line Phalanx on the Wall to the south and the *11th* on Height 202, the ring round the Acropolis was closed along its whole length for the first time in the siege. From the crest of Gibbet Hill, the Third's spotters could direct fire onto the inner face of the Cinder Wall and the lower slopes of the cone. The Archflamen's army could no longer move between the northern and southern halves of its perimeter without being seen. Its last stores outside the Wall, in the old casting yards along the foot of the vents, were taken or destroyed in the cycles that followed.

Thalia reported to the Director that the Arsenal was enclosed, and that the Third could begin preparing the assault on the Acropolis.

She wrote it at her desk on the three hundred and thirty-fifth cycle, late, with the notebook in the drawer and the map of the nulls on the wall behind her, and sealed it with the crescent, and sent it.

It was the last good news the Hunt would send to Luna for some time.
