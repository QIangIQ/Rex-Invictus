**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 250**

The Sapper-Prefect of the Forge's First Sapper Regiment was a narrow grey woman of about ninety named Hadria Voss, and she came into the Marshal's office on the two hundred and fiftieth cycle with a roll of survey sheets under one arm, and sat down across the desk from Kastellan without being asked, and said, "You know you can't order me."

"I know," Kastellan said.

"Good. The last Marshal of the Third who tried to order a Sapper-Prefect of the Forge was in Rotation Ninety of the Acheron war, and the Forge took its sappers home in the middle of the siege, and he lost the siege." She unrolled the sheets on the desk and weighted the corners with his inkwell and his stylus case and a lump of slag that Castorre had used as a paperweight and nobody had moved. "So. You'll ask. I'll ask my lords. My lords will say yes, because they want the Arsenal back more than you do. And then I'll do it my way."

Kastellan looked at her for a moment. Iason Dray, standing at his shoulder, looked at the ceiling.

"Hold Charybdis," Kastellan said. "Rotation Forty of the Cocytus front. You had the Fourth Company of the Ninth."

Voss stopped weighting the sheets.

"I did," she said slowly.

"You took the north gallery under the Ice Gate. You found the Frost Masons' counter-mine with your own geophone and fired a dart into it from ninety metres while their picks were still going. I was on the surface. I was the colonel of the *31st*." He leaned forward over the survey. "We went through the Gate on your mine, Prefect. I've wanted to thank you for forty years. I never knew your name."

Voss looked at him across the desk. Then she gave a short, dry bark of a laugh.

"Well, shit," she said. "That's one way to start." She went back to weighting the sheets. "All right, Marshal. Tell me where you want to dig."

He told her. He'd been thinking about it since the cycle he took command, and he'd known since he read Corvis's papers on the inner belt that he would end up here, at this desk, with a Sapper-Prefect of the Forge and a roll of survey sheets. The belt was broken. The Cinder Wall was eleven hundred metres in front of the *22nd*, and between the belt and the Wall the Frost Masons had covered the ground with pillboxes and pits, and behind the Wall was the cone and the Acropolis and the ceiling-lances and the wards. The Third could go on walking into the guns, he said, and it would get there in the end, in four or five rotations, and the Répertoire would charge it twenty million transfers and a million nulls for the walk. Or it could go underneath.

"Under the pits," he said, putting his finger on the survey sheet. "Under the strongpoints, where the guns don't bear. Come up behind them and beneath them. Open a front the Archflamen can't see. If I have to, under the Wall, and under the cone itself, under the lances and the wards, and come up inside."

"He'll fight you for every metre."

"I'm counting on it. I've more soldiers than he has, Prefect, and more stores, and more shells, and they keep coming down the cables at Kappa every cycle, and his don't. He hasn't the strength to match me on the surface and below it at once. If he tries, he'll stretch himself until he breaks."

Voss studied the sheet for a while.

"Your colonels won't like it," she said.

"No."

"They've never fought underground. None of them. The Third's never done it on any scale in six hundred years." She tapped the sheet. "And your soldiers will like it less, Marshal, once they find out what it means."

"I know what it means."

"Do you?" She looked up at him. "At eighty metres there's nothing that'll carry an upload. Twenty metres of basalt and the signal's mush. Eighty and it's nothing. A soldier who dies in my galleries doesn't come back."

"I know."

"Your Répertoire knows too. It'll have sent you a paper on it already."

It had. Dray had brought it in that morning: six leaves from the Répertoire's Assessor at Watchpoint Hades, objecting that a soldier lost ninety metres down was a permanent loss, that a gallery couldn't be priced in transfers as a trench was priced, and that every sapper lost below ground would have to be found again from the Forge's rolls, which the Répertoire couldn't draw on. Kastellan had read it twice and written his answer on the back of the last leaf.

"I told it," he said, "that the Third's losses on the surface are coming close to the point where a transfer and a null are much the same thing. A soldier who's gone up eight or nine times comes back so slowed he can't fight. I've walked the Old Ladders' bays. I've seen them. And a mine that takes a strongpoint for a few hundred sappers is cheaper in the end than a frontal assault that takes it for a hundred thousand transfers and four thousand nulls." He sat back. "The Lord Marshal agrees with me. So does the Directorate, since it's paying for your soldiers. The Répertoire can note its objection."

Voss rolled up the survey sheet slowly.

"Nine regiments," she said. "A hundred and eight thousand sappers, in companies of six hundred. Forty boring engines. My lords are sending the listening companies too, the full establishment, which they haven't done for anyone since Charybdis." She stood. "Each regiment answers to its own Sapper-Prefect, and the Prefects answer to the Forge, and the Forge answers to you, when it feels like it. That's how it's going to be. You won't like that either."

"I'll live with it."

"Good." She tucked the roll under her arm and went to the door, and stopped, and turned round. "Marshal."

"Prefect."

"The Frost Masons are here. On the Flats. Under them. They came up the river with the drowned." She held his eyes. "You know what they are. You were at Charybdis. My sappers will meet them under the ground, and they'll go onto the List by the thousand, and every one of them volunteered for it, and is paid three times what your trench soldiers are paid to do it. I want you to know their names. When they're gone. I want somebody up here to know them."

"Send them to me," Kastellan said. "Every one. I'll read them."

**LEAF 54-47, THE FORWARD TRENCH. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 252**

Sapper-Lieutenant Ansel Rook of the *66th* Sapper Company, of the Forge's Fourth Sapper Regiment, went into the forward trench of the *22nd* Trench Regiment on Leaf 54-47 with a borer's drill on his shoulder on the two hundred and fifty-second cycle and began, as his trade required, by making a hole in the ground.

He was twenty-nine. He had been a sapper of the Forge for eleven years, since the cycle he turned eighteen in the sapper hall of Hold Pyrgos, where his father and grandfather had been sappers before him and his mother had kept the roll, and he had come down the cables from the Forge with the rest of the Fourth Regiment eight cycles earlier and seen the Flats for the first time out of the window of the cable car, and thought it was the ugliest place he had ever seen in his life. He'd seen nothing since to change his mind. He was a long, lean, quiet young man with big hands and a habit of standing still and listening before he did anything, which his first optio had told him was the mark of a sapper and his mother had told him was the mark of a man who was going to be late for his own wedding.

The bore-hole went down at the back of the fifth fire bay, behind a false wall of sandbags the *22nd*'s soldiers had built for him at his request without asking why. He worked it himself, with a sapper named Brin Hollis holding the drill steady and a sapper named Nell Drover reading the core as it came up, and the *22nd*'s soldiers sat on the fire-step and watched them with interest.

"What are you looking for?" a trooper asked him, on the second hour. She was young and pale and had a crane-hand's thick forearms.

"Rock," Rook said.

"There's rock everywhere. It's all rock."

"Not the right rock."

The core came up in long grey cylinders, and Drover laid them out along the floor of the bay one after another and read them aloud, and Rook wrote it down. Cinder and ash, the first thirty metres of it, loose and grey and dry. Then pumice under the cinder, light and pale and full of holes. Drover broke a length of it in her hands and it crumbled.

"Useless," she said.

"Useless," Rook agreed, and wrote it down.

It was useless. Every sapper on the Flats found the same in the first ten cycles, and the reports went back to the Sapper-Prefects and from them to Quarterdeck Nineteen, and the Third's engineers, who had dug trenches in the cinder for nine rotations and had always rather liked it, were surprised to learn that it was the worst ground for mining any sapper of the Forge had ever seen. The cinder ran. A gallery cut through it filled itself as fast as it was dug, the walls sliding in grain by grain behind the pick until the face was buried. The seep-storms drained straight down through it, so a tunnel in the cinder turned into a stream within a cycle, and the wet cinder swelled and pushed on the bracing from every side at once, and gave no warning before it did.

Below the cinder, the core changed. At thirty-two metres it came up dark and dense and glassy, old lava, shot through with long twisting channels.

"Seep's cut it," Drover said, turning a length of it in the lamp. "Look. Channels all the way down. There'll be caves in it, and rivers, running down toward the big river." She put her palm flat against the core and held it there. "And it's warm."

"How warm?"

"Warm enough." She looked at him. "There'll be heat veins in this, Lieutenant. Same as the ones they had on the surface. I've read the reports. The ones that boiled the *44th*'s trenches in Rotation One."

Rook wrote it down. They went on drilling.

At eighty-one metres the drill bit into something that stopped it dead, and Hollis swore and braced himself and leaned on it, and it went on, slowly, screaming, and when the core came up it was black and hard and grained and dry, and so hot that Drover dropped it.

"Spur," Rook said.

He picked it up in his gloves and looked at it. The basalt of the spur itself. The rock the Arsenal stood on, the shelf of Depth 40 that ran down under the Flats and under the Cinder Wall and the cone and on down toward the Phlegethon, hard and dry and hot, that his grandfather had told him about when he was a child in Hold Pyrgos. You could cut galleries in it with picks, his grandfather had said. You could cut a gallery in that and it would stand for a thousand years without a prop.

"Lieutenant," Hollis said, quietly.

Rook looked up.

The *22nd*'s trooper was standing at the edge of the false wall, watching them, with an odd expression on her face.

"You're going down there?" she said. "Eighty metres?"

"Yes."

"I heard the relays don't reach that far."

Rook looked at her for a moment.

"They don't," he said.

**THE SIGNALS OFFICE, LEAF 54-47. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 274**

The Third's signallers tried to fix it. Rook gave them that.

They came down into the *66th*'s first gallery on the two hundred and seventy-fourth cycle, a Signals Optio named Bede Ancrum and two troopers with a relay set on a sledge, and they spent nine cycles running a shielded line down the incline behind the sappers and setting relays along it every forty metres. A relay in a gallery needed a line to the next relay and so on up to the surface, and it needed power, and the trench relays the Third had brought were built to stand on a parapet in the open air and failed in the heat of the deep basalt within hours. Ancrum's troopers wrapped them in wet sacking and fanned them and swore at them. The relays failed anyway.

On the two hundred and eighty-third cycle a counter-mine charge the Archflamen's miners had set somewhere above the gallery went off and brought down forty metres of roof behind the face, and cut the line, and the three relays beyond the fall went dead with it. There was a sapper squad at the face. They dug themselves out in eleven hours, all five of them alive. When they came up the incline into the lamplight at the shaft foot, black with dust, the fifth of them, an old sapper named Osric Teague, sat down on the floor and laughed until he wept.

"Eleven hours," he said. "Eleven hours with nothing on the line. If one of us had gone, Lieutenant. Eleven hours."

"I know," Rook said.

Ancrum gave up on the twenty-ninth cycle. He came to Rook's dugout at the head of the shaft and stood in the doorway, a thin anxious young man with a signaller's flash on his collar, and said that nobody on the Flats had the craft to build a relay that would work at eighty metres, and that he was sorry.

"Not your fault," Rook said.

"I've put in a request to the Fleet, Lieutenant. And to the Répertoire. And to the Forge. Somebody somewhere must know how to do it." Ancrum's face was miserable. "I'm sorry."

He went back up. Rook sat at the table in the dugout for a while after he had gone, looking at the gallery map on the wall, the long thin line of the *66th*'s first gallery going down the incline and under the Archflamen's pits on Leaf 54-47.

Then he got up and went down the shaft, to the face, and told the squad there what Ancrum had told him, and they nodded and went back to work. None of them had expected anything different. They were sappers of the Forge, and sappers who died deep didn't come back, and every sapper of the Forge had known it since the first day they walked into a sapper hall. They had all signed for it. They were paid three times what a trench soldier was paid, for it. They buried their own dead underground where they fell, and the Forge wrote their names on the roll of the sapper hall with the name of the gallery where they had fallen, and their pay for the rest of their engagement went to whoever they had named to receive it.

Rook had named his sister, in Hold Pyrgos. She had two children and a husband who drank.

The Répertoire kept a separate list for losses below ground. It had to. They didn't fit anywhere else in its books. A soldier who died in a gallery ninety metres down neither transferred nor went null in the ordinary way, but simply stopped, and the Répertoire had no column for that. It put them on a list of their own, and the sappers heard about it, and gave it a name within a week, and the name went up the line to Quarterdeck Nineteen and into the Répertoire's own reports, and nobody ever called it anything else.

They called it the Deep List.

**THE OLD LADDERS' BAYS, LEAF 53-41. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 300**

The trench soldiers found out what the Deep List meant within twenty cycles, when the Sapper-Prefects began asking the Line Phalanxes for soldiers to carry spoil and fight off counter-mines in the galleries, and the Line Phalanxes began sending them.

The provosts' figures for the next ten cycles were the worst they had ever recorded. The Third's soldiers had walked across no-man's land into the guns for nine rotations without complaint, or without much, and transferred and come back through the vats and walked again. The provosts had recorded refusals to go over the parapet, now and then, in every rotation of the siege, a handful in each regiment. They had never recorded anything to match the refusals to go below ground, where whole sections and whole maniples refused together. In the *37th*, a cohort called up to carry spoil for the *58th* Sapper Company sat down in its support trench and refused to move, and its centurion sat down with it.

Kastellan read the figures on the three hundredth cycle and sent an order down the line that afternoon. No trench soldier would be sent below except as a volunteer.

He expected, he told Dray, that he'd get almost none. He was wrong.

The volunteers came out of the Old Ladders.

Trooper Abelard Crisp of the *37th* Trench Regiment had been up sixty-one times. He was one of the oldest soldiers on the Flats, by transfers, and the Répertoire had flagged him in Rotation Seven as a candidate for the Forlorn Cohorts on account of his reaction times, and his centurion had argued him off the list. He came to the *58th* Sapper Company's shaft head on Leaf 53-41 on the three hundred and second cycle with his rifle and his pack and a slate from his centurion, and handed the slate to the sapper optio at the shaft head, and stood waiting.

The sapper optio read it and looked at him.

"Sixty-one," she said.

"Sixty-one," Crisp said.

"Do you understand what happens down there? If you're killed?"

"I understand it," Crisp said. He was a short, grey, slow-moving man of indeterminate age, with pale washed-out eyes. "Optio. Every time I come back through the vats there's a little less of me. I know there is. I can feel it. I forget things. I'm slower on the trigger. Some mornings I wake up in the dugout and I can't remember which leaf we're on, and some mornings I can't remember my wife's face, and it takes me an hour to get it back." He looked at her steadily. "Down there, if I go, I go whole. I'd rather that. I'd rather go whole once than keep coming back in pieces."

The sapper optio looked at him a long time.

"All right," she said. "Get your boots off. We work barefoot down there."

Crisp sat down on the fire-step and began unlacing his boots.

There were thousands of them in the end. The Old Ladders came forward from every regiment on the Flats, from the bays where they sat on the fire-steps with their slowed hands and their long blank pauses, soldiers who had been up thirty times and forty and fifty, and they went down the shafts in their bare feet with the sappers and carried spoil and stood guard at the gallery junctions and fought off the counter-mines, and went onto the Deep List with the sappers. Kastellan read their names with the rest. He'd told Voss he would.

He found, after the first rotation of it, that there was one thing he couldn't give them.

The sappers' losses went onto the roll of the Forge's sapper halls. Their pay went to whoever they'd named. The Third had nothing of the kind for its trench volunteers. When an Old Ladder died ninety metres down, the Répertoire entered him as a null like any other, and his family received the same notice that went to the families of soldiers lost to the Echo Mast. *Null. Pattern not received. Pay ceases from this cycle.*

Kastellan petitioned the Directorate to extend the Forge's terms to the Third's volunteers on the three hundred and fortieth cycle of Rotation Ten.

The petition lay before the Directorate for a rotation. The Directorate agreed in Rotation Twelve, and by then a good many of the families concerned had stopped expecting anything.

**THE ARSENAL. THE NINTH CASTING HALL. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 290**

Prefect Ostorian of the Arsenal's garrison had learned, in the twenty rotations of his command, to recognise the moment when he was being robbed, and he recognised it on the two hundred and ninetieth cycle, standing in the Ninth Casting Hall with a slate of the garrison's thrall returns in his hand and a Frost Mason in front of him.

He had never been this close to one before.

It was tall, taller than he was, and it stood utterly still in the red light of the casting lines with the heat of the open moulds on its back, and the heat didn't touch it. It wore armour of grey ice. He'd heard the thralls say so and hadn't believed them, and it was true: plates of grey ice, cloudy and thick, fitted to its body as closely as skin, and in the heat of the Ninth Casting Hall at Depth 40, where a man's sweat dried on his face before it could run, the ice didn't melt. There was a faint cold breath off it. He could feel it on his hands. Its face, what he could see of it inside the ice of its helm, was grey and smooth and calm, with eyes that were two dark holes in frozen water.

It had come for his miners.

"The garrison's thrall cohorts," Ostorian said, carefully, "are the garrison's. I command them. I need them on the walls. The Third is at the Cinder Wall, Mason. Eleven hundred metres from it. My cohorts are all that stand between it and the cone."

The Frost Mason looked at him for some time before it spoke, and when it did, its voice was low and clear and slightly hollow, the voice of something speaking from the bottom of a well, and he felt the cold of it in the roots of his teeth.

"The Third is under the Cinder Wall, Prefect," it said. "Under your walls. Under your feet. They began digging thirty cycles ago, beneath the old belt. We have heard them at it since. Nine regiments. Forty engines. And the listeners of the Forge." It paused. "We have met the listeners of the Forge before. At Charybdis. And at other places."

Ostorian said nothing.

"The Master of the Ice has asked the Archflamen for every thrall in the Arsenal who knows how to cut and brace and listen," the Frost Mason said. "The Archflamen has granted it. Your thrall miners are ours from this cycle. And the heat-well crews from the casting lines."

"The Steward will never agree to give up the heat-well crews."

"The Steward has not been asked."

The Frost Mason turned and walked away down the length of the Ninth Casting Hall between the open moulds, its ice armour throwing back the red light of the molten slag in long pale gleams, and the thralls at the casting lines stopped work as it passed and stood with their heads bowed, and didn't look up until it had gone.

Ostorian stood in the hall for a long time with the slate in his hand.

He knew what it meant. He had known since the drowned came up the river and the Legates of the deep rivers came with them, and the Cohort of Nemesis took the docks, and the Kindlers came up the smugglers' stair, that the defence of the Arsenal was passing out of his hands and into the hands of the deep, one piece at a time. The Frost Masons recognised no authority but their own Master of the Ice. Once the war under the ground was theirs, the ground beneath the Arsenal would become a country neither he nor Dace could see into or command, and the Archflamen would approve every request they made, as the Archflamen had approved every request made by every one of the deep's commanders since the Mast first spoke.

The Third's intelligence officers, reading the Arsenal's traffic on Quarterdeck Nineteen where they could, noted afterward that from Rotation Eleven onward the name of Prefect Ostorian appeared in it less and less, and the Frost Masons' more and more. They concluded that the defence of the Arsenal was passing out of the hands of the soldiers who had begun it.

They were right. Ostorian could have told them so.

**THE GALLERY BENEATH LEAF 54-47. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 330**

The *66th* went down the shaft on Leaf 54-47 on the three hundred and thirtieth cycle, and Rook went first.

The shaft dropped straight down from the back of the fifth fire bay behind the false wall, through the cinder and the pumice and the old lava, eighty-four metres on iron ladders bolted into the rock, and at the bottom of it the gallery ran away west under Leaf 54-47 toward the Archflamen's pits. Two sappers wide. A metre and a half high in the first stretch, so that a sapper walked bent double and a tall one like Rook walked with his head almost between his knees. The walls were black basalt, hard and dry and hot, cut with picks and smoothed with the flat of a blade, and they stood without a prop as his grandfather had promised they would.

It was silent.

That was the thing about the deep that nobody who hadn't been down could understand, and that Rook would never be able to make his sister understand in all the letters he wrote her. On the surface the Flats were never silent. The guns, the shells, the engines, the whistles, the seep-storms, the voices, the endless dull rumble of a hundred and nine kilometres of front; nine rotations of it, without a pause. Eighty metres down, there was nothing. The rock swallowed all of it. A sapper in a deep gallery heard his own breathing and his own heart and the faint tick of the rock as it cooled and shifted, and nothing else, and after a few hours of it the silence came to have a weight of its own, a thick soft pressure that seemed to lie against the eardrums.

And in the silence, sound carried.

It carried through rock for hundreds of metres. A pick striking basalt carried. A boot on a gallery floor carried. A dropped tool, a cough, a whisper, the clink of a canteen against a belt buckle; any of it could be heard through the rock by an enemy listening on the far side, and an enemy who heard it would know where you were, and drive a counter-mine toward you, and bore a dart into the wall beside your gallery and fire it. So the sappers wrapped their picks in cloth. They worked barefoot. They tied their canteens down and padded their buckles, and they didn't speak at the face. They spoke by hand signs, the old Hephaestean sapper-signs that every sapper of the Forge learned in the sapper hall before they learned to hold a pick, and the Old Ladders who came down to carry spoil learned them too, in a week, or were sent back up.

They worked in squads of five. Two cut at the face, kneeling side by side, with their wrapped picks, taking the basalt down in flakes no bigger than a hand. One carried the spoil back along the gallery in sacks to the shaft foot, where it was winched up and used for sandbags in the trenches above. One braced, where the rock needed it, which in the deep basalt was rarely. And one listened.

The listener sat in a niche cut in the gallery wall ten metres back from the face, with a geophone augur pressed flat against the rock, and listened through every watch. Brin Hollis was the *66th*'s best. She was a small dark silent woman from the listening companies of Hold Pyrgos, who had been with Rook for six years and could tell the sound of an Arsenal borer from the sound of a Frost Mason's pick through two hundred metres of basalt, and the sound of a Frost Mason's pick from the sound of the rock cooling, which was harder. She sat in her niche with her eyes closed and the augur's cups over her ears and the long bronze probe of the geophone against the stone, and didn't move for hours at a time.

At the end of every watch she reported to the listening room.

The listening companies of the Forge had sunk their posts every hundred metres along the front, from the Mora Cut to the Lethe Cut, and every one of them was linked by cable to a listening room beneath the command bunker of its Line Phalanx. Rook had seen the *7th* Line Phalanx's, under the old Drum: a long low vault cut in the basalt with the gallery maps spread across a table the length of a Thureos, and listening officers bent over them by lamplight, plotting every sound the posts reported. A counter-mine. A borer. A pick. A voice. Every sound that couldn't be accounted for by the *7th*'s own galleries went onto the map as a pin, and every pin was a counter-mine ordered against it. By the middle of Rotation Eleven, Rook was told, the listening net beneath the Flats was the densest the Forge had ever laid.

The Frost Masons answered it. Rook learned how on the three hundred and fortieth cycle, when the Third's guns opened a barrage on the Archflamen's pits on Leaf 54-47 for a raid on the surface, and the whole of the deep went suddenly, horribly loud. He felt it through the rock. A dull continuous thunder from above, eighty metres up, every shell of the barrage coming down through the basalt as a long deep shudder, until the gallery walls hummed with it and the dust sifted down from the roof. Hollis came out of her niche at the end of the barrage with her face grey.

She signed it to him. *Couldn't hear. Nothing. Two hours.* And then, after a moment: *They were digging. I know they were. Under the guns.*

That was how the Frost Masons dug. During the barrage, when the noise of the guns drowned everything else below ground. The Third's listeners came to dread the hour of the heaviest bombardment more than any other, and after a while, so did everyone else in the galleries.

**BENEATH LEAF 54-47. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 402**

On the four hundred and second cycle of Rotation Ten, Hollis heard them.

She came out of her niche at the third hour of the dark watch, without a sound, and touched Rook's arm, and he looked at her, and she signed it to him. *Counter-mine. Above us. Close.* She put up four fingers, then two. *Six metres. Maybe less. Digging. Stopped.* And then: *Waiting.*

Rook stood motionless in the gallery and listened. He could hear nothing at all. He hadn't expected to. Hollis heard things nobody else could.

He signed to the squad at the face to stop.

They stopped. They knelt at the face with their wrapped picks in their hands and didn't move. Behind them in the gallery the spoil-carrier set down his sack without a sound, and the bracer stood still with his prop. Rook signed back along the gallery to the shaft foot, and the signal went up the ladders to the surface, and all through the *66th*'s galleries beneath Leaf 54-47, the work stopped.

They waited. Hollis went back into her niche and pressed the geophone to the rock above the gallery roof and closed her eyes.

An hour. Two hours. Rook knelt in the gallery in the dark with his lamp hooded, listening to his own heart, and thought about his sister, and about Hold Pyrgos, and about the roll of the sapper hall there, where his father's name was, and his grandfather's, each with the name of the gallery where he had fallen.

At the end of the fourth hour Hollis came out of the niche and signed: *Nothing. No digging. Nobody in it now.* She hesitated, and signed: *Or they're very quiet.*

Rook thought about it for a long time.

Then he signed for a section.

---

He chose eight of them, and himself. Hollis, who would listen. Drover, Teague, a big quiet sapper named Mattias Cole who'd been with the *66th* for twenty years, and four others. And Abelard Crisp, the Old Ladder from the *37th*, who had been carrying spoil for the *66th* for forty cycles since the *58th* lent him, and who came forward when Rook signed for volunteers before anyone else did.

They cut up through the roof of their own gallery toward the enemy counter-mine above it, slowly, in silence, with the wrapped picks, taking the basalt down a flake at a time, while Hollis lay on her back in the gallery under them with the geophone pressed to the roof and listened for any sound above. It took nine hours. At the end of the ninth hour Cole's pick went through into empty air, and a little dust fell down into the hole, and nothing else.

Rook went up first.

He came up through the hole into the enemy gallery in the dark with his lamp hooded and his pistol in his hand and his shotgun on its sling across his back, and knelt in it, and listened. Nothing. The gallery ran away from him in both directions, east and west, black and silent. It was bigger than the *66th*'s, three metres wide and two high, cut clean and square with an Arsenal borer, and the walls were faced with something smooth and grey that gave off a faint cold breath against his skin. He touched it.

Ice.

He signed the section up after him. They came up one after another through the hole, barefoot and silent, and he divided them into two halves of four in the dark: Drover, Teague and two others to go east with him, Cole and Crisp and the other two to go west. Hollis and a sapper named Ivor Lund stayed at the hole with a dart and its firing cable, to drop the gallery behind them when the section came back.

They went.

He led the eastern half for sixty metres down the enemy gallery in the dark, one step at a time, his bare feet on the cold smooth ice of the floor, and saw nothing and heard nothing, and at sixty metres, where the gallery turned, he stopped and signed his four to the wall and waited.

He heard them coming before he saw them.

Unwrapped picks, and the clink of a canteen. Voices, low, in the thrall-speech of the Arsenal, which the Third's soldiers called the grunt, and the light of a lamp coming round the corner of the gallery from the east, swinging. Six of them. Thrall miners of the Arsenal, in their leather aprons and their grey thrall-cloth, with picks on their shoulders and a lamp held up by the one in front, walking west along the gallery toward the place where he'd come up. Toward the hole. Toward Hollis.

He pressed himself flat against the ice of the wall in the dark with his four beside him and let them go past.

They went past him at a metre. He could have touched the last of them. He smelled them, slag and sweat and the strange bitter herb the thralls chewed on the casting lines, and he heard their breathing, and he saw the lamp swing past and go on west down the gallery. They hadn't seen him. They were walking straight between the two halves of his section.

He signed to Drover and Teague and the others. *Follow. Close. Wait for Cole.*

They followed. Barefoot, in the dark, on the ice, ten metres behind the swinging lamp. Ahead of the lamp, somewhere in the dark of the gallery to the west, was Cole's half of the section, coming back toward them as Rook had ordered. He hoped. He hoped Cole had heard the miners coming, as he had, and gone to ground against the wall, as he had. He hoped Cole had waited.

The miners stopped. The one with the lamp held it up and stared ahead down the gallery, and said something sharp in the grunt.

Cole's half of the section opened fire on them from ahead.

Rook opened fire on them from behind. The six thrall miners of the Arsenal were caught in the dark between the two halves of the *66th*'s section in a gallery three metres wide, with shotguns firing into them from both ends, and it was over in about four seconds. Rook's shotgun was louder in the enclosed gallery than anything he had ever heard in his life. The noise of it came back off the ice walls in a long hammering roar, and the muzzle flash lit the gallery white, and when it stopped there was a ringing silence and a smell of powder, and the lamp lying on the floor of the gallery on its side with its flame guttering, and the six miners lying round it.

Nobody spoke. He could hear nothing at all. His ears were full of a long high whine.

He signed. *Back. Now. To the hole.*

They went back. Both halves together, at a run now, in the dark, west, toward the hole where Hollis and Lund were waiting with the dart, and Rook knew as he ran that every Frost Mason within a kilometre of the gallery had heard the shooting through the rock, and every thrall miner, and was coming.

They came out of a side gallery forty metres short of the hole.

He saw their lamps first. A dozen lamps, swinging, coming fast out of a side gallery on the left, between the section and the hole, and then the miners under them, thralls in their aprons with rifles and picks, and behind them, tall and pale and gleaming in the lamplight, a Frost Mason in its grey ice.

They had reached the hole before the section did. Rook saw it. He saw the lamps reach the place where the hole was, and he saw Hollis and Lund kneeling at the hole with the dart between them, frantically setting the charge in its bore, and he saw them look up. He saw Lund raise his pistol.

He saw the Frost Mason reach them.

He never knew exactly what it did. It was too fast, and too far, and the lamps were swinging. He saw it put out one long grey hand toward Hollis, and he saw her fall back from it across the mouth of the hole with ice spreading across her face and her chest in long white feathered lines, and he saw Lund fire into it twice at a range of a metre and the rounds go into the ice of its armour and stop there, and then the thralls were on Lund with their picks.

Hollis and Lund went onto the Deep List ninety metres down.

Rook didn't stop. There was no time to stop. He fired the shotgun into the miners at the hole until it was empty, and dropped it, and drew his pistol and fired that, and the section came up beside him firing, and the thralls at the hole went down and the Frost Mason turned toward them. He felt the cold come off it. He felt the air in the gallery turn to ice in his lungs, and his hands go numb on the pistol, and something hit him hard in the left side, and he looked down and saw a thrall's pick-head stuck in him below the ribs.

"Down," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange to him in the ringing silence. "Down the hole. All of you. Now."

They went down the hole. Cole first, then Drover, then the others, sliding down through the rough-cut shaft into the *66th*'s own gallery below, and Rook stood at the top of it with his pistol, with the pick-head in his side, and covered them. Crisp was the last.

Crisp stopped at the edge of the hole and looked at him.

"Lieutenant," he said. "Go on. I've got this."

"Get down the hole, Crisp."

"Lieutenant." Crisp's pale eyes were quite calm. "Sixty-one. I've had my go. Go on down."

Rook looked at him. Then he went down the hole.

He came down into his own gallery hard, onto his wounded side, and the pain went through him white, and Cole caught him and dragged him away from the foot of the hole. He looked up. He saw Crisp at the top of it, lit by the swinging lamps of the enemy gallery, standing with his rifle firing up the enemy gallery toward the Frost Mason, steady, unhurried, the slow careful shots of a soldier who had been up sixty-one times. He saw the cold come over Crisp. He saw Crisp fall forward into the mouth of the hole and lie across it, and he saw the miners come up behind him.

"Dart," Rook said to Cole.

The *66th* had its own dart in its own gallery, bored into the wall thirty metres back from the foot of the hole and walled in behind sandbags with its cable running back to the shaft foot, set there on the first cycle of the gallery against exactly this. Cole dragged Rook back along the gallery thirty metres, forty, fifty, and the others ran ahead of them toward the shaft. Behind them, Rook heard the miners come down the hole.

He heard the first of them drop into the gallery, and then the second.

"Fire it," he said.

Cole fired it.

The dart went off with a sound Rook felt in the marrow of his bones rather than heard, a single vast dull thud through the rock, and the roof of the *66th*'s gallery came down for forty metres between the shaft foot and the hole, in a long grinding roar of falling basalt, and buried everything in it. The miners. The hole. Abelard Crisp, sixty-one transfers, lying across the mouth of it.

Then the dust came down the gallery after them in a long rolling cloud and filled the lamps, and Rook couldn't see anything at all.

---

The *66th* Sapper Company sent two names to the Deep List that night. Brin Hollis. Ivor Lund. It sent four wounded to the surface, and Rook was among them, with a thrall's pick-head in his side that the medicus at the *22nd*'s aid station dug out of him without anaesthetic, since the aid station had run out.

Crisp's name went onto the *37th*'s returns, as a null. The Répertoire had no column for a trench volunteer lost below ground.

With the enemy gallery located, the *66th* fired further darts over the next three cycles and destroyed the rest of it, a kilometre and a half of gallery under Leaf 54-47, and the Archflamen's miners didn't dig there again for a rotation.

Rook lay in the aid station for eleven cycles with his side bound up. He wrote to his sister. He told her about the Flats, and the ugliness of them, and the silence of the deep, and the Behemoths walking. He told her about Hollis, a little, and not about how she had died. He didn't tell her about Crisp at all, at first. On the ninth cycle he tore the letter up and wrote it again, and that time he told her about Crisp.

*He was from the Third,* he wrote. *A trench soldier. He'd been up sixty-one times. He said every time he came back there was less of him. He said he'd rather go whole once. He went whole, Ama, I saw him. Will you do something for me? Will you go to the sapper hall and ask Mother to put his name on the roll? I know he wasn't Forge. I know it's not the custom. Ask her anyway. Abelard Crisp, of the 37th. The gallery is the 66th's first, beneath Leaf 54-47, on the Cinder Flats. Tell her I asked.*

His mother put it on the roll. She wrote to tell him so. It was the first name on the roll of the sapper hall of Hold Pyrgos in nine hundred years that had not been a sapper of the Forge.

**THE GALLERIES. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLES 1 TO 50**

The Archflamen's miners answered raids of that kind with traps, and stronger guards in their galleries, and after the first rotation of it the Frost Masons began to do something worse.

They began to fill their abandoned galleries with ice.

The *66th* found the first of them on the twelfth cycle of Rotation Eleven, beneath Leaf 54-49: an enemy gallery that the listeners had heard being dug in Rotation Ten, and then heard nothing from for twenty cycles. The sappers cut up into it from below and came up into a solid block of grey ice that filled it from wall to wall and floor to roof, and was cold enough to burn the skin through their gloves. They tried to cut through it. It closed behind the pick. They tried to blast it, and the dart's charge cracked it, and the crack healed itself in an hour as they watched. In the end they went round it.

Then the Frost Masons began to leave galleries open.

They left them open and empty, and silent, for the Third's sappers to find. And when a sapper section cut up into one and found it empty and silent, and went along it in the dark in their bare feet, searching, they found, sooner or later, that there were darts already bored into its walls, walled in behind ice, with their firing cables running away through the rock to somewhere the Third's listeners had never heard.

The *58th* Sapper Company lost a whole section that way on the thirtieth cycle of Rotation Eleven. Nine names on the Deep List. The listeners heard nothing at all before the dart went off.

After that, nobody went into an empty gallery without listening to it for a day first. The Frost Masons learned that too, and began to put thrall miners into the empty galleries to dig, now and then, so that they wouldn't sound empty.

The war beneath went on.

---

**THE LISTENING ROOM OF THE *7th* LINE PHALANX. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 58**

Sapper-Captain Renée Marchand of the *20th* Sapper Company had been looking at Vault 9 for a rotation and a half, and on the fifty-eighth cycle of Rotation Eleven she stood in the listening room under the old Drum with her hands on the edge of the long map table and looked at it again.

It was one of the Arsenal's outer vaults. Cut into the basalt under the old inner belt beneath Leaves 55 and 56, two kilometres outside the Cinder Wall, a long low warren of storerooms and passages and armouries ninety metres down, connected to the surface by four great shafts and to the Arsenal behind it by a gallery the old plans called the Throat. The Archflamen still used it as a sheltered depot, for shells and armour and stores, the Third's intelligence officers believed. A rotation of Phaethon fire had done nothing to it. The Phaethons could crater the ground above it as much as they liked, and they had; everything that mattered lay too deep in the rock.

The Third's archive on Quarterdeck Nineteen had the plans of it. That was what had made the whole thing possible.

Marchand had never seen anything like those plans in thirty years of sapping. The Third had dug the inner belt itself, six centuries ago, when the Arsenal was the Third's anchor against the Phlegethon, and the Third's engineers had mapped every metre of ground they dug, every gallery and shelter and shaft, and surveyed the rock beneath it to a depth of a hundred and twenty metres, and filed it all in the archive in eleven cases, where nobody had looked at it for five hundred years. The Arsenal's own plans of its outer vaults were at Watchpoint Hades, among the Répertoire's records of the forge-hold, and the Répertoire had sent copies down on the cables when Kastellan asked. The plans were old. The Archflamen's thralls had altered much of what they showed. But between them, they gave the Hephaestean sappers a fuller map of the rock beneath an enemy's works than any sapper of the Forge had ever had in any siege.

Vault 9 was on them. Every storeroom of it. Every passage.

She had gone after it at the start of Rotation Eleven with the idea of tunnelling to it in silence, breaking in by surprise and overrunning it from below, before the Archflamen knew anyone was coming. The Frost Masons had heard her coming at the twentieth cycle. They had counter-mined the ground round the vault so heavily since that a second war had grown up in the dark round Vault 9, beneath Leaves 55 and 56, a maze of shafts and galleries and counter-galleries that the listening room's map showed as a dense black tangle of pencil lines and pins. With surprise gone, Kastellan had committed the Forge's boring engines to speed the work. The *7th* and *8th* Line Phalanxes had put their sapper companies into the ground there without pause. The fighting below had been as heavy as anything on the surface, and the sapper companies had lost more soldiers in it than the trench regiments above them, every one of whom had gone onto the Deep List.

They'd won it. By weight of numbers, slowly, cycle by cycle, the Hephaestean sappers had gained the upper hand and driven the Frost Masons back from the vault, and on the fifty-eighth cycle Marchand stood at the map table and told the *7th* Line Phalanx's listening officers that she could break in.

"Two galleries," she said, and put her finger on the map. "Here and here. Under the north storerooms. We've cut them right up under the floor. Two breaching charges, three hundred tonnes each, tamped in behind sandbag walls to drive the blast upward. That brings down two storerooms and gives us two breaches into the vault." She moved her finger. "Five companies go in through the breaches and hold them. The *20th* first. Behind us, the breacher cohorts of the *22nd* and the *49th* in plate, with meltas and demolition charges, to exploit it. We clear the vault room by room from the north storerooms to the Throat and blow the Throat behind us, and the vault's ours."

The senior listening officer of the *7th*, a heavy, bald old sapper named Gethin Oakes, looked at the map for a long time.

"And the surface?"

"The *49th* and *50th* attack on either side of the vault, hard enough to draw in the Archflamen's cohorts. The *4th* Ordnance Regiment fires a box barrage over the vault to seal it off from above. Nobody gets in or out through the shafts while we're inside."

"The barrage will deafen the listeners."

"I know."

"The Frost Masons dig under the barrage."

"I know that too." Marchand looked at him. "Gethin, we've been at this a rotation and a half. They've lost the ground round the vault. They're pushed back. I've got two galleries right up under the floor and I don't know how much longer I can hold them. If I don't go in now, they'll find a way to come back."

Oakes was silent a moment.

"What's in the vault?" he asked.

"Shells. Armour. Stores. Garrison, the intelligence officers think a few thousand thralls and Sworn."

"Do they know?"

"They think so."

Oakes rubbed his bald head slowly.

"All right," he said. "I'll send it up to the Marshal. Sixty-fourth cycle?"

"Sixty-fourth."

---

The Archflamen knew the attack was coming. The Third's intelligence officers learned afterward how much he knew, and how long he had known it, and what he'd done about it. None of it was in the plans.

He'd emptied the vault. Most of its stores and its armour had been withdrawn deeper into the spur through the Throat in the cycles after the Frost Masons lost the ground round it, quietly, by night, and what the *20th* Sapper Company would find when it came up through the floor of the north storerooms was a vault nearly empty of anything worth taking.

Soldiers it still had in plenty.

Inside, the defenders had blocked every main passage of Vault 9 with barricades of slag drums and shell crates and sandbags and blocks of cut basalt, every barricade covered by heavy guns, so that every passage would have to be stormed and paid for. And into the vault, on the fiftieth cycle of Rotation Eleven, the Archflamen had sent reinforcements.

The Third's intelligence officers learned their name from a captured thrall, much later. They were a warband of the Cinderborn. They called themselves the Ninefold Brand, and they had come up the river with the drowned in Rotation Eight, and they had sought nothing more since than close fighting from room to room. The Archflamen gave it to them.

---

**VAULT 9. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 64**

Marchand fired the charges herself, at the first bell of the sixty-fourth cycle, from the firing station at the shaft foot four hundred metres back along the first gallery, and the blast was felt in the trenches of the *22nd* ninety metres overhead.

She felt it as a single enormous blow through the soles of her bare feet and up through her whole body, and the gallery round her jumped, and the lamps went out, and dust came down from the roof in a solid curtain. Then a wind came down the gallery from the face, hot and thick with smoke and powdered basalt, and went past her and on toward the shaft, and she was on her feet and running into it before it had finished.

The first squads were already ahead of her. She caught them up at the face, where the gallery ended in a ragged hole in the roof with rubble pouring down through it, and went up through the hole behind them into Vault 9.

It was dark and full of smoke. The north storerooms had come down. She came up out of the hole onto a slope of rubble metres high, broken basalt and shattered shell crates and twisted steel shelving and things she didn't look at, with the lamps of her sappers moving across it ahead of her through the smoke, and the roar of the box barrage coming down on the ground above, a dull continuous thunder through the vault roof that made the dust dance in the lamplight. Somewhere ahead in the smoke there were voices, shouting, in the grunt.

Nobody fired at them.

For some minutes, nobody fired at them at all. The *20th*'s squads came up through both breaches and spread out across the rubble and into the passages beyond the north storerooms, moving fast, with their shotguns and their grenades and their lamps, and met nothing. Marchand went forward with the first squads down the main passage south from the storerooms toward the heart of the vault, and the passage was empty and silent but for the barrage overhead, and the smoke thinned as they went, and she began to think, for a minute, perhaps two, that it was going to be all right.

Then the defenders beyond the rubble, who had held their fire until the shock had passed, opened up down every passage at once.

The first barricade was sixty metres down the main passage from the storerooms, slag drums and shell crates stacked to the roof with firing slits cut through them, and the heavy gun behind it fired down the length of the passage into the *20th*'s first squads at a range of forty metres. Marchand saw the whole of the leading squad go down at once. She was behind them, against the passage wall, and she saw the muzzle flash of the heavy gun light up the passage from end to end, a long flickering white, and the squad in front of her come apart in it.

"Down," she shouted. "Get down, get against the walls, get down."

They got down. The gun went on firing over them, down the length of the passage, the rounds smashing into the rubble slope behind them and the walls, and she lay against the foot of the wall with her face on the floor and the stone chips stinging her neck and heard other guns opening up from other barricades, all through the vault, down every passage the *20th* had gone into. And the box barrage went on overhead, shaking the ceilings, so that the whole vault was full of noise and smoke and dust and muzzle flashes and screaming, and nobody could hear any order she gave.

Sappers caught in the open were cut down where they stood. That was how the *20th*'s war diary put it afterward, and it was true.

They went at the barricades. They had to. Grenades first, crawling forward along the foot of the passage walls under the fire until they were close enough to throw, and the grenades went over the barricades and burst behind them, and the heavy guns stopped, for a moment, and the sappers went forward in a rush. Some of the rushes reached the barricades. The ones that did fought over them with shotguns and pistols and knives, in the smoke, against thralls in grey and Sworn in black, and some of them took the barricades, and then the next barricade, forty metres further on, opened fire on them down the passage, and it began again.

Every barricade cost them dead and wounded. Every gunner they killed behind one was replaced by another out of the dark of the passages beyond.

Marchand called the flame sections forward at the second hour. They came up through the breaches with their heavy flamers on their backs and went forward along the passages under the fire, and filled them with fire and fumes, long roaring jets of burning promethium that lit the vault orange and filled it with black smoke, and the barricades burned, and the thralls behind them burned or ran. It helped. For an hour it helped. And then the first attack stalled, as the vault filled with smoke so thick nobody could see three metres, and the flame sections ran out of fuel, and the barricades beyond the burning ones went on firing, and the *20th* Sapper Company held a foothold in the north end of Vault 9 and nothing more.

---

She came forward herself at the fourth hour.

Her optio, a short tough grey-bearded sapper named Hamund Garrow who had been with her for nineteen years, told her not to. She went anyway. She went forward along the main passage under the fire to the furthest barricade the *20th* held, a heap of burned slag drums seventy metres in from the north storerooms, and crouched behind it with the sappers there and looked at the passage beyond through the smoke, and at the next barricade, forty metres on, and the heavy gun behind it.

"We're not getting through that," she said to Garrow.

"No, Captain."

"Not down the passages. Not one barricade at a time. They've got every passage covered and they've got more gunners than we've got sappers." She looked at the passage wall beside her. Cut basalt, smooth and old, a metre thick perhaps, with another passage on the far side of it, according to the plans. "We go through the walls."

Garrow looked at the wall.

"Meltas," he said.

"Melta-charges. We blow our own passages through the inner walls. We come into the rooms from directions they're not covering. We come at the barricades from the side and behind." She looked round at the sappers crouching behind the barricade with her. "And gas. I'm authorising gas grenades. Drive the thralls off the barricades with it."

"The Sworn have masks, Captain."

"The thralls don't."

It worked. For a time, it worked. The sappers blew their own passages through the inner walls of Vault 9 with melta-charges, one after another, a metre-wide hole burned through a metre of basalt in a long white hiss and a stink of molten rock, and came through the holes into rooms the defenders weren't covering, and came at the barricades from the side and from behind. The gas grenades went over the barricades and the thralls behind them came up out of their firing slits coughing and blinded, and the sappers shot them. The *20th* took six barricades in three hours. The heavy guns fell silent one by one. Movement came back into the fight. Marchand went from hole to hole behind her sappers with Garrow beside her and the plans of the vault in her head, room by room, choosing every wall, and began to believe again that they could reach the Throat.

At the seventh hour, a melta-charge opened a welded blast door at the foot of a stair.

She was ten metres from it. The door had been on the plans, an old Arsenal blast door between the south storerooms and a stair that went down to a lower level of the vault, and the defenders had welded it shut, and her sappers had put a melta-charge on it to burn through. The charge went off in a long white flare. The door glowed red, and then white, and then a ragged hole two metres across burned out of the middle of it, and its edges ran molten and dripped onto the floor.

Behind the door, on the stair, the Ninefold Brand were waiting.

She heard them before she saw them. She heard them singing.

It was a hymn of the Red Furnace. She'd heard the Cinderborn sing it once before, far off, across no-man's land on Gallows Rise, in Rotation Eight, and she'd never forgotten it. A long, low, rising chant, in a language nobody in the Third knew, in many deep voices together, that seemed to come up out of the rock itself, and the river fire in their bodies lit the stair behind the burned door red and orange as they came up it. They came through the hole in the door while its edges were still glowing white. They came through it into the south storeroom in a long running rush, tall and thin and black and grey, with slag-rough skin and the fire running in it in long glowing seams, and their blades in their hands glowing red, singing.

Marchand was among the first killed.

She had her pistol up. Garrow said afterward that she'd got off three rounds, and that one of them had hit. The Cinderborn she'd hit didn't stop. It came across the ten metres of the storeroom floor in two strides, singing, and she was still firing when it reached her, and Garrow saw its blade go through her, and saw her fall, and saw it step over her and go on, toward the sappers behind her, without breaking the hymn.

---

In the passages beyond, the Cinderborn killed sappers by the hundred.

They were taller than any soldier of the Third and faster and stronger, and the gas had no effect on them at all; they walked through it singing. They moved through the vault at will, through the sappers' own melta-holes, through the passages the *20th* had taken, and the sappers fell back before them and sealed their breaches against them with sandbags and charges and fought to hold what they'd taken. Garrow took command of what was left of the *20th* in the north storerooms and held them through the night against three attacks of the Ninefold Brand, with shotguns and grenades at point-blank range.

On the second cycle, Kastellan committed the breachers.

They came up through the two breaches from the galleries below at the first bell, the breacher cohorts of the *22nd* and the *49th* in their plate, two hundred kilograms of steel to a soldier, with meltas and heavy flamers and demolition charges, in the hope that their plate would succeed where the sappers had failed. They went forward into the vault behind Garrow's survivors.

They suffered as the sappers had, in the same fire-swept passages. In places they cleared a barricade, and turned its guns on its builders, and held it, and then the Cinderborn came at them through their fire, singing, and rifle rounds and grenades did little to the Cinderborn, and the breachers' heavy flamers did nothing at all. Garrow saw a Cinderborn walk through a stream of keyed fire from a breacher's heavy flamer, through the whole roaring length of it, the fire sheeting harmlessly off its body, to kill the breacher who fired it.

The Third's soldiers had no answer to an enemy that fire didn't harm.

---

On the third cycle the Frost Masons brought down one of the two approach galleries.

They'd prepared it in secret, in the weeks of the war round the vault, under the barrage: a counter-mine dug up to within a few metres of the *20th*'s first gallery, with a dart bored into the rock above it and walled in behind ice, and they fired it at the second hour of the third cycle with a sapper squad on its way to the vault with ammunition. The gallery came down for sixty metres. The squad went onto the Deep List under it.

The whole of the battle now hung on a single gallery.

Garrow pulled squads out of the fighting in the vault to dig counter-mines round it and man more listening posts. Under the box barrage the listeners could hear almost nothing. They knelt in their niches with their geophones against the rock and their faces strained, and heard the barrage, and nothing else, and every one of them knew the Frost Masons were digging out there somewhere under the noise of it, toward the last gallery, and couldn't hear where.

By the end of the fourth cycle, the five companies of the first wave, each six hundred strong at the start, were mostly below half their strength. The *20th* Sapper Company numbered one hundred and eighty-seven. All of its dead were on the Deep List. A party Garrow sent back to clear the first fall was buried in its turn by a second dart.

At the sixth hour of the fourth cycle, Kastellan called the attack off.

The sapper squads came back down through the breaches into the last gallery, and back along it to the shaft, and up the ladders to the surface, after four cycles in a battle without quarter, carrying their wounded. They'd failed to take Vault 9.

Garrow was the last of the *20th* up the shaft. He came up the ladder out of the dark into the grey light of the *22nd*'s trench on Leaf 55-38, black with dust and smoke from head to foot, barefoot, with Marchand's lamp hooked to his belt, and sat down on the fire-step, and didn't speak for an hour.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 70**

The first battle for Vault 9 had cost the five assault companies more than two thousand names on the Deep List, and the breacher cohorts committed after them some nine thousand transfers, for an objective that would have yielded little if it had been taken. Kastellan learned that last part on the sixty-ninth cycle, from a thrall taken in the vault. The stores were gone. They'd been gone for weeks.

He wrote his report to Watchpoint Hades that night, and it was candid. The Lord Marshal's staff remarked on it afterward. Most commanders who lost a battle of that kind wrote reports that took six leaves to say so. Kastellan's took one.

*The sappers were beaten in Vault 9 by the Cinderborn,* he wrote, *against whom the Third has no proven answer above ground or below it. Fire does not harm them. Gas does not harm them. Plate and heavy flamers did not stop them. I ask the Lord Marshal for help in finding an answer. The Cinderborn are the Archflamen's best soldiers, and wherever they are found in strength the Third will have to meet them with something other than its trench regiments.*

The Lord Marshal had no help to send. None was available, at the time.

Kastellan sat at his desk that night after Dray had taken the report away, and read the names. Voss had sent them up from the sapper halls, as he'd asked her to: two thousand and forty-one of them, on eleven slates, in the order the Forge's sapper rolls had them, each with the name of the gallery where it had fallen. *Renée Marchand, Sapper-Captain, 20th Company, Fourth Regiment. Vault 9, the south storeroom.*

He read them all. It took him until the first bell.

---

**VAULT 9. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLES 210 AND 360**

There were three battles for Vault 9 in all.

Ansel Rook fought in the second, on the two hundred and tenth cycle of Rotation Eleven, with the *66th*. He'd come back to the company in the fortieth cycle with a long pink scar under his ribs and a stiffness in his left side that never quite went away, and he'd spent the cycles since driving one of three new approach galleries under Leaves 55 and 56, so that the loss of one gallery could never again decide the fight. The breaching charges were smaller, that time, and set under the vault's outer storerooms, where the defenders were fewest. And the assault companies went in with heavy flamers keyed by children of Hephaestus, sapper volunteers from the Forge's own holds, whose fire was their father's fire and came from his forge, and the Cinderborn could not simply walk through it.

Rook saw it done. He saw a son of Hephaestus in the *66th*, a young sapper named Teodric Penhallow with a smith's arms and a stammer, stand at the corner of a passage in the outer storerooms with a keyed flamer and put a jet of his father's fire into a Cinderborn of the Ninefold Brand as it came round the corner singing. It stopped singing. It burned. It burned white, the river fire in its seams guttering out under the god's fire, and it fell, and didn't get up.

The sappers took the outer third of the vault in a cycle and held it for six cycles against counter-attacks from the inner passages. Then the Frost Masons flooded two of the three approach galleries with seep they had been storing behind walls of ice for a rotation, a black boiling flood that came down the galleries without warning and filled them to the roof, and the companies in the vault were withdrawn before the third could be cut.

The third battle came on the three hundred and sixtieth cycle, as a diversion for the *22nd* Trench Regiment's assault on the surface above. The sappers broke into the vault from below as Saldane's breachers came down the shafts from above, and the defenders, caught between them, were driven back into the inner storerooms, and the sappers sealed them in.

The *22nd* overran Vault 9 from the surface at the end of Rotation Eleven, and the Archflamen's soldiers trapped inside by the advance were given no quarter.

Rook was there for that too. He stood with the rest of the *66th* at the sealed doors of the inner storerooms while the Hephaestean engineers worked at the old heat-well conduits that ran beneath the vault from the Arsenal's casting lines, conduits the Archflamen's engineers had shut off at the start of the siege, and opened them, one after another.

They let the Phlegethon in.

The river came up the conduits from the casting lines, slowly at first and then in a long rising roar, the molten river-fire of the Phlegethon pouring up into the inner storerooms of Vault 9 through the floor, and the sappers stood at the sealed doors and felt the heat come through them and listened. They heard the Ninefold Brand begin to sing, on the other side of the doors, as the river came up round them. Then they heard nothing, and the fire filled the vault to its roof and set there, entombing the defenders and the Cinderborn with them, if the river's fire could kill the Cinderborn. Nobody ever found out whether it could.

The sappers called it their revenge. Rook didn't call it anything. He stood at the door with his hand on the hot steel and thought about Marchand, whom he had met twice, and Hollis, and Crisp, and the roll of the sapper hall at Hold Pyrgos, and after a while Cole touched his arm and he went back up to the surface.

**THE SAPPER HALL, HOLD PYRGOS. ROTATION TWELVE**

The Directorate extended the Forge's terms to the Third's trench volunteers in Rotation Twelve. The petition had been before it for a rotation.

Rook's mother wrote to him about it. She'd had the news from the Forge, and she thought he'd want to know. The families of the Third's soldiers lost below ground would receive their soldier's pay for the rest of their engagement, she wrote, as the Forge's families did, and their names would be entered on a roll, with the name of the gallery where they had fallen.

*I have put the rest of them on ours,* she wrote. *The ones from your company's galleries. Your Marshal sends the names to the sapper halls now, did you know? The Third's with the Forge's. Every one. I don't know whose idea that was. I have put them all on the roll at Pyrgos with the galleries beside them, after your Trooper Crisp. The other halls are doing the same, I'm told. The old women in the hall think I've gone mad. Let them.*

*Come home, Ansel. Dig as if you meant to stay, your father always said, and you have. Now come home.*

He kept the letter in the pocket of his tunic for the rest of the war.

By the end of the siege, the Deep List held two hundred and twenty thousand names.
