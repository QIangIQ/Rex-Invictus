## Chapter 4: The Cinder Stair

*"Dig, then shoot. Shoot, then dig. The ground keeps whatever you give it."*

*Centurion Ilsa Marrow, 6th Cohort, 44th Trench Regiment*

---

**LANDING SHELF KAPPA, THE HEAD OF THE CINDER STAIR. ROTATION ZERO, CYCLE 74**

Tobin Ashe had been told three things about the Cinder Stair before he came down the relay cable to Kappa, and two of them turned out to be lies.

The first was that it was a road. It was, technically, in the sense that people walked on it. It went down the face of the shelf wall from the lip of Landing Shelf Kappa to the Cinder Flats in forty-one switchbacks of cut black basalt, each one about four metres wide and two hundred long, with a drop on the open side of anything up to nine hundred metres and no parapet anywhere, because the Hephaesteans who cut it eight thousand years ago had been demigods with a good head for heights and hadn't seen the need. Ashe had looked over the edge of the first switchback on the morning he arrived and been sick on his boots, and the gang-boss had laughed at him, and then the gang-boss had looked over the edge himself and gone quiet.

The second was that the debt-bondsmen of the labour corps would be paid. They were paid. It went straight onto the debt-rolls of the Lethe Hold, against what they owed, and Ashe had done the sums on the cable down and worked out that if he laid track on the Cinder Flats for the rest of his natural life at the corps' rate, his grandchildren would owe a little less than his grandfather had.

The third thing, that nobody laying the track would carry an implant, was true.

"Line up, line up, you sorry bastards. Faces to the wall. Let the soldiers by."

The gang-boss was a big red-faced Lethe man named Corrin who had been a bondsman himself for twenty years and a gang-boss for twelve, which meant he still owed money but was allowed to shout. He came down the line of them with his cudgel tucked under his arm, slapping the backs of heads, pushing them up against the inner wall of the switchback, until the four hundred bondsmen of Gang Eleven were standing in a long row with their noses to the basalt and their backs to the drop.

Ashe turned his head as far as he dared and watched the *22nd* Trench Regiment go down the Stair.

They came down off the shelf in a column one cohort wide, which was all the Stair would take, and the column went down past him for six hours. He'd never seen so many soldiers in his life. He'd never imagined there were so many. They were in the grey plate of the Third, with the Third's falling-and-rising crest on the pauldron, carrying rifles and picks and entrenching tools and bedrolls and great square packs that stuck out on either side so that they had to turn sideways to pass each other, and every one of them had the small hard bump at the base of the skull under the rim of the helmet where the implant sat.

They didn't look at the bondsmen. They looked at their feet, and at the drop, and at the Arsenal.

From the third switchback, the Arsenal was visible across the Flats, small and black and sharp, a cinder cone on a spur of basalt nineteen kilometres away with the Phlegethon wrapped around the foot of it in a burning orange loop. There were lights on the cone. There were lights all over the Flats in front of it, long irregular lines of them, and Ashe didn't need anybody to tell him that those were trenches. The soldiers looked at the Arsenal as they went past, every one of them, a quick glance and then back to their feet. Nobody talked about it.

Nobody fired at them, either. The *22nd* was on the Stair for three cycles in the end, strung out down forty-one switchbacks on the open face of a cliff, one cohort wide, in full view of every gun on the spur, and not one shell came near it. Ashe heard the soldiers talking about that at the end of the first cycle, when they stopped for the night on the switchbacks below and he could hear them through the rock. They were saying that the Archflamen was waiting. They were saying that he wanted the whole Third down on the Flats before he showed his guns. They were saying a great many other things, mostly obscene, about the Archflamen and his mother and what he could do with his guns, and Ashe lay on the hard stone of Gang Eleven's switchback with his blanket over his head and listened to them, and envied them so badly it ached.

They'd die down there, a lot of them. Everybody knew it. They'd die, and the bump at the back of their skulls would send them up the relay to the ceiling and the Watchpoint, and nine days later they'd wake up in a vat somewhere with a new body and go back down the Stair and do it again.

Ashe would die down there too, and for him it would be the only time.

---

The work started on the second cycle, while the *22nd* was still going down past them.

The Third needed rail. It needed rail down the face of the shelf wall to bring the heavy guns of the *10th* Ordnance Phalanx down to the Flats, because a Phaethon heavy mortar weighed eleven hundred tonnes in its cradle and no relay cable in Tartarus would carry it. It needed rail across the Flats, from the foot of the Stair to the depots and the gun lines and the trenches, to carry the shells. And it needed somebody to lay it, and the somebody it found was the debt-rolls of the Lethe Hold, four hundred and twenty thousand men and women who owed the Empire money and had no way to pay it but their hands.

The soldiers called them the Quiet Ones. Ashe found out why on his first day.

They cut the rail bed into the face of the shelf wall beside the Stair, a second road beside the first, with picks and heat-lances and blasting charges that the Hephaestean engineers set and the bondsmen carried. It went down at a steeper angle than the Stair, in long straight runs with no switchbacks, and it had to be cut deep enough into the basalt to carry eleven hundred tonnes of gun on a cradle, and it had to be level, to the millimetre, or the gun would go over the edge. They worked in gangs of four hundred, in two shifts a cycle, with the Hephaesteans calling the angles and the gang-bosses shouting and the Third's soldiers going down the Stair beside them in an endless grey column, and every gang worked hanging over the drop on ropes.

On the first day, a man in Gang Nine slipped. Ashe saw him go. He was cutting at the face eighty metres down the rail bed from Ashe's own rope, a thin young man with a shaved head and a debt-brand on the back of his neck, and his foot went off the edge of the cut and his rope went taut and then snapped at the anchor, and he went down the face of the shelf wall in a long slow fall, turning over in the air, all the way down to the Flats, nine hundred metres, without a sound.

Ashe waited for somebody to shout. Nobody did. The soldiers on the Stair beside them stopped for a second, and looked over the edge, and one of them said something, and then they started walking again. The man's gang-boss came along the cut and looked at the broken rope and wrote something in a book.

Then Gang Nine went back to work. After a moment, so did Gang Eleven.

That evening, at the end of the shift, when the bondsmen of Gang Eleven were sitting on their switchback eating their ration-paste out of the tin, Ashe asked Corrin why nobody had shouted.

The gang-boss looked at him for a long time.

"You're new," he said.

"I'm new."

"Right." Corrin scraped the bottom of his tin with his spoon. "When a soldier falls off the Stair, his mates shout. They shout because he'll be back in a fortnight and he'll want to know they gave a shit. They'll say, 'Oi, Benno, you dozy fucker, you fell off the bloody Stair,' and he'll say, 'Yeah, I know, I was there,' and they'll all have a laugh about it." He put the tin down. "When one of us falls, there's nobody coming back to laugh about it. So what's the point in shouting?"

Ashe didn't have an answer to that.

"You'll get used to it," Corrin said. "Everybody does. And you'll stop talking, after a while. Everybody does that too. That's why they call us what they call us." He picked up his cudgel and stood up. "Get some sleep, new boy. We go down another twenty metres tomorrow."

**THE CINDER STAIR, SWITCHBACK 19. ROTATION ZERO, CYCLE 162**

Centurion Ilsa Marrow had been killed twice, and she had decided on the way down the Cinder Stair that if she had to die a third time she'd prefer it not to be because some ration-clerk in the column ahead of her had stopped to piss.

"Move!" she bellowed down the switchback. "Move, move your fucking feet! Who's holding up the column? Who's the shit-for-brains holding up my column?"

The column didn't move. It was the *44th* Trench Regiment, a hundred and seventy-nine thousand soldiers in plate and packs coming down the face of the shelf wall one cohort wide, and Marrow's *6th* Cohort was about a third of the way down it, on Switchback 19, and the switchback ahead of them had stopped. She could see why from here. Somewhere around Switchback 23, a long way below, a Mattock crawler of the engineers had thrown a track on the turn and was sitting sideways across the Stair with its dozer blade hanging over the drop and its crew standing around it in a little puzzled cluster, looking at it as if they hoped it would fix itself.

"Fucking engineers," she said.

"Fucking engineers, Centurion," her optio agreed. He was a tall gloomy man named Saul Brandt who had served with her for nine years and been killed nine times, and who could look at anything at all and find something about it to be miserable about. He was looking at the drop now. "Long way down, that."

"It's nine hundred metres, Saul. It's been nine hundred metres every single time you've asked."

"Just saying."

"Well, stop just saying." She pushed her helmet back and wiped the sweat off her forehead with the back of her glove. It was hot on the Stair. It was always hot on the Stair. The heat came up off the Flats in long slow waves that smelled of sulphur and cinder, and the shelf wall soaked it up all day and gave it back all night, and the *6th* Cohort had been sweating in its plate for a cycle and a half with no shade but the overhang of the switchback above. "How long to fix a track?"

"On the turn? With that lot?" Brandt considered the engineers below. "Hour. Two. They'll have to get the blade off before they can get the track back on, or she'll go over."

"Lovely." Marrow sat down on her pack. "Sit them down, Saul. Water, and nobody goes near the edge, and anybody who has to piss does it against the wall and not off the side, because I've had Hollis from the *4th* Maniple pissing off this Stair since we started and I swear to every god in the College that if he pisses on one more Quiet One I'll throw him off it myself."

"Centurion."

The cohort sat down. It took a while, on a road four metres wide, with six hundred soldiers wearing packs the size of a small dugout, and there was a good deal of shoving and cursing and somebody's entrenching tool got kicked over the edge and went down the face of the shelf wall clanging off the rock all the way to the Flats. Marrow ignored it. She was looking across the drop at the rail bed.

The Quiet Ones had been working on it for three months. It went down the face of the shelf wall beside the Stair in long straight runs, cut deep into the black basalt, and on the upper half of it there was track already laid, two lines of dull grey steel that came down from the lip of Kappa and ended at a cutting about level with Switchback 26. Below that the bed was still raw rock. The bondsmen were working on it in their gangs, hanging off the face on ropes with their picks and heat-lances, hundreds of them, hundreds upon hundreds of them strung across the black rock, and none of them talked. Marrow had been coming down past them for a cycle and a half and she hadn't heard one of them say a word.

As she watched, a gang on the lower bed hauled on a rope, and a long section of steel rail came down the face of the shelf wall on a cradle of cables and settled into the cutting at the bottom. A Hephaestean engineer in a red coat walked along it with a level, and nodded, and the gang began to spike it down.

"Poor bastards," Brandt said, following her eyes.

"Mm."

"No implants, I heard. Not one of them. Lethe debt-rolls."

"I heard that too."

"Lose one off that face, he's gone." Brandt shook his head. "I heard they've lost two hundred since they started. Off the face alone. That's not counting the ones the heat got, or the ones who got crushed when the rails came down."

Marrow didn't say anything. She'd heard the same number. She'd heard it from her colonel, at the briefing before the *44th* went down, along with a great many other numbers about the siege, most of which she'd found worrying, and that one she had found worst of all, though she couldn't have said why. A soldier of the Third died and came back. That was the deal. You signed up at twelve or thirteen, they put the implant in, and after that the worst thing that could happen to you was a bad fortnight. She'd been killed twice, once by a shell on a training range on Tamaris and once by something out of the Lethe fog on a posting she didn't like to think about, and she had woken up both times in a vat at the Watchpoint with nectar in her mouth and a new body that ached in all the old places. It was the only way she knew how to think about the war. The ground kept whatever you gave it, and you gave it your body, and you got a new one, and you went back.

The Quiet Ones gave it their bodies, and that was the end of it.

"Centurion?" Brandt said.

"What?"

"You've got a face on."

"I've always got a face on." She stood up, and looked down the Stair, and saw that the engineers on Switchback 23 had got the dozer blade off the Mattock and were swinging it in over the road on a winch. "On your feet. They're nearly done. And Saul."

"Centurion."

"Tell Hollis if he pisses on the Quiet Ones once more, I'll have him on the wire party every night till the end of the rotation."

"He'll say it's hot and they don't mind."

"I mind," Marrow said.

---

It took the *44th* three cycles to get down the Stair, which was about average, and when Marrow's *6th* Cohort finally came off the last switchback onto the Flats at the start of the fourth, she stopped at the foot of the shelf wall and looked up, and for a moment she couldn't see the top.

The shelf wall went up into the dark. Above it, a long way above, the ceiling was a faint pale smear of light where the relay cables ran up from Kappa to the convoys holding station in the low geometry eleven kilometres north of the lances, and the cables themselves came down out of the smear in long silver threads, dozens of them, every one of them moving. She could see things on them, small dark shapes coming down and going up in endless strings. Shells, mostly, the colonel said. Rations, water, nectar for the forward vats, timber and steel, and above all shells. The quartermasters had been told to stock the Flats for four rotations. They were stocking it for six.

At the foot of the Stair the Third had built a town. It had been six months since the *22nd* came down, and in six months the Third had dug in. There were depots dug into the ash and roofed with steel and basalt, and railheads where the Quiet Ones' track came down and ran out across the Flats in four directions, and tent lines and dugout lines and field hospitals and vat stations, and a Flamens' chapel made out of a shell-crate with a gold lamp painted on the door. There were relay pylons going out across the Flats behind the line, tall black spires of iron humming faintly in the heat, every one of them carrying the uploads of the Third up to the ceiling and the noosphere and the Watchpoint. And there were guns. Marrow had never seen so many guns. There were guns in the ash everywhere she looked, mortars and siege guns and divine batteries dug into their emplacements under nets and slag, row behind row of them, all the way out to the horizon, and none of them firing.

Nobody was firing. That was what she noticed most. Six months the Third had been on the Flats, and the guns of the Arsenal hadn't fired a single shell at it, and the Third hadn't fired a single shell back.

"Waiting for us," Brandt said, at her shoulder.

"Waiting for something." She shifted her pack. "Come on. We're due at the forward line by the end of the cycle, and I want to see what the fuckers have dug before they start shooting at me."

---

**THE GUN LINES, NORTH-WEST FLATS. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 41**

Gunner Nessa Carrow's job was to put Gun Four of Battery *26* exactly where the keyer told her to, and she was the best in the *3rd* Ordnance Regiment at it, and she had been waiting six months to prove it.

She'd been waiting so long that she'd started to dream about it. In the dream, she was in the layer's seat of Gun Four with her hands on the traverse wheels and her eye to the sight, and the order came, and she laid the gun, and the keyer slid the shell into the breech and put her hand on it and spoke the word, and the shell went out across the Flats in a streak of gold fire and came down on the outer belt in a burst of solar light so bright that it lit up the whole Arsenal, and then she woke up, and it was another cycle, and the Third still hadn't fired.

Today it was going to fire.

Gun Four was a divine mortar of the old Apollonian pattern, a squat bronze-and-steel tube three metres long set in a cradle of black iron in a pit cut six metres into the ash. It fired a shell the length of a woman's arm and the weight of a small child, and when the shell was keyed at the breech by a demigod of the battery, it burst in solar fire hot enough to melt the concrete of a bunker. Battery *26* had six of them, in six pits, in a line, with a ready pit behind each one where the keyed rounds were stacked, and a single keyer for the whole battery, which was the way of it in the Third. Keyers were scarce. Battery *26*'s was a boy of nineteen named Aster Lenn, a son of Apollo with bright fair hair and a nervous laugh, who had been posted down to the Third from the ceiling eight months ago and had never fired a shell in anger in his life.

"Are you nervous?" Carrow asked him.

They were sitting on the lip of the pit in the second watch, waiting for the order. It was hot, and quiet, and the Flats in front of them were dark all the way to the outer belt, twenty-six kilometres away, where the Archflamen's trenches made a faint long line of lamps against the dark.

"No," Lenn said. Then, "Yes. A bit. Are you?"

"I've been waiting six months for this."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting." She grinned at him. "Don't worry about it. All you've got to do is put your hand on the shell and say the word. I'll do the hard part."

"You'll point a tube."

"I'll point a tube at a target twenty-six kilometres away that I can't see, through a sight that was made before my great-grandmother was born, at a trajectory the Répertoire gave me eleven seconds ago, with a correction for the heat-shimmer off the Phlegethon that I'm going to have to make up as I go along," Carrow said. "And then you'll put your hand on it."

Lenn laughed. It was the nervous laugh, but it was a laugh.

"All right," he said. "You do the hard part."

At the change of the second watch, the order came.

---

It came down from Quarterdeck Nineteen, through the relay pylons, to every gun on the Flats at once. It wasn't spoken. It came as a single amber line on the pane at Carrow's elbow, in the same unhurried script as everything else the Répertoire said, and it was the shortest order she'd ever seen.

*All batteries. Open fire.*

"Gun Four!" the battery-centurion bellowed from the command pit. "Fire mission! Target Gamma One Six, outer belt, the wire! Lay on the Répertoire's solution! Keyer to Gun Four!"

Carrow was already in the seat. Her hands went to the wheels by themselves. She brought up the solution on her pane, a long string of figures for bearing and elevation and charge, and laid the gun on it, the traverse wheel clicking under her right hand and the elevation under her left, her eye to the old brass sight, the bubbles creeping into their marks. She made the correction for the shimmer. She made a correction for the slope of the pit, which had settled four millimetres since she'd last checked it, because the Flats were the Flats and nothing on them stayed level for long.

"Laid!" she shouted.

The loaders were already moving. Two of them came out of the ready pit with a shell between them, a long fat cylinder of grey steel with the sunburst of Apollo stamped on its nose, and they slid it into the breech of Gun Four, and Lenn stepped up beside them and put his hand flat on the base of the shell.

He closed his eyes. He said the word.

Carrow had heard keyers do it a hundred times in training and it still made the hair stand up on her arms. The word was quiet and belonged to no language she knew, and when Lenn said it, the shell under his hand began to glow, a soft gold light that came up from somewhere deep inside the steel and spread out along it until the whole shell was shining, and the air in the pit went hot and bright and smelled of summer, which she had never smelled in her life, and the loaders slammed the breech shut on it.

"Keyed!" Lenn shouted.

"Fire!"

Carrow pulled the lanyard.

Gun Four went off with a sound she felt in her teeth. The whole pit jumped. The shell went up out of the tube in a streak of gold fire that climbed into the dark over the Flats, and up, and up, until it was a small gold star going away from her, and on either side of it, along the whole of the line, she saw other stars going up, hundreds of them, gold and silver and red, rising off the Flats from horizon to horizon.

Nine hundred and forty guns of the Third opened fire at once, and for a moment the whole of the Cinder Flats was bright as day.

She would remember that moment for the rest of her life, which was a long time, given the vats. Six hundred years the Flats had been dark, lit only by the Phlegethon, and now the shells of the divine batteries were bursting over the Archflamen's outer belt from the Mora Cut to the Demus Gorge, solar shells from the children of Apollo that hung in the air a hundred metres over the trenches and burned like small suns, and iron shells from the batteries of Ares that burst in rings of jagged steel, and builder-shells from the aedificant batteries of Athena and Hephaestus that came down on the concrete of the bunkers and folded it in on itself. And behind it all, from eighty kilometres back, came the Phaethons of the *10th*. She heard them go over, a long slow rushing sound high in the dark, and a moment later, far away on the western Flats, she saw a flash of red light on the outer belt so big that it lit up the underside of the ceiling ninety kilometres overhead.

"Fuck me," Lenn said quietly, beside her.

"Load!" the battery-centurion was shouting. "Load, load! Next round! Same target! Keep them coming!"

They kept them coming.

---

They fired all through the second watch, and the third, and into the fourth. Gun Four fired three hundred and nine rounds. Carrow laid every one of them. Lenn keyed every one of them, his hand on the base of each shell and his eyes closed and the word coming out of him every time, and by the end of the third watch his voice had gone to a croak and his hand was shaking, and still the shells went gold under it.

By the fourth watch the barrel of Gun Four was glowing red. They were pouring condenser water over the breech from canvas buckets to keep the keyed shells from cooking off in the chamber, and the water came off it in clouds of steam that filled the pit and soaked them all to the skin, and Carrow couldn't see the sight for the steam and was laying by the feel of the wheels and the numbers on her pane. Her ears had stopped working some time in the third watch, and she didn't care, happier than she had ever been in her life.

The Arsenal still hadn't fired back.

"They're not going to," one of the loaders shouted at her, between rounds. "They're scared shitless! We've got them pinned!"

"Keep loading," Carrow said.

At the start of the fifth watch, the Arsenal's gunners replied.

---

She didn't see the first shells. Nobody in Battery *26* did. They fell short, a long way short, out in the dark of the Flats between the Third's line and the outer belt, and the gunners of Battery *26* were too busy and too deaf to notice them. The second salvo fell wide. Somebody saw that one. The battery-centurion came out of the command pit and stood on the lip of it for a while, looking out over the Flats at the bursts, and then went back in again and started shouting into the relay.

The third salvo fell closer. It fell on a battery of the *14th* Ordnance Regiment four kilometres to the south, and Carrow saw the fire of it, solar fire, gold, the same as her own, burning on the Flats where a battery had been.

"They've got our shells," Lenn said. He'd stopped keying. He was standing beside the breech with his hand still raised and his face gone white under the soot. "Nessa. They've got our shells. They're keying them."

"Of course they've got our shells. They've got eighty-one percent of everybody's shells. Key the fucking round, Aster."

He keyed it. She fired it.

The fourth salvo fell on the Third's line all along its length. It fell on the trenches of the forward regiments, and on the gun lines behind them, and on the rail-line where the Quiet Ones were still laying track, and it wasn't gold anymore. Some of it was gold, solar fire from shells keyed by the Arsenal's children of Apollo. Some of it was red, the iron rings of Ares. And some of it was a colour Carrow had never seen before, a deep dull orange that wasn't sunlight or fire, that came down on the Flats and burst in great slow clots of molten metal that spread across the ash and set it burning.

"Hephaestean," the battery-centurion said, from the lip of the command pit. "Forge-fire. They've got keyers from the casting lines."

The fifth salvo was ranging. Carrow could see it. It walked up the Flats toward the gun line in a long steady march of bursts, one after another, four hundred metres apart, then three hundred, then two, and every burst was closer to Battery *26* than the last. Somebody on the Arsenal's side of the Flats was watching her gun flashes. They didn't know where the Third's guns were. They were firing on the flashes, and Battery *26* had been firing for nine hours.

"Cease fire!" the battery-centurion was shouting. "Cease fire! All guns! Cover! Get your fucking heads down!"

Carrow came out of the layer's seat. Lenn was still standing at the breech with his hand raised, staring up the Flats at the bursts walking toward them. She grabbed him by the arm and pulled, and he came, stumbling, and they went down into the bottom of the pit together beside the loaders, and pressed themselves against the wet ash of the wall.

She heard it coming. It made a sound she would remember for the rest of her life too, a thin high whine coming down out of the dark, getting louder.

It wasn't on Gun Four. It went past Gun Four, close enough that she felt the heat of it on her face, a deep dull orange streak, and came down in the ready pit behind the gun, where three hundred keyed rounds had been stacked that morning and forty were left.

The forty rounds in the ready pit went up together.

---

She woke in a vat.

She woke the way everyone woke, coming up out of the dark with the taste of nectar in her mouth and nose and the warm golden weight of it all around her, and an orderly's hook catching the harness at her shoulders and pulling her up toward the light, and she came out of the vat coughing and choking and naked and shaking with cold, into a long white hall full of vats and orderlies and the smell of honey, and the first thing she said, when she could speak, was, "Gun Four."

"It's all right," the orderly said. She was an old woman with a gun-loader's arms. She wrapped a blanket around Carrow's shoulders without being asked. "You're at the Watchpoint. Hades. You're home. Can you tell me your name?"

"Gun Four," Carrow said again. "Where's Gun Four?"

"I don't know what that is, dear. Can you tell me your name?"

"Carrow. Nessa Carrow. Layer. Gun Four, Battery *26*, *3rd* Ordnance Regiment, *3rd* Siege Army." She was shaking so hard her teeth were chattering. "How long?"

"Eight days," the orderly said. "You came up with eight others from the same battery. All in good order. No nulls." She looked down at her slate. "Lenn, Aster, keyer. Mosca, Dov, Borro, loaders. Abend..."

Carrow stopped listening. She put her face in the blanket and breathed. Eight days. All in good order. No nulls. She tried to remember the end and couldn't. She remembered the whine coming down out of the dark, and the heat on her face, and then nothing, and then the vat. It had always been like that, the three times she'd been killed before. There was never any pain to remember. There was the moment before and then there was the vat, and in between, somewhere, there was a gap that was nothing at all.

"I want to go back," she said.

"You will, dear. Two weeks, three at most, and you'll be back with your battery."

"I want to go back now."

The orderly looked at her for a moment, and then she smiled, and patted her shoulder through the blanket.

"They all say that," she said.

---

It was four weeks before she got back to Battery *26*, and when she did, Gun Four was gone.

It was gone completely. Where its pit had been there was a crater twenty metres across, glazed black at the bottom where the solar fire had burned for six hours after the ready pit went up, and the ash around the crater had been fused into a long smooth sheet of black glass that crunched under her boots. Lenn was standing at the edge of it, looking down, when she came up from the railhead. He'd been back for a week. He looked older than she remembered, though she knew that was nonsense; he was in a new body that was exactly as old as the last one had been.

"They're not replacing it," he said, without turning around. "Gun Four. The battery-centurion asked. There's a waiting list for divine mortars as long as your arm. We might get one in Rotation Two."

"Rotation Two." Carrow came and stood beside him. "That's a year."

"I know."

"So what do we do till then?"

"Load for Gun Three." He shrugged. "Dig. The battery-centurion says the Répertoire's revised its estimate of the artillery duel. It's not sure we'll win it anymore. They found us at thirty-one kilometres in four hours, Nessa. In the dark. On our own flashes. Nobody's done that to the Third in six hundred years."

Carrow looked at the crater for a while.

"Lucky hit," she said. "Extreme range."

"That's what the Répertoire says."

"Is that what you say?"

Lenn didn't answer. He was looking out across the Flats toward the outer belt, twenty-six kilometres away, where the Arsenal's lamps made their long faint line against the dark.

"I keyed three hundred and nine shells on the first night," he said at last. "Every one of them burst in solar fire over their trenches. I don't know how many people I killed. A lot. Thralls, most of them. People who'd been sold down to the casting lines for debt." He was quiet for a moment. "They don't have implants, Nessa. None of them. Every one of them I killed stayed dead."

"They'd have done the same to us."

"They did do the same to us. We came back." He turned away from the crater. "Come on. The battery-centurion wants us on Gun Three by the end of the watch."

**THE FORWARD LINE, NORTH-WEST FLATS. ROTATION ONE, CYCLES 41 TO 52**

While Battery *26* was dying in its pit, Ilsa Marrow was digging a hole.

The *29th* Trench Regiment went forward first. Marrow watched it go from the old line in the second watch, with the barrage going over her head and the Flats lit gold and silver and red by the bursts on the outer belt. A hundred and seventy thousand soldiers walked out into the dark on a front of nine kilometres, in open order, with rifles slung and picks in their hands, and they walked for six hours across fourteen kilometres of ash, and then they stopped and started to dig. They weren't attacking. Nobody was attacking yet. The guns would need weeks to soften the outer belt, and the job of the *29th* was to get forward under the cover of the barrage, while the enemy kept its head down, and dig a line twenty-six kilometres from the outer belt, so that the guns could come up behind it.

The *44th* went forward six hours later, on the *29th*'s left, to dig the flank.

It was the first time Marrow had walked across the Flats toward an enemy who had guns, and it was nothing like the Lethe holds. In the holds, the enemy came at you out of the fog, and you held the line, and the line was where it had always been. Here, there was no line. There was just the ash, black and soft and deep, sinking under her boots with every step, and the dark, and the gold and silver light of the bursts on the outer belt far ahead of her, and the faint flashes along the horizon where the Arsenal's gunners were starting to reply.

She didn't know about Battery *26* yet. She saw the fire of it, a long way behind her on the gun line, a great gold flare that went up into the dark and burned and burned, and she thought, *some poor bastard of a gunner*, and kept walking.

At the end of the sixth hour, the colonel called the halt.

"Dig," Marrow said, and the *6th* Cohort dug.

---

They dug rifle pits first, one to a pair of soldiers, a metre and a half deep and two long, with the spoil thrown forward to make a parapet. It took an hour for a fit soldier in good ash. It took the *6th* Cohort three, because the ash wasn't good. It was wet underneath, a few centimetres down, where the seep had soaked into it, and it slumped back into the pit as fast as they could throw it out, and it was hot, and it stank of sulphur, and every few minutes a shell from the Arsenal came down somewhere along the line and they had to throw themselves flat in their half-dug holes and wait for it to pass.

By the end of the seventh watch, the first rifle pits of the siege had been dug along nine kilometres of the Flats. Marrow went down her stretch of them at the end of the watch with Brandt behind her, checking them, kicking the parapets to see if they'd hold, and found that most of them would.

"Now join them up," she said.

That took four days. Each rifle pit was joined to the next by a narrow trench, so that each section had a single trench of its own. The section trenches were joined into maniple trenches, and the maniple trenches into the cohort trench, and the cohort trench into the trenches of the cohorts on either side. On the fifth day the *6th* Cohort held a continuous line two kilometres long and a metre and a half deep, with fire bays cut every eight metres and traverses between them so that one shell couldn't kill the soldiers of two bays, and behind it the reserve had started on the second line.

It was all in the manual. The Third had been digging on the Cinder Flats for six hundred years, and its manual hadn't changed in four hundred of them, and every soldier in the *6th* Cohort could have recited it in their sleep. The fire bays every eight metres. The traverses. The dugouts six metres down, roofed with steel and basalt. The communication trenches running back to the support line in zigzags. The wire teams going out every night to lay three belts of razor-wire against patrols and raiders. They'd done it all a thousand times, in training, on the Flats, in peacetime, with the Arsenal sitting on its spur nineteen kilometres away and the thralls in the casting lines who now held its guns waving at them from the ore road.

Marrow kept noticing that none of it was any different now. The Third had trained for this exact war for six hundred years, and now it was fighting it, and it went exactly as the manual said, every metre of it, apart from the shells.

The Arsenal's gunners fired on the working parties every watch, sparingly, a shell every hour or so, at random, somewhere along the line, and every one of them was keyed, and every one of them killed somebody. The *29th* lost three hundred and eighty soldiers in its pits on the first night. The *44th* lost two hundred and six in the first four days. The centurions logged the transfers and kept digging. And from the slag heaps in front of the outer belt, four kilometres away, the Archflamen's marksmen shot anyone who put his head above the parapet for longer than a second. Marrow lost eleven soldiers to them in the first week, every one with a single round through the visor.

"Four kilometres," Brandt said, looking at the eleventh, a girl of seventeen named Tessa who'd been in the Third for four years and had just died for the second time. "Who the fuck shoots at four kilometres?"

"Somebody with a very good rifle," Marrow said, "and a great deal of time on their hands." She closed the girl's visor. "She'll be back in a fortnight. Get her on the stretcher. And tell everybody to keep their fucking heads down."

---

On the eleventh cycle, the ground boiled.

It happened in the *29th*'s sector, two kilometres to the right of Marrow's stretch of line, in the trenches of the *4th* Cohort. Marrow heard it before she knew what it was: a long, rising, terrible sound, carried down the line in the still air of the second watch, that she took at first for a shell coming in and threw herself flat for, until it went on too long. It went on, rising and falling, for more than a minute, and it was human, and it was coming from under the ground.

She got up and ran.

By the time she reached the *4th* Cohort's line, it had stopped. There was a crowd of soldiers of the *29th* standing around the mouth of a dugout shaft, staring down into it, and steam was coming up out of the shaft in thick white clouds that smelled of sulphur and something else, something worse, and one of the soldiers was sitting on the parapet with his head in his hands and his whole body shaking.

"What happened?" Marrow asked.

A centurion of the *29th* looked at her. He was grey in the face.

"Sappers," he said. "Cutting the dugout. Six metres down." He swallowed. "They hit a vein."

She knew what he meant. Everybody who'd served on the Flats knew about the veins. Under the ash, threading through the basalt, ran heat veins from the Phlegethon, thin channels of river-fire that had worked their way up through the rock over thousands of years and lay a few metres under the surface, waiting. Most of them were small. Some of them weren't. A trench cut across one would fill with steam, and a dugout cut into one...

"How many?" she asked.

"A maniple." The centurion looked down into the shaft. "Sixty-one. They came up the shaft at a thousand degrees."

Marrow didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. Sixty-one soldiers of the *29th* had been boiled alive in a hole six metres under the Flats, and they'd be back in a fortnight, waking in vats at the Watchpoint, screaming. Then they'd come back down the Stair, and their centurion would put them back in the line, and the line would be dug somewhere else.

"Heat-rods," she said. "Walk every line with heat-rods before you dig it."

"We are now," the centurion said.

They did, after that, the whole of the Third, every line and every dugout, the engineers going out ahead of the working parties with long steel probes that they pushed down into the ash every metre, listening for the hum of a vein. The Third's trench maps of the campaign marked the veins in red from that cycle on. There were a great many of them.

---

**THE FLATS. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 70**

It went on like that, for weeks.

The Third dug, and the Arsenal shelled it. The Third's guns fired on the outer belt every watch, and the Arsenal's guns fired back at their flashes. The two armies sat facing each other across twenty kilometres of ash, the Third in its new trenches and the Archflamen's thralls in the outer belt, and neither of them crossed it.

Marrow stood in her fire bay one night at the end of the seventieth cycle with Brandt beside her and looked out over the parapet through a periscope at the outer belt, which she'd been looking at every night for a month.

It was a formidable obstacle. She'd been told so in the briefing and hadn't believed it until she saw it. Where it was complete, it was nine kilometres deep. In front of it was a belt of slag-cast tank traps a hundred metres deep, great jagged lumps of black slag poured into moulds by the casting lines and set out across the ash in rows. Behind the traps was an anti-tank ditch eight metres wide and five deep. Behind the ditch were belts of razor-wire so dense that the Third's shells had been falling on them for a month and they still stood, and behind the wire were the trenches, and under the trenches, she'd been told, shelters cut sixteen and twenty metres into the basalt, deep enough that all but the heaviest shells passed over them.

And under all of it, the mines. The Répertoire said three or four million in the outer belt alone, laid by hand to a plan drawn up by the Arsenal's ordnance clerks, who had been making mines for eight thousand years.

"We're going to have to go across that," Brandt said.

"Yes."

"On foot."

"Probably."

"With the mines."

"Saul." She took her eye from the periscope. "Is there something you'd like to say?"

He thought about it for a while.

"It's going to be a fucking awful war, Centurion," he said.

Marrow looked out over the parapet again, at the long faint line of lamps on the outer belt, and the dark ash in front of it, and the gold flicker of the Third's shells still bursting over the wire.

"Dig, then shoot," she said. "Shoot, then dig. The ground keeps whatever you give it." She picked up her entrenching tool. "Come on. We've got a sap to cut before the end of the watch."

**THE RAIL-LINE, NORTH-WEST FLATS. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 88**

Tobin Ashe had been on the Flats for eight months when the Arsenal's guns found the rail-line, and by then he had stopped talking.

It had happened slowly, the way Corrin had said it would. He'd talked to the others of Gang Eleven in the first weeks, on the switchback at night, about the Lethe Hold and their debts and their families and what they'd do when the siege was over and they'd paid off what they owed. Then he had talked to them less, as the weeks went on, and Gang Eleven had gone down the shelf wall and out onto the Flats with the rail-line behind it, and men had begun to die. A man of Gang Eleven fell off the face of the shelf wall on the fourth week. Two were crushed under a rail-cradle on the sixth. Eleven died of the heat in the eighth, laying track across the Flats in the open with the ash at eighty degrees and the Third's soldiers watching them from the shade of their trenches. Each time it happened, the gang-boss came and wrote something in his book, and the gang buried the dead beside the line where they fell, and went back to work.

There was nobody to tell. Nobody coming back to laugh about it.

By the eighth month he had stopped talking altogether. Most of Gang Eleven had. They worked in silence, eating in silence, sleeping in silence on the bare ash beside the track with their blankets over their heads, and when the Third's soldiers passed them on the line, going up to the trenches or back to the depots, the soldiers looked at them and then looked away, and none of them said anything either.

The rail-line was four hundred kilometres long now. It ran out from the foot of the Cinder Stair across the Flats in four directions, to the depots and the gun lines and the forward trenches, and it carried shells, mostly. Ashe had laid perhaps a twentieth of it himself, a metre at a time, with his hands. He'd been proud of it once, a little, in the early weeks. He didn't think about it anymore.

On the eighty-eighth cycle of Rotation One, Gang Eleven was laying a spur out to a new gun line on the north-west Flats, eleven kilometres behind the forward trenches, in the open, at the end of the second watch. Ashe was spiking down a rail with a long steel hammer, swinging it up and bringing it down on the spike in a steady rhythm that he no longer had to think about, when he heard the whine.

He'd heard it before. Everybody on the Flats had heard it by now, the thin high sound of a keyed shell coming down out of the dark. He'd heard it a hundred times, and every time it had come down somewhere else.

He stood with the hammer in his hands and looked up.

He saw it coming, a long dull orange streak against the dark, falling toward the rail-line in a slow curve, bigger and brighter and closer than any of the others had ever been. Beside him, Corrin was looking up too, with his cudgel in his hand and his red face gone suddenly pale, and around them the bondsmen of Gang Eleven had all stopped work and were standing on the track with their hammers and their spikes, looking up at it.

Nobody ran, since there was nowhere on the Flats to run to, and nobody said anything either.

It came down thirty metres along the line, among the rail-cradles, and burst in a great slow clot of molten metal that spread out across the track and the ash and the men of Gang Eleven, and Tobin Ashe, who had owed the Empire a little less than his grandfather had, saw it coming toward him across the ground, orange and bright and quiet, and thought, quite clearly, that at least now nobody would have to shout.

---

Gang Eleven lost a hundred and forty men on the eighty-eighth cycle. The gang-boss of Gang Twelve, who came to take over the line the next morning, wrote all their names in his book, and the bondsmen of Gang Twelve buried them beside the track where they fell, in a long row, in the ash, without markers.

Over the following ten rotations, the Quiet Ones laid four thousand kilometres of track across the Cinder Flats and buried sixty thousand of their own number beside it. The Third's soldiers came to call the rows of graves along the rail-lines the Quiet Mile, and in the later rotations of the siege, when the cost of it was known, they took to leaving a single spent shell-casing on the ash beside the track as they went past, upright, as a marker.

None of the sixty thousand came back. Their names are on a memorial on the ceiling at Landing Shelf Kappa, at the head of the Cinder Stair, cut into the black basalt of the shelf wall where the first switchback begins, in a single long column of very small letters that goes down the face of the wall and over the edge.
