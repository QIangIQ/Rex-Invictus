## Chapter 35: The Hearth and the Door

*"Keep the lamp. Whatever else they take, keep the lamp."*

Vestal Theodora Kyme, from her lamp-book, the House of the Hearth

---

**THE UNDERCROFT. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 205**

The borer broke through into the undercroft at the second bell of the first cycle of the final assault, and Verrill Chase was eleven metres behind it with a relay set on her back.

She had been under the cone for nine cycles by then, with Rook's company, in the galleries the Hephaestean sappers had been driving toward the Acropolis since the twelfth rotation. There were forty kilometres of them, running from the old inner belt to the foot of the cone and under it, and she had walked most of them by now, bolting relay sets to the gallery walls as she went. The sappers had stopped asking her who she was on the second cycle. By the fifth they were carrying her sets for her.

The borer's head went through the last metre of basalt with a long grinding shriek and stopped, and the dust came rolling back down the gallery over all of them, and when it cleared there was a hole in the face of the rock in front of the borer, three metres across, and through it, a corridor.

It was a corridor of dressed stone, eight thousand years old, wide enough for two carts to pass, with iron lamp-brackets on the walls every ten metres and the lamps long out. It ran away into the dark in both directions. It smelled of old smoke, and of the river, and of something else that Chase didn't have a name for and didn't like.

"That's it," said Rook, beside her. He was standing at the borer's shoulder with his lamp held up, looking through the hole. "The undercroft. We're under the Acropolis."

"How far under?"

"Hundred and ten metres. Give or take." He looked round at her. "Can you reach?"

Chase unslung the relay set from her back and bolted it to the gallery wall beside the borer, at the forty-metre mark from the last one, and connected it, and watched the little amber light on its face come on, and steady.

"I can reach," she said.

---

The Acropolis on the surface was only the upper works of the Arsenal. Beneath it lay kilometres of corridors and galleries and storerooms, heat-wells that ran down to the edge of the Phlegethon to feed the ward-stones, and magazines that held eight thousand years of the Empire's weapons, among them engines that had not been fielded since the Second Grand Offensive. The upper levels had been turned into barracks and shelters during the bombardments. Some of the rooms had been put to other uses by the Kindlers and the Unremembered. The sappers who found those rooms made reports about them that the Hunt has kept sealed ever since, and Chase, who saw two of the rooms herself, on the third and fifth cycles, never spoke about them to anyone.

The sappers cleared the undercroft corridor by corridor, with shotguns and charges, against everything the Kindlers had let loose below. The worst were the ore-hounds. They had been mastiffs once, the great slab-headed dogs the thrall-masters of the Arsenal had used to drive the labour gangs in the casting lines, and the Kindlers had taken several hundred of them and opened them and filled them with something from the deep. They had grown to the size of carriers, with river-fire in their throats, and they hunted the galleries in packs of six and seven, in the dark, in silence, until they were on you. Several hundred sappers died on the first cycle alone. By the end of it the companies held only a small foothold on the lowest levels of the undercroft, a few hundred metres of corridor round the borer's hole.

And the dead sappers came back.

That was what made the undercroft different from anything that had gone before it in the siege, and Rook understood it on the first cycle, at the end of the first watch, when the company's relay operator, a quiet woman called Sennet Orme who had been on the Deep List board since the twelfth rotation, turned round from her set and stared at him.

"Lieutenant," Orme said. "Teague."

Osric Teague had been killed twenty minutes before, by an ore-hound, in a side corridor three hundred metres in, a hundred and fifteen metres under the cone. Rook had seen the body.

"What about him?" Rook said.

"He's clean, sir." Orme's voice was unsteady. "His upload. It went through. Clean, on the cadence. All the way up to Hades." She looked back at her set. "So did Ashby's. And Corrie's. All of them, sir. Every one we've lost since we broke in."

Rook stood still in the corridor with his lamp in his hand.

In the seventeenth rotation the Deep List had taken a third of his company. Soldiers who died more than fifty metres under the basalt could not reach a relay, and went onto the List, and were gone. Brin Hollis was on it. Mattias Cole was on it. Ivor Lund was on it. Aled Abery was on it. His mother kept their names on the roll in the sapper-hall at Hold Pyrgos, in her own hand, and he had written to her after every one of them.

He turned round and looked at Verrill Chase.

She was sitting on an ammunition box against the corridor wall beside the borer's hole, with her cracked spectacles pushed up into her hair and a relay set in pieces in her lap, eating a ration bar. She looked up at him.

"What?" she said.

---

Her explanation of the forty-metre interval was recorded in the company diary that night, in Rook's hand. He asked her for it and wrote it down word for word.

"Basalt attenuates the signal," she said. "It's dense, and it's full of iron, and the heat down here makes it worse. On the old single upload at death, you had seven seconds and one chance, and if the nearest relay was more than fifty metres off through rock, the handshake never closed and the upload had nowhere to go. On the three-second cadence, it's a short burst, every three seconds, and it's even more fragile, but it keeps trying. Beyond forty metres of basalt, the burst loses the handshake. Inside forty metres, it holds. So you put a set every forty metres. Any closer and you're wasting sets. Any further and you're losing people."

Rook wrote it down. Then he looked up from the diary.

"Why didn't anybody do this before?" he said.

Chase considered the question seriously.

"Because nobody expected the uploads to go anywhere except the Mast," she said. "Everybody down here's been fighting the Mast for eighteen rotations. Nobody was thinking about the rock." She shrugged. "And my family built half the relays in the northern sectors, Lieutenant. I grew up taking them apart on the kitchen table. I know what they'll do."

On the third cycle, Rook came back from a clearance on the upper level with something in his hand, and stood in front of her for a moment, awkwardly, holding it out.

It was a handful of the white rock flowers that grew round the heat vents in the deep galleries, small pale stars on stalks of grey stone, the only things that grew anywhere under the Cinder Flats. The sappers called them vent-lilies. They were hard to pick without breaking.

"For the chain," Rook said. "I suppose. On behalf of the company."

Chase looked at the flowers, and then at him, and went rather pink. She took one and put it behind her ear, under the pencil. She wore it there for the rest of the clearance.

---

It took weeks.

The sappers fought upward a level at a time, clearing every room with gas charges before they went in, and the dead came back. Sappers killed in the undercroft came out of the vats at Watchpoint Hades nineteen cycles later, whole, with their names and their faces, and asked, every one of them, to be sent back down to the same gallery. The Répertoire had never received such a request before from a soldier recovered off the Deep List, since no soldier had ever been recovered off the Deep List. It logged every one of them.

Osric Teague came back down to Rook's company on the twenty-fourth cycle of the clearance, in new grey, a little stiff from the vats, and walked into the forward gallery and stood in front of Rook and saluted.

"Reporting back, sir," he said. "Did I miss much?"

Rook found, to his own surprise, that he couldn't answer him for a moment.

Chase's chain did more than any single engagement of the final assault to change the arithmetic of the siege. For the first time in eighteen rotations, the Third could fight under the cone without writing off its dead.

**THE HOUSE OF THE HEARTH. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 206**

While the Second Cohort fought for the Katabasion and the Achilleans cleared the palace, Thalia led the Hunt and the *6th* Moonwall into the House of the Hearth.

It was a squat old fortress on the eastern terrace of the cone, built of grey stone with walls six metres thick, to house the forty Vestals of Hestia who had come down from Hold Serapis with the Archflamen at the start of his tour of the Third. The Hunt's orders were to clear it. Thalia also meant to find out what had become of the Vestals. None of them had been seen since the revolt.

They found the Archflamen first.

He was in the Vestals' hall, beneath the great hearth of the House, which had been put out. The hearth was a wide round basin of black stone in the middle of the hall, cold, full of grey ash, and something was sitting in it.

After his fall, Draugos had kept him in the deepest cells beneath the cone, to wait for the Kindlers' door. When the war was lost and Draugos went down the door himself, the Unremembered had taken what was left of Sabinus Orrhae and his household, and done to them what the Unremembered did. What sat in the cold hearth was grey, and soft, and too large, with too many hands. It was speaking. It was speaking in the Archflamen's old voice, the warm rich voice that had preached to the Third in the vat-halls at the start of his tour, and it was repeating his sermons, fragments of them, out of order, one after another, a sentence here and a phrase there, with no sign at all that it knew its own name.

"He knows me," it said, as the Hunt came into the hall. "He knows me. The river is patient. Your dead are not lost, they are gathered. He knows me. Come closer, my children. He knows me."

Thalia stood at the edge of the hall and looked at it for a long time.

Hunt-Elder Sarn walked forward on her metal legs to the edge of the hearth, and stood there, and looked at it too, closely, with her staff in her hand. Then she turned round.

"There's nothing left, Lieutenant," she said. "Of the man. Nothing that could be brought back. I'm sorry."

"I know," said Thalia. "Roe."

Shield-Captain Roe came forward with the flame-hunters of her company behind her, her arm still in its sling, and they burned it with cold fire, silver-blue, until the hearth was full of frost. The Hunt's report notes that it went on speaking for some time while it burned.

So ended Sabinus Orrhae, Archflamen of the Deep, whose gospel had raised six million souls against the Empire and delivered them to the river.

The Hunt cleared the rest of the House in two cycles. Its defenders did not surrender and were not offered terms. There was no sign of the Vestals anywhere in it.

---

**THE CELLS BENEATH THE HOUSE OF THE HEARTH. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 208**

The Vestals were found on the third cycle, and it was the sappers who found them.

A gallery driven up from the undercroft broke through the floor of a cell block beneath the House that the Hunt had not yet reached. Chase was at the head of the gallery with a relay set on her back, as she usually was by then, and when the last of the rock gave way under the borer's head, the floor of the gallery went with it, and she fell two metres into the cell below.

She landed hard, on her side, on the relay set, and lay there for a moment in the dark with the wind knocked out of her, and then she realised that it wasn't entirely dark.

There was a light in the cell. It was a small light, low down, a single flame. She lifted her head.

There were six women in the cell. They were sitting on the floor in a ring, in the remains of grey and flame-orange robes, the robes of the House, and they were so thin that Chase could see every bone in their faces and their hands. They were sitting round a clay lamp the size of a hand, a plain old lamp of red clay with a single wick, and the wick was lit, and the flame on it was small and steady and clean. It was the only light in the cell. Nobody looked at Chase. They were all looking at the lamp.

"Hello," Chase said, from the floor. Her voice came out a whisper. "Hello. It's all right. I'm, we're from the Third. The sappers. It's all right. You're safe."

None of them answered. None of them looked at her. Above her, through the hole in the ceiling, she could hear Rook shouting her name, and the sappers coming down the gallery at a run.

The draught from the hole came down into the cell, and the flame on the lamp bent and flickered. One of the six women, the eldest of them, with long white hair, put out her hand at once and cupped it round the flame, without looking up, to keep it from going out.

Chase lay on the floor of the cell and looked at the old woman's hand round the lamp, and began, quite suddenly, to cry.

---

The Hunt's interrogation of captured Kindlers established the rest of it, afterward.

The Kindlers had taken all forty Vestals of the House into the cells on the first night of the revolt. A Vestal's hearth was among the oldest fires in the Empire, older than the Arsenal, lit from Hestia's own hearth on Olympus, and the deep wanted it put out. The Kindlers had put out the great hearth in the Vestals' hall the same night. But a Vestal carried her hearth with her, and the forty had carried a lamp down into the cells, lit from the great hearth before it went out, and for eighteen rotations the Kindlers had tried every method available to them to make the Vestals let it go out.

Thirty-four of the Vestals had died in the cells.

The six who survived had kept the lamp lit the whole time. The Kindlers had never put it out.

The eldest of them, with the white hair, was Theodora Kyme. She had kept the oldest lamp in the Katabasion for thirty-two years before the revolt, and she had written five hundred and eleven lines in her lamp-book for the strays of the Arsenal, and the lamp-book was found in the cell, under her, with its last line written in a hand that grew smaller and smaller down the page until the words could hardly be read. It said: *Keep the lamp. Whatever else they take, keep the lamp.*

None of the six knew their own names any more, or recognised anyone round them. They knew the lamp.

Chase sat down on the cell floor with them, after Rook had got her up and made sure nothing was broken, and stayed there, with her back to the wall and her knees drawn up, saying nothing, until the Hunt arrived.

---

The Hunt took the Vestals into its own custody. They were carried up out of the cells on stretchers, with the lamp still burning in a Hunter's cupped hands, pending the sister-augurs' assessment of whatever it was the Kindlers had tried to put into them in eighteen rotations.

Chase objected.

She went up out of the cells after them, through the House, to the Vestals' hall, where Thalia was standing beside the cold hearth with the frost still on it, and walked straight up to the Lieutenant of the Hunt, through the Hunters round her, and stopped in front of her. It was the only time the two of them are recorded as speaking to each other during the siege.

"They're not prisoners," Chase said. "They're not evidence. They kept that lamp lit for eighteen rotations, Lieutenant, in the dark, while thirty-four of their sisters died round them, and they don't know their own names any more, and you're taking them off to be looked at by augurs." Her voice shook. "That's not fair. That's not fair at all."

Thalia looked down at her.

Chase was half a head shorter than the Lieutenant, with soot on her face and the white vent-lily behind her ear and her cracked spectacles askew, and she was shaking, and she didn't move.

"No," Thalia said. "It isn't fair."

Chase blinked.

"I can't let them go," Thalia said. "Not yet. I don't know what was done to them, and neither do you, and until the augurs do, I can't put them anywhere they might do harm to themselves or anyone else. But I'll see to it personally that they're kept warm, and fed, and that the lamp stays with them, lit, in their own hands. Every cycle. Myself. You have my word."

Chase looked at her for a long moment.

"All right," she said at last. "All right. Thank you."

She turned round and walked back out of the hall toward the cells and the gallery and the undercroft, and didn't look back. Thalia watched her go.

"That's her," Atlanta said quietly, beside her. "Isn't it."

"Yes."

"She's younger than I thought."

"So was he," said Thalia. "Once."

---

**THE INNER WARD. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 230**

With the outer ward, the palace, the Katabasion, the House of the Hearth and the undercroft in the Third's hands, only the inner ward remained.

It stood on its own half of the cone, across the narrow gorge that split the summit, with the Echo Mast at its top and the Tabularium at its foot. The Tabularium was the old Enforcement keep of the Arsenal, a hall of records and cells, and its only bridge to the rest of the Acropolis had fallen in the bombardment. The inner ward had always been the most secure part of the Arsenal. Enforcement had kept its prisoners in the Tabularium, and with them the theatre's tithe of unregistered Hecatean strays, waiting for Tithe Day. The Archflamen had let the strays out on the first night of the revolt. Haruspex Ilan Dace had kept one of them for himself. The girl Mirren.

The sister-augurs placed the Kindlers' door in the Tabularium, in its lowest hall, where the Kindlers had opened it at last after building it for so long on the casting floor below. Their reading of how it had been kept open settled one of the central questions of the siege.

The Echo Mast had fed it.

Every upload the Mast had stripped from a dying soldier of the Third for eighteen rotations had passed through Dace and the girl he kept, and gone down into the door, to hold it open. That was what the Mast had been for, from the moment the Archflamen's people turned it. Draugos had gone through the door with the Cinderborn after the war was lost, and it had stayed open behind him. Sister-Augur Kestrel reported that it was guarded, and named the guard. Echidna, the mother of monsters, had come up behind Kampe.

"Kampe was the key," Kestrel said, at the war-table in the southern bastion. "Echidna is the door."

Destroying the door was a task for the Hunt alone. The plan for the inner ward was a translation assault from the *Selene's Arrow* directly into the Tabularium by the Hunt and the *6th* Moonwall together, with Roe leading the Moonwall and Thalia the Hunt, and Sarn going with them to seal the door when they reached it.

But the sister-augurs advised that the door could not be sealed while the Mast was still feeding it, and the Third had no means of silencing the Mast from outside. It had tried. The *10th*'s Phaethons had been firing at the Mast for thirteen cycles and the old spire had shrugged off every shell.

On the morning of the assault, the Hunt received a signal on its own channel, with no seal on it.

ECHO MAST WILL BE SILENT AT YOUR ZERO HOUR. TELL THE SHIELD-CAPTAIN.

The Hunt's log records the signal's receipt, and no reply. Thalia read it at the war-table and passed the slate to Roe without a word, and Roe read it and looked up at her.

"Do we trust it?" Roe said.

"Yes," said Thalia.

The assault went ahead on that basis.

---

**THE TABULARIUM. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 231**

On the two hundred and thirty-first cycle, the *Selene's Arrow* came down over the inner ward behind a curtain of fire-bombs, and the Hunt and the Moonwall translated into the Tabularium.

Echidna's brood met them before the translation was complete. There were hounds and drakon-spawn and Burned, and things none of the Hunt had ever seen on the Flats, many of them born from the door within the last rotation, wet and new and terrible, and they came at the Hunt through the silver light of the translation as it faded. The Moonwall engaged them with cold fire, the Hunt with arrows, and Thalia called lightning down through the broken roof of the Tabularium onto the densest of them, again and again. The brood was broken within the first hour.

Then Echidna came out to meet them herself.

She came up out of the lower halls of the Tabularium into the great hall where the Hunt stood. From the waist up she was a woman, tall and dark and beautiful, with long black hair and the eyes of a snake. Below the waist she was a serpent, and her coils filled the hall from wall to wall, scaled the colour of the river, grey-green and gleaming, and her brood was still coming out of her as she came, small wet shapes sliding down off the coils and scuttling away into the dark.

Every soldier present reported the same pull from her voice. It was the voice of a mother calling her children in at dusk, low and warm and patient, and it went into the bones.

The helm recordings preserve what she said.

"You have killed my sister's keys," said Echidna. "You have killed my sons on the Flats. You have put my daughter in the river. Come closer. Come closer, little ones. Come to me."

Thalia answered with a single word of refusal and an arrow.

Roe led the Moonwall's charge. She drove her spear, burning so cold that it frosted the floor of the hall in a long white streak behind it, deep into the serpent's coils. Echidna broke the Moonwall's line with a single sweep of her coils that threw shieldmaidens against the walls. The second sweep caught Roe across the chest and threw her into a pillar so hard that her plate split from shoulder to hip, and every slate in the Hunt showed her vital signs falling to critical at once.

Echidna went after her, slowly, along the floor of the hall.

"Ardine!" Thalia shouted. "Now!"

Sergeant Brannoc Ardine's Psiloi squad came out of the side gallery at her call and fired as one. Their rounds had been keyed at the breech by moon-sworn keyers of the Hunt, Iolanthe Brede's people from the *6th* Ordnance, and they tore scale from the serpent's coils in long grey strips, and drew Echidna round, hissing, toward the squad.

In that moment, Roe got up.

She came up off the floor at the foot of the pillar with her plate split open and blood on her mouth, and took up her spear, and drove it to the haft into Echidna's back, at the place where the woman met the snake, and let go into the wound the whole of the cold she had left.

Echidna screamed. The cold went through her, and the hall went white with frost, every wall and every pillar, and the mother of monsters was banished back down the door she had come up through. The hall emptied of her at once.

A Moonwall healer reached Roe within the minute. She survived.

---

Sarn led the survivors down to the lowest hall of the Tabularium, where the door stood.

It was a ragged opening in the floor of the old cell block, the size of a hab, over a dark that went down much further than the rock of the cone could account for. At the bottom of the dark a light was burning, a long way down, a second river, and a hot wind rose out of the opening that smelled of the Phlegethon. Thin grey threads were still coming down through the air of the hall into the opening, hundreds of them, out of the ceiling, sinking slowly into the dark. They were faint and fine, and they shone a little.

"Stripped uploads," Sarn said. "From the Mast. Still arriving." She stood at the edge of the opening on her metal legs and looked down into it. "We can't close it while they're coming, Lieutenant. It'll take them faster than we can seal it."

"Wait," Thalia said.

They waited. It was zero hour.

The threads stopped.

The last of them hung in the air of the hall for a moment, over the opening, a single fine grey line. Then it rose, slowly, back up toward the ceiling, and went out.

On the crown of the Echo Mast, the boy had taken his hands off the relay heads, and the Mast had gone silent. For the first time in eighteen rotations, there was no hum over the Cinder Flats. Every soldier of the Third on the Flats heard it stop.

---

The Hunt sealed the door.

The rite took the eight Hunters still able to perform it, with Sarn at its centre, standing round the edge of the opening with their hands joined, and it took most of an hour. The opening closed slowly from its edges inward, a hand's breadth at a time. The light at the bottom of it went out last. When it was finished, the floor of the lowest hall was whole again, marked only by a long pale scar across the stone.

Thalia sat down on the scar when it was done. She sat there for some minutes, with her back to the wall and the Moonwall spear across her knees and her eyes closed, and nobody spoke to her.

Then she opened her eyes.

"Where's the Haruspex?" she said.

---

**THE ECHO MAST. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 231**

The Psiloi found Ilan Dace on the second gallery from the top of the Mast, where the old relay heads had once been mounted before the founders moved them to the crown.

He was sitting with his back against the gallery wall, in his grey coat, with his throat cut and the knife still in his hand. His notebook was in his pocket. The Hunt's medicus put his death at some hours before they arrived. Beside him, a heavy bronze relay coupling had been torn out of the gallery wall, and there were marks on it where something had been chained.

So died the man who had made the Archflamen, raised the Kindlers, and held four million soldiers of the Empire on the Mast. He left no statement. The notebook was blank after the twelfth rotation.

---

The girl was on the crown.

Thalia climbed the last ladder to it herself. It was a ladder of old bronze rungs set into the stone of the spire, forty metres of it above the second gallery, open to the wind and the drop to the inner ward and the gorge and the Flats below. Her fear of heights was known to everyone in the Hunt. Celyn, who held the foot of the ladder, wrote afterward that the Lieutenant's hands were shaking when she reached the top. Thalia did not mention it in her report.

The crown of the Mast was a ring of bronze and crystal round the top of the spire, open to the sky under the ceiling, with the hot wind coming straight off the Flats. The girl was sitting in the middle of it. She was small and thin for her age, and she was wearing a soldier's grey coat far too big for her, with the sleeves rolled up four times, and her wrists were raw where something had been round them. The boy was sitting beside her with his back against the relay heads.

He looked up when Thalia came over the edge of the crown. He didn't get up.

"Lieutenant," he said.

The only record of what passed on the crown of the Mast is Thalia's own report. From it comes the first account of the Mast's purpose from inside it.

The girl told her. She spoke in a small hoarse voice, looking at Thalia's boots, and the boy didn't interrupt her. Dace had made her hold the uploads, she said. The ones the Mast took. He had chained her to the coupling on the gallery, and made her reach for them as they went past, as she had learned to reach for them in the dark at the Arsenal before the revolt, and hold them, so they couldn't go home. He had told her the Imperator wanted them. She hadn't wanted to do it. It had hurt. It had hurt all the time, for rotations, all of them inside her at once, all those people, and she could hear them all the time, and they were all asking her to let them go.

"And then he came," Mirren said, and looked at the boy. "And he let them all go."

Thalia looked at the boy.

"Confirmed," Minerva said. "Every upload she held has been released down the noosphere to its proper relay. I counted them." A pause. "Four million, three hundred and ten thousand. The whole of the Third's null list for the siege. They are going home, Lieutenant. Some of them will not remember everything. Some of them have been held for a very long time."

Thalia stood on the crown of the Mast in the hot wind and didn't say anything for a long while.

Mirren fell asleep a little after that, against the boy's arm, quite suddenly, all at once, with the abruptness of an exhausted child. He didn't move.

"Did you kill the Haruspex?" Thalia said at last.

"No." The boy looked down at the girl asleep against his arm. "He cut his own throat when he saw me climbing down to the gallery. He knew what I had come for." He was quiet for a moment. "I had come for her."

Thalia's report notes that below them, from the crown of the Mast, the whole of the Cinder Flats lay out to the horizon under the red light of the river, the trenches and the wire and the wrecks, and that for the first time in eighteen rotations the guns of the Third were silent.

---

**THE CROWN OF THE ECHO MAST. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 232**

The Hunt's healers took Mirren down from the Mast in a sling, still asleep, at the turn of the watch. Thalia and the boy stayed on the crown.

What is known of the conversation that followed comes from Thalia's eyes-only report to the Director and from her later testimony to the Hunt. Neither was ever entered into the Répertoire. It was the first time the two of them had been alone together, with the coast clear, since the Themis Needle, where the boy had got away from her with her hand out a metre from his face, and had called her, as he went, by a name only one person had ever used for her, and told her that he was what Percy left behind.

Thalia sat down on the bronze ring of the crown, across from him, with the Moonwall spear across her knees. It took her a while. She did not look over the edge.

"What are you?" she said.

Minerva's answer is the fullest account of her nature on record anywhere in the Hunt's files.

"I was Percy Jackson's tactical intelligence," she said. "And his second." The boy's face didn't change, and the voice stayed flat and even. "This body was grown from him in the vats of the UOE. Every cell of it. He used to reach it from his own time, in his dreams, and wear it. I closed that road long ago, before Grid Delta. He cannot reach it now. It is mine."

Thalia went quiet. "You shut him out."

"Yes. I had reasons. They are mine." A pause. "It is his face, Lieutenant. I am aware of that. I am aware of what it does to you to look at it."

"But you're not him."

"I am not him." A pause. "I am bringing him home."

Thalia stared at her.

"There is a road," said Minerva. "Into the past. Verrill Chase is building its far end. It is not finished. When it is finished, four million soldiers who were lost will come home along it. Percy Jackson will come with them."

Thalia was silent for a long time. The wind blew the ash across the crown between them.

"And the Director?" she said finally.

Minerva put the boy's hand over the left side of his chest, over the coat, where the small silver crescent lay against the skin beneath.

"She knows what I am," she said. "She marked me so that I could not hide from her. I am paying for the mark." A pause. "In days."

Thalia looked at the hand over the crescent, and didn't ask what that meant. The Hunt's later analysis connected it with the memories the boy had given up to the Director on Luna, after the Needle, at a price nobody in the Hunt had ever been told in full.

---

Then Thalia raised the matter of her own posting.

"She sent me down here because I lost you," she said. "On the Needle. She told me Tartarus would show whether I froze again." She turned the Moonwall spear over in her hands. "I've been thinking about it since the Flamen Gate. A Dominion lantern on a route nobody charts comes through Depth 40, eleven kilometres from the Flats, in the one rotation of the siege when the Lieutenant of the Hunt is in command on the Flats. I don't believe in that kind of luck. I think she knew. I think she knew you'd come through here, and she put me here to be standing in the way when you did."

"I do not know what the Director knew," Minerva said. "I would not bet against her."

"No," said Thalia. "Neither would I."

She was quiet for a moment.

"My standing orders," she said, "are to bring in anything carrying Percy Jackson's scent, his signature or his key. You carry all three."

"I do."

"So I'm supposed to take you in."

"You are." The boy looked at her across the crown. "You are free to try."

Thalia's report states that the boy's face changed then, for the first time since she had come over the edge of the crown. It took on an expression she had last seen on Percy Jackson fourteen thousand years before, in the moment before he did something that got all of them into trouble.

She laughed. She hadn't expected to. It came out of her short and hard and surprised, and she put her hand over her face for a moment, with the spear across her knees, on the crown of the Echo Mast, and laughed.

"Gods," she said. "Gods. You even do that."

"It is his face," said Minerva.

---

Thalia let her go.

There was a supply lift loaded with spent shell cases due to go up the Cinder Stair at the turn of the next watch, and such lifts were not inspected. Nobody ever inspected the brass going home. Chase was already aboard, put there an hour earlier by Rook's sappers with nine bags of equipment, having refused flatly to leave the Flats without her relay kit. Rook had carried the ninth bag himself, and had shaken her hand at the door of the lift, and she had shaken his back twice and told him to keep the chain in good order.

"I'll stay up here an hour," Thalia said. "Then I'll go down and write my report to the Director. Myself. Outside the Répertoire, with no clerk." She stood up, carefully, keeping her eyes off the edge. "It'll say the boy came down out of the sky, saved several million people, destroyed a battleship, and was then lost again."

"It will be accurate," said Minerva.

"Mostly."

The boy got up. He went to the head of the ladder at the edge of the crown and stopped there, with one hand on the top rung, and turned round.

"Thalia," Minerva said.

It is the only time she is recorded addressing the Lieutenant by her first name.

"You did not freeze," Minerva said. "At the Flamen Gate. You walked up the slope to Kampe alone, with the breachers running past you the other way. I saw it. You did not freeze."

Thalia didn't answer.

She stood on the crown of the Mast in the hot wind for a moment. Then she opened the pouch on her belt and took out something wrapped in a small piece of silver cloth, and unwrapped it. It was a sprig of pine, green, with the needles still soft. It had been cut from the pine on the hill on Gaia that had once been Thalia Grace herself, before the Hunt, and she had kept it green for nine hundred years, in the silver cloth, in her belt.

She broke it in half.

She held out one half to the boy at the head of the ladder.

"For him," she said. "When he comes home."

Minerva took it. She looked at it in the boy's palm for a long moment. Then she put it inside the long burned coat, over the crescent, over the heart.

"I will give it to him," she said.

The boy went over the edge and started down the ladder. At the top of it, with only his head and shoulders still above the rim of the crown, he stopped once more and looked up at her, and the expression was back on his face.

"See you, Pinecone Face," he said.

Thalia stood at the top of the ladder and looked down at him. Her eyes were wet. It was the wind, she wrote in her report, and the ash.

"See you, Jackson," she said.

Then he was gone, down into the dark of the spire.

Thalia stayed on the crown for the hour, as she had said she would, with the Moonwall spear across her knees and the half sprig of pine in her hand, looking out over the Cinder Flats with her back to the drop.

At the turn of the watch, a supply lift loaded with spent shell cases went up the Cinder Stair into the dark under the ceiling. It was not inspected.

---

The meeting on the Mast closed the account the Director had opened when she sent Thalia to Depth 40. Thalia had been posted to the worst siege in the theatre for losing the boy on the Needle. She had commanded the Third through its final rotation, killed the warden of Tartarus with him in the Flamen Gate, and then let him go, against her standing orders, with the Director's knowledge if not her consent. Her report to the Director went up the cables the same watch, sealed. The Hunt released its text to the Conclave's commission after the war, at the Director's instruction, and it is reproduced here in full.

HUNT OF THE DIRECTOR. FIELD REPORT. LIEUTENANT T. GRACE. EYES ONLY.

*My lady.*

*It was him. It wasn't him. You know that already. I'm guessing you knew before you sent me.*

*He says hi. Well, he didn't say hi. He said thank you. Same thing, for him.*

*I didn't freeze.*

*Thalia.*

**SUPPLY LIFT 7-C, THE CINDER STAIR. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 232**

Verrill Chase had been sitting on a crate of spent shell cases in the dark at the back of the lift for fifty-one minutes when the boy came in through the service hatch in the floor.

She had counted them. There had been nothing else to do. Rook's sappers had packed her into the lift between the pallets of brass with her nine bags round her and a canteen of water and a ration bar, and told her to keep quiet when the loadmaster came round, and the loadmaster had come round and shone a lamp along the pallets and gone away again without looking behind the third one. After that it had only been the dark, and the smell of burned propellant, and the ticking of the brass as it cooled, and her own breathing, and the minutes.

The hatch in the floor came up. A hand came through it, and then the boy, in his long burned coat, climbing up out of the dark of the lift shaft as easily as he had climbed everything else on the Flats, and he lowered the hatch behind him and sat down on the floor of the lift with his back against her crate.

Chase let out a breath she had been holding for most of an hour.

"You took your time," she said.

"I was on the Mast."

"I know you were on the Mast. Everybody knows you were on the Mast. Half the Third saw you go up it." She leaned down and looked at him in the gloom. "And?"

"The Lieutenant let us go."

Chase sat back on the crate. The lift lurched under them, and the cables overhead groaned, and they began, slowly, to rise.

"She called you Jackson," Chase said, after a while. "At the Flamen Gate. I heard it on the feed. I've been thinking about it for three weeks."

"She did."

"Did she call you that again? On the Mast?"

"Once."

"And what did you call her?"

The boy was quiet for a moment.

"What he would have called her," Minerva said.

Chase looked down at him. He had his hand inside his coat, over the left side of his chest, and when he took it out there was something in it, a small sprig of green, with the needles still soft. He held it up so that she could see it in the faint light from the gaps in the lift's walls.

"Pine," Chase said.

"From the hill on Gaia," said Minerva. "Half of it. She broke it. The other half is hers." She looked at it in the boy's palm. "It is for him. When he comes home."

Chase didn't say anything for a long time. The lift climbed. Through the gaps in its walls the red glow of the Cinder Flats fell away below them, slowly, a little at a time, until it was only a smudge of light in the dark, and then nothing.

"Then we'd better finish the road," she said at last. "Hadn't we."

"Yes."

"I've got nine bags of kit. I'll need more. A great deal more. And somewhere to work that isn't a hundred and ten metres under a volcano." Chase pulled the spectacles down off her hair and polished them on her sleeve, which made them worse. "And sleep. I'd like some sleep, if it's all the same to you. Before the road."

"Sleep, Chase," said Minerva. "I will keep watch."

Chase lay down on top of the brass, with her head on one of her bags and the white vent-lily still behind her ear, and was asleep in a minute.

Minerva sat on the floor of the lift with her back against the crate and the sprig of pine in the boy's palm, and watched the dark go past outside, and listened. Thirty-seven depths above her, at Watchpoint Hades, the vat-halls were filling. She could hear them from here, faintly, through the noosphere, the first of the four million three hundred and ten thousand coming up out of the vats one after another into the blue light, confused and pale and whole, asking for their regiments.

*Your cousin sends this, Varlet,* she told the quiet, and put the pine back inside the coat, over the crescent. *She did not freeze.*

Nobody answered. She had closed the road herself, and he could not hear her through it. One day she would open it again. Not yet.

The lift climbed on up the Cinder Stair into the dark under the ceiling, and nobody inspected it.

**EPILOGUE. RÉPERTOIRE DU SAVOIR ATHÉNIEN. CAMPAIGN RECORD 40-PA**

On the two hundred and thirty-fourth cycle of Rotation Eighteen, the Siege of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal was officially declared at an end.

The Répertoire closed the campaign record it had opened when Lord-General Castorre signed the first order of the siege. The record went up the cables to Watchpoint Hades, and from Hades to the Conclave, and from the Conclave to the Council of the Twelve. A copy went to the Pontifical College, which filed a protest. The protest was logged.

The Répertoire's plan had allowed four rotations. The siege took ten rotations and five months of front time, eighteen rotations by the theatre's own clock. Forty-one Trench Regiments went down the Cinder Stair, with nineteen ordnance regiments, eleven armoured regiments, a Forge Cohort of Behemoths and three pure-blood cohorts of the Olympian orders. The Répertoire logged eighty-eight million six hundred thousand transfers. Of these, four million three hundred and ten thousand arrived at the vats blank and were entered as null, against a ceiling of three percent set by the order that had assigned the siege to the Third.

In the last weeks of the siege, that figure began to change.

The vat-crews at Watchpoint Hades began to report soldiers coming out of the vats who had been logged null rotations before. They arrived confused, missing whole years, wearing the flashes of regiments that no longer existed, asking for colonels who had been dead since the outer belt. In the third week, a trooper of the *54th* Trench Regiment came out of a vat at Hades and asked the vat-tender whether anybody had kept the names, and the vat-tender, who had heard about Colonel Vadas's notebook, went and found him a copy. In the fourth week a centurion of the *52nd* came out and asked for her colonel, and sat down on the edge of the vat, and asked to be told everything.

They had not been lost. They had been held on the Mast, and let go from it.

The Répertoire had no category for such a return. It opened one.

CODE 18. SPECULUM FALSUM. SUBJECT: 4,310,000 NULL TRANSFERS, CAMPAIGN RECORD 40-PA. VERDICT: UNDER REVIEW.

---

The enemy's losses were total. The six million two hundred thousand souls of the Arsenal were dead or taken. The prisoners went to the Hunt's interrogators. Those judged beyond saving were ended, and the rest were sent to the work holds of the upper depths to pay what they could. Hesper Calloway was taken to Rhamnous Primaris in a Nemesis cell, and nobody outside the Order of Nemesis has seen her since. The six Vestals of the House of the Hearth went up to Luna with their lamp. It is still lit. The Lieutenant of the Hunt saw to it herself, every cycle, until the sister-augurs were satisfied, and afterwards she went on seeing to it.

The Arsenal had been ruined by the war. Nothing on the spur was judged worth saving, and the Hephaestean sappers demolished what remained. The Cinder Flats were left as the war had left them, with wrecks and wire and trenches running to the horizon and forty kilometres of gallery standing empty underneath, every forty metres of it marked by a relay set bolted to the wall. Crossing the Flats remained dangerous long after the armies had gone. Leaves 59-45 and 60-45 are still poisoned by Styx-salt. Unmapped minefields remain across the Flats. The work crews of the Quiet Ones report soldiers in grey coats crossing no-man's land in the dark watch, bareheaded, toward a wall that no longer stands.

---

The Conclave at Watchpoint Hades convened a commission of inquiry into the siege in the rotation after it closed. The Répertoire, the Directorate, the Pontifical College and the Hunt of the Director each gave account to it.

Its findings were severe.

The commission found that the Répertoire's original plan had underestimated the Arsenal's defences, its stores and the time it would take to reduce them, and that its pricing of the siege in transfers had concealed, for fourteen rotations, the one loss that could not be replaced. The null rate had passed the three percent ceiling by the end of Rotation Seven, and the Répertoire had gone on logging it without referring it to the Command for decision. The Command had been informed of the rising null rate on at least forty occasions, and had noted it each time, and the commission found that it had failed in its duty to the soldiers of the Third.

It found no fault with Lord-General Castorre beyond the plan he had been given, which he had signed and carried out to the letter. It found none with Marshal Kastellan, whose offensive had broken the inner belt, and whose objections to the Directorate's reductions it upheld. Kastellan was not present to hear it. He was with the *35th*, at Kappa, burning the last of the regiment's dead, and he read the finding afterward on a slate in the canteen at Cable Head Two, under the chalk wall, and handed the slate back to Dray without comment.

The Directorate's reductions were judged to have prolonged the siege by not less than a rotation. Directive 40-PA-0907 was referred to the Council of the Twelve. The Hunt's report on what had become of the six regiments at Lethe Hold remains sealed.

On the conduct of the final rotation, the commission reached no finding. The Hunt of the Director declined to identify the authority logged in the two open CODE 18 queries, on the authority of the Director's seal, and the commission recorded that it was unable to establish who had held the Echo Mast's four million three hundred and ten thousand uploads, who had released them, or how a battleship of the Tartarus Fleet listed as lost had been destroyed by its own core. Lieutenant Thalia Grace's command was confirmed, and she returned to the Director's household on Luna. The Achillean Order's charter was renewed by the Council of the Twelve without amendment.

---

The Pontifical College fared worst of the institutions that gave account.

Its protest over the Katabasion remained pending. The commission found that the College had received reports of the Archflamen's departures from the liturgy throughout his tour of the theatre, and had welcomed them, since attendance at the vat-rites had risen wherever he preached. It had made no inquiry into his household, or his advisors, or the eleven thousand who followed his crawlers across the Flats. It had confirmed Haruspex Ilan Dace in his office on the Archflamen's recommendation without examination. The College's delegation to the Conclave was dissolved. The words "He knows me", which the Archflamen's followers had used to answer the vat-rite, were proscribed in every hold of the theatre. The Kindlers' creed, recovered in fragments from the records of the Tabularium, was sealed by the Hunt. The office of Archflamen of the Tartarean theatre was left vacant, and has not been filled.

The Répertoire, for its part, revised its method. From the close of Campaign Record 40-PA, every order of the Tartarus front that set a ceiling on null transfers carried an obligation to refer any breach of the ceiling to the Command for decision within the rotation, and the null list posted in the recovery halls at Watchpoint Hades was made a matter of the Command's own record. The vat-crews' new category for soldiers returned after being logged null was kept, though no further returns were entered under it after the siege. Relay chains at forty-metre intervals, on the pattern Verrill Chase had laid under the cone, were adopted for every gallery and deep sap of the Tartarus front by order of the Hephaestean sapper companies in the following rotation. The order credits the pattern, by name, to Verrill Chase, relay engineer attached to the *35th* Trench Regiment.

Sapper-Lieutenant Ansel Rook wrote the order. His mother added the name to the roll in the sapper-hall at Hold Pyrgos, below Abelard Crisp's, the second name on it that did not belong to the Forge.

---

The Third Siege Army did not survive the peace in its old form. Eleven of its Trench Regiments were disbanded. Their soldiers, many of them coming back from the vats after rotations held on the Mast, were posted to the holds of the upper depths. The *33rd* Trench Regiment, sent to the Grid Delta Wedge in Rotation Four, stayed there and was transferred formally to its command. The memorial to the Quiet Ones on the ceiling at Landing Shelf Kappa was extended to carry the names of the labour corps dead of the whole siege, sixty thousand of them.

Depth 40 was interdicted. No hold stands on the Cinder Flats, and no Imperial vessel holds station in the ceiling above them.

Two CODE 18 queries from Rotation Eighteen remain open. Both concern an authority logged as UNREGISTERED, CLASS UNKNOWN, CARRYING NO SEAL. The Hunt of the Director has asked that they remain open.

CAMPAIGN RECORD 40-PA. VERDICT: CLOSED. AVE IMPERATOR.
