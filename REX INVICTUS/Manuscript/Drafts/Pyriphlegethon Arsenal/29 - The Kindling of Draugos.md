## Chapter 29: The Kindling of Draugos

*"Every engine has its limit. So does every soldier. Find it before the enemy does."*

Cradle-Lord Doros Varne, Forge Cohort Talos

**THE CASTING PITS, BELOW THE CINDER CONE. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 201**

The casting pits had not poured metal since the Big Push. The Third's gunners had found the great crucibles in Rotation Eleven and broken three of them in a single watch, and the Archflamen's founders had let the others go cold rather than feed them under shellfire, so that the long floor at the foot of the cone was dark now except for the pits themselves, where the old slag still glowed in the bottom of each one, a dull orange under a crust of black, and threw its light up onto the faces of the people standing around the rim.

There were a great many of them. Draugos had asked for witnesses, and the bands of the deep had sent them: Cinderborn in their furnace-plate, four deep along the eastern rim, the Kindlers in their brown robes on the high gantry above the pour-channels, the war-chiefs of the smaller bands with their champions around them, each little knot of warriors standing apart from the next, watching the others as much as they watched the pit. On the western rim stood the Slagborn of the Lower Bend, three thousand of them, the second most savage band the Phlegethon had sent up onto the Flats and the only one that had never once given ground to the Cinderborn in four hundred years of quarrels at the bend. Their tribune stood at the front of them with his arms folded and his great hammer leaning against his leg.

His name was Kharos. He was a head taller than Draugos and broader across the shoulders, and his skin had been burned and healed and burned again so many times in the river that it had gone grey and hard and shone in the slag-light with the dull gleam of old iron. He had killed eleven war-chiefs in single combat at the bend, and he had been waiting for this one for a long time.

Draugos came down the slag-ramp alone. He had taken off his furnace-plate and wore only a kilt of chain and the iron collar of the Red Furnace's war-priests, and his bare chest and arms were a map of four centuries of fire, whorls and ridges of scar laid one over the next until no original skin remained anywhere on him. He carried a short heavy blade in each hand, black, forged from the slag of the river, and he walked to the edge of the largest pit and stopped there and looked across it at Kharos, and the whole long floor went quiet.

"You've asked for command of the Slagborn," Kharos said. His voice was a deep rasp, the voice of a man who had breathed the river's fumes for most of his life. "You've asked in front of the bands. I can't refuse that."

"No," said Draugos.

"Then I'll tell you what I told the other eleven." Kharos picked up his hammer. "The Slagborn follow the strongest. Show me."

They fought on the rim of the pit, in the orange light, with the slag crust cracking under their feet and the heat rolling up out of the dark around them. Kharos swung first, the hammer coming around in a long flat arc that would have taken the head off a Laistrygon, and Draugos went under it and in close and opened the tribune's side to the ribs with one blade while the other turned the haft aside, and stepped back out again before the hammer could come round a second time. The cut smoked. Kharos looked down at it and laughed, a short hard bark, and came on.

It went on for a long time. The bands of the deep had seen a great many fights at the bend, and they watched this one in silence, with the attention of connoisseurs. Kharos was the stronger, and he knew it. Every blow he landed would have broken a lesser fighter in half, and twice he caught Draugos with the haft and knocked him sprawling on the crust, and the second time the war-priest rolled to the lip of the pit and hung there for a moment with one arm over the glow before he twisted back onto the rim and came up with both blades in his hands. Draugos was faster. He fought without hurry, without any sound at all, cutting and stepping away, cutting and stepping away, and the tribune's grey skin opened in a dozen places and closed again, smoking, and opened again.

Kharos began to tire before the end of the first watch. The bands saw it before he did. His swings came a fraction later, and went a fraction wider, and on the forty-first of them Draugos stepped inside the hammer and drove both blades into the tribune's chest to the hilts, and held them there, face to face, and spoke to him, quietly, something nobody on the rim could hear.

Then he put his foot against the tribune's knee and pushed him backwards into the pit.

The crust broke under Kharos with a sound of shattering glass, and the old slag beneath it took him, and he went down into the orange light with Draugos's blades still in his chest and did not come up. The heat of the pit flared once, high and white, and threw every shadow on the casting floor long across the stone, and then sank back to its dull glow.

Draugos stood on the rim with his empty hands at his sides and looked across the pit at the Slagborn.

Nobody moved for a long moment. Then the first of them, an old champion with half his face burned away, came forward to the western rim and knelt, and laid his weapon down in front of him on the crust, and after him the rest of them came forward in their hundreds and knelt in rows along the western rim until the whole of the Slagborn of the Lower Bend was kneeling there in the light of the pit, three thousand of them, with their weapons on the stone in front of them.

Up on the high gantry, the Ashmother of the Kindlers watched it with her hands folded in her sleeves.

**THE KINDLERS' GANTRY. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 204**

She had known Draugos since he was a boy at the bend. She had been old then.

The Kindlers kept the deep fire. They had kept it for longer than the Arsenal had stood, in the hottest stretch of the Phlegethon below the spur, where the river ran white and the rock of the banks had gone soft and glassy with the heat. They were the Red Furnace's priests, and the Red Furnace's war-priests were theirs to anoint, and they had anointed Draugos in the river four hundred years ago and watched him grow into the most terrible fighter the bend had ever produced. They had never trusted him. Nobody who knew him trusted him. The Ashmother had stopped expecting to be surprised by him a long time ago, and so she received him on the gantry three cycles after the duel with a kind of tired attention, sitting in her iron chair above the cold pour-channels with the other eight Kindlers standing behind her.

He had put his furnace-plate back on. He stood in front of her with his helm under his arm and the slag-light of the pits coming up between the gantry's slats onto his face.

"You've been at the riverbank," he said. "Since the fifth rotation. Working at the door."

"Since before the fifth rotation."

"You opened it a crack for the Archflamen. You brought a few things through." He tilted his head toward the cone above them. "He paraded them on the terraces and his people cheered. It changed nothing on the Flats. The Empire is still closing its ring."

"The door was small," the Ashmother said. "He asked for a small door."

"And you could open a larger one."

She looked at him for a while. Below the gantry the casting floor had emptied, and the Slagborn had gone back to their camp on the Lower Bend under their new war-chief's banner, and a single Cinderborn sentry walked the rim of the great pit where Kharos lay.

"We know how," she said at last. "We've always known how. What we lack is the price. A door of the size we'd need would cost tens of thousands of lives, Draugos, in the hour of its opening. Living ones, given to the river all together. The Archflamen won't pay it. He still believes his people love him, and he means to keep them."

"I'll pay it."

"Where would you find tens of thousands of lives to give?"

Draugos smiled. It was the first time any of the Kindlers had seen him smile, and the eight behind her chair shifted on the slats. "There are three million people on the Flats," he said, "and in the Arsenal. Workers. Prisoners. Soldiers who've stopped fighting. The Empire takes its dead home to its vats, Ashmother, and the river gets nothing. I'll give the river what the Empire keeps from it. As many as you need, all together, in the hour you choose." He paused. "And in return you'll swear to me. All nine of you. For the rest of this war."

"And what comes through the door?"

"You know what comes through."

She did. They all did. The Kindlers had spent a great part of the siege listening at the riverbank, down at the white bend, to what moved below the Phlegethon in the deep places of the pit, and the largest of the things they had heard down there had a name older than the river itself.

"Kampe," the Ashmother said.

"Kampe," said Draugos. "The warden of Tartarus. She held the Hundred-Handed for the Titans before Olympus had a throne in it. She's been chained down there since the gods won their war, and she's waited a long time for somebody to open a door large enough." He set his helm down on the gantry at her feet. "Open it, and I'll give her the Empire's army to eat. Swear to me."

The Ashmother looked down at the helm, and at the pit below, where the glow had sunk to the dull orange of old embers, and at the eight Kindlers who stood behind her chair waiting for her to speak. Then she stood up from the iron chair, slowly, with both hands on its arms, and knelt in front of him on the slats, and the eight behind her knelt with her.

**THE BLACK SHALE CAMP, THE LOWER FLATS. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 222**

The Brethren of the Black Shale were the oldest band of the deep on the Flats. They had come up out of the Phlegethon's furthest channels, where the river ran through beds of black stone that broke into sheets as thin and sharp as knives, and they wore that stone sewn over their leathers in overlapping plates that clicked when they moved. Their war-chief was an old woman called Sabra Ostrel, who had fought against the Cinderborn at the bend and beside them on the Flats and had refused, three times in three rotations, to put her Brethren under any other chief's banner.

Draugos sent a herald to her camp on the two hundred and twenty-first cycle with a simple message. Every band of the deep on the Flats would follow him now, or it would burn. The Slagborn had chosen. The Kindlers had chosen. The Brethren had until the next dark watch.

Sabra Ostrel sent the herald back with his tongue cut out.

Draugos came at the start of the dark watch, before the time he had given. He came with two hundred of his champions in their furnace-plate, through the slag-fields on the east side of the Brethren's camp, where the Brethren had never posted sentries, trusting that nothing could cross the slag-fields in the dark without being heard. The champions crossed them without being heard. They came into the camp among the war-chief's tents while her champions were sleeping, and they killed Sabra Ostrel in her bed, and every one of her champions in theirs, forty-two warriors of the Black Shale, in the space of a few minutes, and set the tents alight over them. By the time the rest of the Brethren had come running from the outer camp, it was over. The fight that followed was short. When it ended, the surviving Brethren laid down their stone-edged weapons in front of Draugos in the light of their burning tents.

The other bands of the deep came to him over the next six cycles, one after another, their war-chiefs crossing the Flats under truce to kneel to him on the rim of the great casting pit, with Kharos still lying at the bottom of it. The Drake-Riders of the Smoking Fen came. The Ashen Brotherhood came, and the Quarry-Folk of the Glass Bend, and the Bellows-Men. By the end of the two hundred and thirtieth cycle, every band of the deep that had come up onto the Flats had sworn to Draugos the Kindled.

With two exceptions. The Unremembered had never sworn to anyone, and Ysander Pale did not answer the herald Draugos sent to the Lethe Cut. The herald never reached him. Nobody found his body. And the Forsworn would not swear either.

**THE ACROPOLIS. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 238**

Hesper Calloway had known for a long time that the Archflamen was finished.

She had known it, in truth, since the Big Push. She had stood beside his chair at the Katabasion and listened to him talk about the love of his people and the patience of the river, and she had watched his face while he did it, and she had thought: he believes it. A Prefect of the Styx War, eleven hundred years in the deep, she had served a great many masters in her time and broken her oath to every one of them, and she had learned that the dangerous ones were the ones who believed. Sabinus Orrhae believed everything. He believed the Arsenal had freed itself. He believed the river had chosen him. He believed, even now, with the ring closing on the Wall and the Third's shells landing on the Acropolis every watch, that something would come up out of the deep at the last moment and save him, and he spoke of it every cycle at the long table under the niche of the First Descent, his eyes bright in the lamplight, while the stores ran down and the bands of the deep went over one by one to the war-priest at the casting pits.

He was a banner, now, and nothing else. His people still loved him. They still cheered when he came out onto the terraces. But the war was being fought from the Forsworn's command post in the old foundry offices, under Hesper's own hand, and she had been fighting it for most of a rotation without asking the Archflamen what he thought of any of it.

Draugos came to her alone. That surprised her, a little. He came up the long service stair from the casting floor to the foundry offices at the start of the dark watch without his champions, in his furnace-plate, with his helm under his arm, and her own guards let him in on her order. They stood across her map table from each other in the dim light of the foundry lamps. She had never been this close to him before. He smelled of the river.

"You know why I've come," he said.

"You've come to ask me to swear."

"No." He set his helm down on the map, on top of the Wall. "I know you won't. I wouldn't trust it if you did. You've sworn to everyone and kept faith with nobody, Prefect, and your Forsworn are the same, and I've no use for an oath from any of you." He looked at her. "I've come to ask you to stand aside."

Hesper said nothing. Behind her, at the far end of the room, her two guards stood still beside the door.

"I'm going to take the Acropolis," Draugos said. "The Archflamen and his household. His guard. All of it. And then I'm going to take his army, and fight this war to the last of the Empire's soldiers and the last of the Arsenal's people. I can do it without you. I'd rather do it without your Forsworn in my way. Stand aside, and keep your own command, and fight under your own banner until the end, and nobody will ask you to kneel."

"And Orrhae?"

"He's played his part."

She thought about it for a long time, standing at the map table, with the war-priest of the Red Furnace waiting on the other side of it. She thought about the long table at the Katabasion, and the Archflamen's bright eyes, and the stores that would last perhaps another rotation and a half. She thought about the Forsworn: four hundred of them still on the Flats, out of the thousand who had come up the river with her. Every one of them had broken an oath somewhere in the deep and come to her for that reason, and every one of them would go back into the river rather than be taken alive by the Hunt. She thought about the Hunt. She had seen the Lieutenant's crescent go up over the Third's lines in the fifteenth rotation, and she had known then, with the old cold certainty of eleven hundred years, that the war on the Flats would end in the Acropolis, and that there would be nobody left inside it at the end.

"The household guard," she said finally. "Some of them are mine already. Most of the rest are dead. My people have been quietly seeing to it for a rotation." She picked his helm up off the map and held it out to him across the table. "I'll open the apartments to you on the two hundred and fortieth cycle, at the second bell of the dark watch. What you do inside them is your affair."

Draugos took the helm. He looked at her for a moment longer, and then he inclined his head, briefly, the gesture of one war-chief to another, and went back down the service stair to the casting floor.

When he had gone, Hesper stood at the map table for a long time without moving. Then she sent for Ilan Dace.

**THE ARCHFLAMEN'S APARTMENTS. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 240**

Dace had spent most of the siege expecting to die, and it had never occurred to him that he might be warned.

He came to the foundry offices in his grey coat with his notebook in his pocket, at the end of the second watch, and Hesper told him in a dozen sentences, standing at the window with her back to him, looking out at the glow of the Flats. When she had finished, he stood in the middle of the room and stared at her.

"You're going to let him do it."

"I'm going to stand aside," Hesper said. "I've told him so, and I'll keep my word. You're the only one I've told. If you go to Orrhae, Dace, he'll call out his guard, and the guard will die, and he'll die anyway, an hour sooner. If you go to the girl, you might get her out." She turned round. "There's a service lift on the north face of the cone, below the old ore-tipple, that the Archflamen's people stopped using in Rotation Twelve. It comes down at the Lethe Cut road. My people won't be watching it."

"Why?"

She seemed to consider the question. "Because she can hear the uploads," she said finally. "The only one of you who ever learned to. And I've been down in the deep for eleven hundred years, Haruspex, and I've never once heard of anyone who could do that. I'd like to know what it means. I'd rather it didn't go into the river with the rest of them before somebody finds out."

He left her at a run.

He found Mirren in her room at the top of the old records stair, sitting on the floor with her back to the wall, listening, as she sat every dark watch now. She was twelve, or perhaps thirteen. Nobody had ever been sure. She looked up at him when he came in, and she knew from his face.

"They're coming," she said.

"Yes. Get your coat."

They went down the records stair with six of Dace's own guards, the last who had stayed with him, and along the gallery toward the north face, and they were halfway along it when the screaming began in the Archflamen's apartments above them.

Draugos's champions had come up the Forsworn's stair at the second bell, exactly on time, and the doors at the head of it had been standing open for them. The Archflamen's household guard had numbered three hundred at the start of the siege. Perhaps eighty were left that night, and of those eighty, thirty-one had already been killed in the past rotation by Forsworn who had served beside them, quietly, one or two at a time, in the dark watches, their bodies put into the river. The remaining forty-nine fought for the Archflamen at the doors of his apartments and in his long hall and at the foot of his bed, and the champions of the Cinderborn cut them down where they stood and went on through the rooms behind them, through the chapel and the library and the household's quarters, killing everybody they found who was not the Archflamen. They left a trail of it through the apartments that the Hunt's investigators would map, rotations later, room by room, from the bloodstains on the stone.

Dace heard it the whole way along the gallery. Mirren walked beside him with her hand in his and her face turned up toward the noise, listening, and once she stopped, and he had to pull her on.

"Nobody's going up," she whispered. "Ilan. None of them. Nobody's going anywhere."

"I know."

"They're just stopping."

"I know. Keep walking."

The service lift was where Hesper had said. It still worked, more or less. They rode it down the north face of the cone in the dark, the eight of them crammed into the old ore-cage with the wind howling through its bars and the glow of the Flats spread out below them, and at the bottom they stepped out onto the Lethe Cut road with the mist coming in across it from the south, cold and grey, and began to walk.

Dace looked back once, at the top of the cone, where the Archflamen's apartments were. A few of the windows were lit. Something was burning in one of them.

He did not look back again. The Unremembered found them on the second cycle, in the mist, and took them in.

---

Sabinus Orrhae was taken alive.

The champions found him in his long hall, kneeling in front of the little shrine he kept there to the First Descent, with his hands folded in front of him and his eyes closed, praying. He didn't resist. When they lifted him to his feet he looked at them with something close to curiosity, and when Draugos came in, in his furnace-plate, with the blood of the household guard still running in the grooves of it, the Archflamen looked at him too, and for a moment nobody spoke.

"The river sent you," Orrhae said.

"Yes," said Draugos.

The Archflamen nodded slowly. He had been expecting this answer for some time, perhaps.

He and the forty survivors of his household went down into the deepest cells beneath the cone that same watch, down a stair that had not been used since the founding of the Arsenal, to wait there in the dark with the prisoners who were already there, for the hour when the Kindlers would need them.

By the end of the two hundred and forty-first cycle of Rotation Sixteen, Draugos the Kindled was master of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal. Every band of the deep on the Flats was sworn to him, save two. The Archflamen's army, two and a half million strong, took its orders from him through the Archflamen's own officers, most of whom had no idea that anything had changed at the top. The people of the Arsenal still cheered the Archflamen's banner when it went up over the terraces, and on the casting floor at the foot of the cone, under the gantry where the Ashmother sat in her iron chair, the Kindlers had already begun building the door.

On the Flats, eleven kilometres to the south, the Third knew nothing about any of it.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN, FORWARD BUNKER. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 360**

The directive came in over the Directorate's own band, at the start of the second watch, addressed to the commander of the Third Siege Army with no reference anywhere in it to the Lieutenant of the Hunt.

Dray read it first. He read it standing at the signals desk with the slate in both hands, and then he read it again, and then he brought it across the bunker to the map table where Thalia was going over the First Line Phalanx's ammunition returns with Celyn, and put it down in front of her without a word.

DIRECTIVE 40-PA-0907. FROM THE DIONYSIAN LOGISTICS DIRECTORATE, CEILING OFFICE. TO THE COMMANDER, THIRD SIEGE ARMY, PYRIPHLEGETHON THEATRE. THE DIRECTORATE HAS REVIEWED THE REQUIREMENTS OF THE THEATRE AND FINDS THAT THE PRESENT STRENGTH OF THE THIRD SIEGE ARMY CAN NO LONGER BE SUSTAINED AND IS NO LONGER REQUIRED. YOU WILL WITHDRAW SIX REGIMENTS FROM THE LINE AND PREPARE THEM FOR TRANSPORT TO LETHE HOLD, WHERE THEY WILL COME UNDER THE COMMAND OF COMMANDER THALEIA. SUPPLY AND TRANSPORT TO THE THEATRE WILL BE REDUCED ACCORDINGLY. THE REDUCTION OF THE ARSENAL IS DOWNGRADED IN PRIORITY. THE DIRECTORATE CALCULATES THAT THE REGIMENTS REMAINING WILL SUFFICE TO COMPLETE THE SIEGE AT ACCEPTABLE ADDITIONAL COST IN TIME AND TRANSFERS. ANNEX FOLLOWS.

The annex named the regiments. Thalia read down the list once and turned the slate face down on the map.

"The First," Celyn said. She had read it upside down. "All three of its regiments. And the Phaethons."

"And the *9th* Assault, and the *20th* Armoured, and half the independent batteries." Thalia sat back. Her face hadn't changed at all, and Dray, who had served beside her for two rotations now, had learned that this was a bad sign. "Iason. Who signed it?"

"The ceiling office. Under the Directorate's seal. There's no countersignature from the Hunt, Lieutenant, and none from Luna."

"No," Thalia said. "There wouldn't be."

They knew what it was. Everybody in the bunker knew what it was, within the hour, and nobody said it aloud until Celyn did, late in the watch, standing at the map with her arms folded. The College had lost in the Conclave. It had wanted the Third, and the Director had given the Third to the Hunt instead, and it had been working ever since, quietly, in the galleries on the ceiling worlds where the Empire's real business was done, to take back from the Hunt by other means what it had failed to take by vote. Six regiments for Lethe Hold, where the Unremembered had been raiding the vat-lines and Commander Thaleia had been asking for help for two rotations. Six regiments to fight the same enemy, a little further up the river. The Hunt's staff assumed, without any proof at all, that the College had promised the Directorate's masters its votes on some other matter in return, and nobody in the bunker thought the assumption unfair.

"The Directorate can't do this," Celyn said. "The Third's under the crescent. The Director's seal is on the transfer order. They can't reach past it."

"They haven't reached past it," Thalia said. "Read it again. The Third stays under the crescent. Nobody's taken anything from the Hunt. They've just moved six regiments from one front against the Archflamen's allies to another, and reduced the supply to match, and if we object, we're objecting to Commander Thaleia getting the help she's been asking for since Rotation Fourteen." She turned the slate over and looked at the annex again. "It's well done. Whoever drafted it knew what they were doing."

"So we obey it."

"So we answer it." Thalia stood up. "I want two officers of the Psiloi and a clerk of the Hunt on the first car up the cables in the morning. They'll carry the Director's seal and my letter. They're to go past the Directorate entirely, straight to Luna, and put the case to the Director in person, and through her, if she thinks it necessary, to the Council of the Twelve. The Directorate supplies the Third and moves it. It doesn't command it. It's gone over the Hunt's head without a word to the Lieutenant, and I want the Director to know that the Lieutenant noticed."

---

They went up the cables at first bell. Lochagos Ione Avery of the Psiloi, who had run the Hunt's courier lines on the Flats since the crescent went up; her second, a quiet young Psilos called Teucer Lisle; and a clerk of the Hunt's own chancery, Phaedra Quince, who had come down from Luna with the Director's people in the fifteenth rotation and knew the forms of the Council better than anyone else on the Flats. Thalia saw them off herself at Cable Head One, in the brown light, with the ash coming down. Quince had the sealed case on a chain around her wrist.

"It won't be quick," Avery said, at the door of the car. "There'll be a queue."

"There's always a queue."

"I mean, Lieutenant, it'll be weeks. Every petitioner in the Empire waits in the galleries outside the Director's hall. She hears all of them in the end, she's never refused one, but she hears them in order." Avery hesitated. "Unless we tell them who we are."

"You'll tell them who you are," Thalia said, "and you'll wait your turn."

So they waited. Avery sent a short message down the cables every few cycles, in the Hunt's courier cipher, to say where they were in the line. The galleries outside the Director's audience hall on Luna were long and silver and cold, and full of petitioners from every front of the Empire, soldiers and governors and ceiling-world envoys, all of them with business they believed could not wait. The Hunt's people sat among them on the long benches for nineteen cycles.

In the meantime, there was a war to fight, and Thalia fought it.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SEVENTEEN, CYCLE 61**

The answer came on the sixty-first cycle of Rotation Seventeen, in the Director's own hand, and it was unfavourable.

Thalia read it alone, in the small room behind the map table where she slept when she slept at all, and then she sat for a long time with the slate on her knee.

The Director had heard the envoys. She had taken the case to the master of the Dionysian Logistics Directorate in person, and the master had taken it to his patron, and the patron had backed him. In the Council of the Twelve, the Lord of the College had backed them both. It was a strong alliance, and the Director could not break it alone. The Forge had abstained. The Pyrrhus Command had abstained. Others had said nothing at all. The directive would stand, softened in a few particulars in the Hunt's favour that the Director listed at the end of her letter. The *20th* Armoured would stay on the Flats, transferred to the *7th* Line Phalanx in place of the First. The reduction in supply would be smaller than the Directorate had asked for. The remaining five regiments and the Phaethons would go.

*I have done what I can,* the Director had written at the bottom, *and it was not enough. I will not tell you what to do with this. You are my Lieutenant. You know what I would do.*

Thalia knew what she could do. She sat on the edge of her cot with the slate on her knee and thought about it with great clarity, for most of a watch.

She could refuse. She could hold the Flats with the Third behind her and dare the Directorate to come down the cables and take its regiments, and the Third would stand with her. She had no doubt of that. Kastellan's soldiers would stand with her, and Saldane's, and the Hephaestean sappers, and the Forge Cohort, whose Cradle-Lord had once told her that the *Anvil of Talos* took no orders from anyone who had never been to the bottom of the pit. With the Third behind her she might hold out against arrest for long enough to take the Arsenal. It might even be the right thing to do, for the Third and for the siege and for the millions who would transfer in the extra rotations the directive would add.

It would be declared a revolt within the cycle. Her name would go onto a CODE 31 in the Répertoire, beside the names of the others who had taken that road, and Enforcement would come down the cables after her, and there would be a second war on the Flats, the Empire against the Empire, with the Archflamen's people watching from the Wall. She had seen others go that way. She had watched several of them do it, over the years, commanders who believed they understood the Empire's needs better than the Council of the Twelve, and she had watched what happened to them and to the soldiers who followed them.

She thought about the Director, alone in the Council, with nobody beside her.

At the end of the watch Thalia went out into the bunker, where Dray was waiting at the map table with Celyn and the duty staff, and signed the orders for the six regiments to withdraw. Nobody spoke while she did it. When she had finished she put the stylus down beside the slate and looked up at Celyn.

"You disagree," she said.

"Yes, Lieutenant."

"Good. Write it down. Send it to Luna with my report, under your own name." Thalia picked up the First Line Phalanx's ammunition returns again. "And then get me a plan for the Ore Gate that doesn't need the First."

**CABLE HEAD ONE. ROTATION SEVENTEEN, CYCLE 90**

The First Line Phalanx's three remaining regiments left the Flats on the ninetieth cycle, with the three Phaethon regiments of the *10th* Ordnance Phalanx and the *9th* Assault Phalanx behind them, and half the independent batteries that had stood behind every offensive the Third had made since the Big Push.

It took the whole cycle. The cable cars went up full and came down empty, one after another, all day, in a long grinding procession up into the brown murk overhead, and the soldiers of the regiments that were staying lined the road to Cable Head One and watched them go. Nobody cheered. Some of the First's soldiers looked back. Most of them didn't. The Phaethons went up last, dismantled into their component parts on flatbed cars, the great barrels lying in their cradles under tarpaulins with the ash settling on them, and Thalia stood on the platform at Cable Head One with Dray and watched the last of the cars climb away out of sight.

"That's a sixth of the infantry," Dray said quietly. "And the only guns on the Flats that can overload the Acropolis's wards from outside the range of its lances."

"I know."

"Every plan we have for the final assault assumes them, Lieutenant."

"Then we'll need new plans," Thalia said.

---

Avery, Lisle and Quince came back down the cables on the ninety-fourth cycle, on a supply car, in a compartment of their own at the back of it.

The car's crew found them at Cable Head Two, when the car came in. All three had been beheaded, cleanly, with a single stroke each, beneath the implants, and the implants had been taken with the heads, so that nothing had uploaded and nothing would. All three were null. The sealed case was still on its chain around what remained of Quince's wrist, unopened. Nothing else had been taken. Nobody on the car had seen anyone go into the compartment or come out of it, and the investigation that the Hunt opened that cycle never found their killers or put a name to them.

Thalia went to Cable Head Two herself, when Dray told her. She stood in the doorway of the compartment for a long time.

"It's a message," Dray said at last, from behind her.

"Yes."

"They've been watching you. Since the Conclave. They wanted to see what you'd do with the directive." He was silent for a moment. "And you signed it. So they're telling you that they saw, and that you passed."

"And what I'd have got if I hadn't," Thalia said.

She had the bodies sent up to Luna, in sealed caskets, with the Hunt's honours, and she wrote to the Director, and to Avery's mother in the Psiloi barracks on the moon, and to Lisle's, and to the chancery for Quince, who had nobody. Then she went back to Quarterdeck Nineteen and spoke of the matter to no one for a cycle.

At Lethe Hold, Commander Thaleia received the six regiments with more guns and tanks than her sector had held in a hundred years, and used them well. The Unremembered's raids on the vat-lines stopped within a rotation and never began again. The Directorate cited the result, in a circular to every front in the Empire, as proof that its decision had been sound. On the Flats, the Third's officers observed among themselves that the Unremembered had stopped raiding Lethe Hold only once Ysander Pale had drawn the last of his warbands down to the Cinder Flats, where the Third was now six regiments weaker to meet them.

**THE ORE GATE, LEAF 57-45. ROTATION SEVENTEEN, CYCLE 210**

The Ore Gate passed to the *2nd* Line Phalanx, and the *2nd* Line Phalanx gave it the *43rd* Trench Regiment.

Colonel Mara Thyssen had spent most of two months getting ready. She was fifty-one, a daughter of Hephaestus, short and square and patient, with the thick fingers and burn-scarred forearms of the forge-holds, and she had spent her first three rotations on the Flats as a sapper before the *43rd*'s old colonel went null on the Wall and the *2nd* Line Phalanx gave her the regiment. She planned the Ore Gate from the bottom up, with the method she'd been taught for a mine, and it took weeks to bring the guns forward along the shattered roads behind the hook and more weeks for the sappers to dig their saps to within a few hundred metres of the gate, under the eyes of the Wall, at a cost of nine hundred sappers who went onto the Deep List. On the two hundred and tenth cycle of Rotation Seventeen, the *43rd* was ready.

Provost-General Silas Wolde would lead it.

Wolde had commanded the Third's armour in every major offensive since the Big Push. He was a big untidy man with a grey beard and a voice that carried over a battlefield without any help from the band, and the tank crews loved him for going in with them, every time, in his Thureos at the front of the leading wave, and he had come back every time. His Thureos was the *Ash Walker*, a heavy assault tank of the old Forge pattern with a dozer blade on its nose and his own initials scratched into the glacis by his own hand thirty years before, and it had been knocked out under him four times on the Flats and recovered and rebuilt four times, so that by now there was not a great deal of the original tank left in it apart from the initials.

He found Thyssen at the forward command post an hour before the barrage, in the sap-head at the foot of Leaf 57-44, going over the gate's defences on a slate with her battalion commanders.

"Colonel." He crouched down beside her in the trench. "They tell me you used to be a sapper."

"Still am, Provost-General."

"Good. Then you'll understand me when I say I've never seen a gate like this one." He looked up over the parapet. "What's behind it?"

Thyssen showed him. Behind the Ore Gate, the Hunt's augurs reported, the Archflamen's army had massed tanks and guns and infantry in numbers it hadn't committed anywhere on the Wall in rotations, and the spider-legged walkers of the casting lines, the great heavy ones that the Third's crews called furnace-crabs, and something else as well, near the gate, in large numbers, that the augurs couldn't identify. They only knew that it burned hot.

"Cinderborn, maybe," Wolde said.

"The Hunt doesn't think so. The Hunt says the heat signatures are too large for the Cinderborn they've seen before." Thyssen put the slate down. "Provost-General, they're waiting for us. They've known we were coming for a month."

"They've known we were coming since the Big Push, Colonel," Wolde said cheerfully. "That's what a siege is." He stood up. "I'll see you on the other side of that gate."

---

The barrage came down at the first bell of the two hundred and tenth cycle, every gun the Third could still bring to bear on the Ore Gate, and behind it came the Behemoths.

There were two of them, both of the Steropes pattern, both of the Forge Cohort Talos: the *Hammer of Lemnos* and the *Bellows of the Forge*. They walked up out of the Third's lines behind the *43rd*'s breachers in the brown light, sixty metres tall, the ground shuddering under each step, and the missile racks on their shoulders opened as they came, salvo after salvo arcing up over the Third's lines and down onto the gate and the Wall around it, and the fire from the Wall came back at them, heavier and heavier the closer they came. The *Hammer*'s wards lit up gold and blue under the ceiling-lances from the towers on either side of the gate. The *Bellows* took a lance-strike on its shoulder that would have cut a lesser engine in half, and staggered, and came on.

The *43rd* went in behind them. Wolde's *Ash Walker* led the first wave of tanks with its dozer blade down, ploughing a road through the wire for the breachers behind it, his voice coming over the band to every crew in the first wave, steady and unhurried, a man directing traffic at a crossroads. Thyssen watched from the sap-head through a periscope. For the first twenty minutes it went well. The breachers reached the foot of the Wall beside the gate. The tanks reached the ditch.

Then the Ore Gate opened.

Its armoured doors rolled back, both of them, all at once, a hundred metres across, and the Archflamen's counter-attack came out of the gate in a single mass. Tanks and guns and infantry, the furnace-crabs stalking forward on their long jointed legs with flame-throwers spouting from their mandibles, and among them, at the front of them, the things the Hunt's augurs had seen burning hot behind the gate. Warriors of the Red Furnace, Cinderborn in their thousands, and in front of the Cinderborn, Draugos's own champions in their black furnace-plate, each of them a veteran of a hundred fights in the deep, and in front of them all, a single figure in plate that glowed dull red at the seams with the heat of the body inside it.

Thyssen had never seen Draugos before. Nobody in the Third had. She knew him at once.

---

The *Ash Walker* was knocked out in the first ten minutes of the counter-attack. A furnace-crab caught it at the edge of the ditch and hosed it with fire from twenty metres, and the flame found the engine deck and the ammunition, and the tank burned. Two of its crew got out. One of them was Wolde.

He was wounded. He'd been burned along one side of his body and a splinter had gone into his leg, and he came down off the burning tank and into the ditch at the foot of the Wall with his sidearm in his hand and his driver beside him, and the counter-attack rolled over the ditch and past him, driving the *43rd* back toward its own lines, and left him there.

Thyssen saw it through the periscope. She saw Wolde get up out of the ditch, on one leg, with his driver under his other arm, and start back toward the Third's lines across the ground the *43rd* had just lost. She saw the figure in the glowing plate turn away from the fighting and walk after him, without hurry, across the rubble.

Wolde turned round when he heard it coming. He let go of his driver and pushed the boy behind him and raised his sidearm and emptied it into the war-priest of the Red Furnace from five metres away, all twelve rounds. Draugos didn't stop walking. He came up to the Provost-General, who stood there with the empty sidearm in his hand, and he put one hand on Wolde's shoulder, almost gently, and with the other he drove a black blade through the Provost-General's chest and out through his back.

Thyssen saw the relay log the death a second later on the slate beside her. WOLDE, S., PROVOST-GENERAL. NULL.

Draugos pulled the blade out and let the body fall. Then he looked at the driver. His champions came up behind him and took the boy, alive, struggling, and carried him back toward the gate.

The Cinderborn took the soldiers they caught. By the seventeenth rotation, the soldiers of the Third had begun to learn what was done with them.

---

Wolde's death did not end the battle. Thyssen didn't let it.

She was on the band to the *2nd* Line Phalanx's batteries within a minute, and the guns came down on the counter-attack in the open ground in front of the gate, among the furnace-crabs and the Cinderborn and the Archflamen's tanks, and the *43rd*'s second wave went in. The counter-attack had driven the *43rd* back, but the Third had the numbers, and they began to tell. More tanks drove for the gate again, and the *Hammer of Lemnos* strode forward through the fire on its great clawed feet, its wards flaring and failing and flaring again, and levelled its forge-lance at the gate.

The forge-lance was a weapon of the Forge's oldest pattern, a single beam of molten light thicker than a tank, and the Behemoths of the Steropes pattern carried it in place of an arm. The *Hammer* held it on the Ore Gate for eleven seconds. When it stopped, the gate was gone. The armoured doors had melted and run down into the ditch in a slag-flow that glowed for three cycles afterward, and the parapet above them had collapsed, and the gate-towers on either side had been reduced to rubble by the guns and the tanks.

Over the rubble, the fighting swung back and forth for the rest of the cycle. The *43rd* rushed the remains of the gate with fixed bayonets and was thrown back by the Cinderborn, whose champions fought at the front with Draugos among them, and the Third's soldiers who saw him there said afterwards that he fought without a sound and that bullets seemed to make no difference to him at all. It went on through the cycle and the dark watch, with the shells of both sides falling among the combatants on the rubble, the Third's guns firing onto the gate and the Archflamen's guns on the Wall firing onto the same ground. For two more cycles after that, the *43rd* threw itself at the gate, and each time it was repulsed with heavy losses.

On the second of those cycles, the rents began to open in the air above the gate.

They were thin bright lines, a few metres long, that appeared out of nowhere over the rubble and hung there in the smoke for a few seconds, burning white, before they closed. The first of them opened at the start of the second watch. By the end of the cycle there had been forty. The soldiers of the *43rd* who were close to them said that the air beside them smelled of the deep river, and that for a few seconds, through each one, there was a sound of something breathing on the other side.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SEVENTEEN, CYCLE 214**

"They're deliberate," Sarn said.

The Hunt-Elder had come up from the augurs' station at Kappa in person, in the dark watch, to tell Thalia. She stood at the map table with the slate of recorded rents in her hands, the forty bright lines plotted over the Ore Gate in the order they had opened.

"Somebody inside the Wall is opening them. On purpose, Lieutenant. Testing." Sarn set the slate down. "These are small. The Kindlers opened the same kind of thing in the fifth rotation, on the riverbank, and brought a few creatures through, and that was the end of it. But these are being opened as tests. Somebody's making the way ready for something much larger."

"For what?"

"I don't know. I'd like to find out."

"Find out," Thalia said. "Whatever you need. Everybody the Hunt has on the Flats, if it comes to it. I want to know who's doing it and what they're making ready for."

It was Sarn's work over the next half rotation, in the end, that would uncover the whole story: the duel at the casting pits, the Kindlers' oath, the slaughter in the Black Shale camp, Hesper's bargain and the night in the Archflamen's apartments, and the door the Kindlers were building on the casting floor beneath the cone. That night on Quarterdeck Nineteen, she knew none of it.

The Hunt answered in other ways too. The Director sent a second company of the Moonwall down the cables, the *6th* Moonwall's Second, and on the three hundredth cycle of Rotation Seventeen Flame-Captain Dione Tharn of the Hunt took her place in the line on the Wall beside Roe and the *Selene's Arrow*. She was a tall dark woman with a burned hand who said little about where she had served before, and the first thing she did on the Flats was go down to the rubble of the Ore Gate and look at the place where the rents had opened.

---

The battle for the Ore Gate cost the *43rd* Trench Regiment two-thirds of its strength. Thyssen survived it. When the regiment came out of the line on the two hundred and nineteenth cycle she walked back along the sap with what was left of her headquarters, a third of the soldiers who had gone in, carrying Wolde's helmet, which a breacher had found in the rubble by the ditch. The gate remained in the Archflamen's hands, though only in name, since nothing of it was left standing. The Third held the rubble of the outer towers.

The death of Silas Wolde was felt across the Flats. He had commanded the Third's armour in every great offensive since the Big Push. He had always come back. Now he had gone null at the hands of a war-chief of the deep, in front of the whole *43rd*, and the tank crews of the Third took it as an omen and said so, and Thalia did not try to stop them saying it. The Répertoire recorded the battle as a reverse.

The Hunt recorded it differently.

It was at the Ore Gate that the Third first met Draugos face to face, and the Hunt's observers came away from the fighting convinced of something the Third's own officers had not yet noticed. The Archflamen no longer commanded his own army. The black furnace-plate of Draugos's champions had been seen at every point where the defence of the gate was strongest, and the Archflamen's banner, which had flown over every counter-attack the Arsenal had made since the start of the siege, had not been seen at all.

Thalia read the observers' reports in the dark watch of the two hundred and twentieth cycle, and sent them to Luna that night, with her own estimate attached at the bottom. *A change of command has taken place inside the Wall. I do not know how, or when. I believe the man who killed Wolde is now in command of the Archflamen's army, and that he is something other than the Archflamen's servant.*

She was right. It would be half a rotation before the Hunt could tell her how.

**UNDER THE CONE. ROTATION SEVENTEEN**

Beneath the fighting on the Wall, the sappers pushed on.

The war underground was as bitter and as slow as the war above it, and by the seventeenth rotation the galleries were approaching the cone from every side, east and west and north and south, at depths of thirty, sixty and a hundred metres below the floor of the Flats. The Frost Masons counter-mined. They had always counter-mined. But they lacked what the Hephaestean sappers had, the borers and the hundreds of tonnes of explosive and the supply of soldiers to dig that seemed to the Frost Masons to have no end, and they could no longer counter-mine fast enough to keep the galleries away from the undercroft.

So the Archflamen's people began to release creatures into the galleries instead.

Rook's company met the first of them on the eleventh cycle, on the north gallery, sixty metres down. They were drakon-spawn from the river channels, long pale things the length of a cable car, starved and blind, that came out of a side shaft without warning and went through the first section of the company before anybody could bring a flamer to bear. After them came other things, things that came up through the cracks in the rock from somewhere below the galleries and had no names at all in the Hunt's bestiaries, and after those came Laistrygons, giants who had been in the Grey until nothing was left in them but hunger, lowered down the shafts on ropes and cut loose in the dark, so that they wandered the galleries for cycles, eating whatever they found. The sappers fought them in the dark, half a kilometre from the nearest relay, in tunnels too narrow for more than two to stand abreast, and every sapper who died down there went onto the Deep List.

Aled Abery, who had taken Brin Hollis's place as the company's listener, died on the forty-third cycle at his listening post at the head of the north gallery, with his ear still to the rock. Nell Drover lost an arm to a drakon-spawn on the hundred and twelfth and was carried out to the relay by Osric Teague, who walked half a kilometre with her on his back in the dark, and she uploaded clean and woke at Kappa three cycles later with a new arm and asked to go back down. Ansel Rook's company lost a third of its strength in Rotation Seventeen, and almost all of it to creatures.

Thalia pressed the sapper commanders for progress. The battle around the Wall was consuming soldiers and tanks faster than any fighting she had seen, whole regiments were being destroyed in the attacks, and a road in from below might yet prove the best way into the Acropolis. Gault came up to Quarterdeck Nineteen on the hundred and ninetieth cycle with the sapper-prefects' figures and laid them on the map table in front of her.

"Half a rotation," he said. "At least. For a gallery under the undercroft. I can't promise sooner, Lieutenant. Not with what's coming up out of the cracks down there."

"Then the Wall has to go on," Thalia said.

"Yes."

"If only to keep them from moving people below to dig against you."

"Yes, Lieutenant." Gault picked up the figures again with his good hand. "I'm sorry."

"So am I," said Thalia.

---

By the end of Rotation Seventeen the reduction of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal had reached its last stage. The ring was closed on the Cinder Wall along its whole length. The Wall had been breached at the Ore Gate and in three other places. The sappers were within a few hundred metres of the undercroft of the cone. The Archflamen's army had lost the last of its ground outside the Wall and most of its stores, and the Hunt's estimate of its strength fell below two million for the first time in the siege.

Against that, the Third was weaker than at any point since the Big Push. It had lost six regiments and half its heaviest guns to the Directorate. Its armour had been worn down in the hook and on the Wall, and the man who had led it was null. The null rate, which had fallen during the Kastellan Offensive, was rising again as the fighting drew closer to the Echo Mast. The Répertoire's estimate for the assault on the Acropolis, published at the turn of the rotation, was the highest it had produced for any single operation of the siege.

Thalia signed it without comment and sent it up the cables to Luna.

The Director signed it in turn, and sent it back down without a word added.
