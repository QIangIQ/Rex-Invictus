## Chapter 20: Poisoned Cup

*"The Third will pay what it costs. Tell me the price, and then stop telling me."*

*Marshal Theron Kastellan, on taking command*

---

**WATCHPOINT HADES, THE COMMAND DECK. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 1**

Logothete Irene Vasska had audited forty-one commands in thirty years for the Dionysian Logistics Directorate, and she had learned in the first of them that the most dangerous moment of any audit was the moment you handed it in.

She handed this one in at the first bell of Rotation Nine, on the command deck of Watchpoint Hades, to the Lord Marshal of the Tartarean Theatre, in person.

The command deck was the highest point of Watchpoint Hades, a great round chamber at the top of the watchpoint with a floor of black glass, and in the glass, lit from beneath, was the whole of the theatre: every hold and every front from Depth 3 to the Cocytus, every army, every river, every Siege Army and Line Phalanx and Ordnance Phalanx and convoy, in thousands of small soft lights. The Lord Marshal stood at the centre of it. She commanded more armies than her staff could readily count and held a direct line to the Senate on Olympus Primaris, and she had stood at the centre of the black glass for nineteen years, and Vasska had met her twice before and had not much enjoyed either occasion.

"Logothete," the Lord Marshal said. "You've finished."

"I have, Lord Marshal."

"How long is it?"

"Four hundred and six leaves, Lord Marshal. With the tables."

"Give me the substance."

Vasska had expected that. She had written the substance on a single leaf, as she always did, and she gave it to the Lord Marshal now without referring to it, standing on the black glass above the small soft lights of the Cinder Flats.

"The *3rd* Siege Army was given four rotations to reduce the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal, Lord Marshal, and the shells for six. After eight, it stands further from the Acropolis than it did at the end of Rotation Three. It has transferred twenty-three million soldiers on the Flats, and lost six hundred and eighty thousand of them for good, and squandered shells, nectar and vat time in quantities the Directorate has never before placed at the disposal of a single siege." She paused. "Those are the figures. They are not the finding."

"Go on."

"Lord-General Castorre treated the null rate as a technical matter for the Répertoire's officers. He mentioned it to the Command only in the covering notes to his revised timetables. By Rotation Eight, more than one transfer in thirty from the Flats was arriving at Watchpoint Hades empty, above the ceiling the Command set at the start of the siege, and the Command learned of it from my audit. He sent two regiments to the Wedge at Grid Delta without protest and never pressed for the return of the *33rd*. He gave the Third no defence against attack from the air, on the assumption that the ceiling-lances which kept the Fleet from the Flats would keep the air clear for the Third as well, and when the drowned fleet rose the Third had nothing with which to answer it." She paused again. "And above all, Lord Marshal, his command went on planning the reduction of the Arsenal as a siege of forge-thralls and garrison cohorts for five rotations after the Legates of the deep rivers had come up to fight beside them."

The Lord Marshal said nothing for a long time. She was looking down at the black glass, at the small soft lights of the Flats.

"You don't accuse him of cowardice," she said at last.

"No, Lord Marshal. He isn't a coward. He's a careful, competent, honest officer who did exactly what he was told for eight rotations." Vasska had thought about how to say the next part for a long time, and in the end she said it exactly as she had written it. "I accuse him of the more ordinary failing, Lord Marshal. He kept to a plan after the war it was written for had ended."

The Lord Marshal looked up at her.

"And your recommendation?"

"That his command be replaced, Lord Marshal."

"Accepted," the Lord Marshal said.

---

Vasska went back to her quarters on the eleventh tier afterward and sat at her desk for an hour without doing anything at all.

She had been right. She knew she had been right. She had spent eighty cycles in the Third's returns, every one of them, every shell and every transfer and every null, and the finding was the only finding the figures allowed. She had written it carefully and without malice, and she had not overstated it by a word, and the Lord Marshal had accepted it, as Vasska had known she would.

And somewhere on Quarterdeck Nineteen, an old man who had done exactly what he was told for eight rotations was about to find out.

She thought about the Lethe holds in Rotation Three. Eleven thousand tonnes of shells on the books that weren't in the vaults. Two quartermasters sent to the Cocytus front. She had been right then too.

She got up and went to the window. Watchpoint Hades looked down from its tier over the vat halls, rank on rank of the great glass cylinders standing in their cradles under the pale lamps, thousands of them, most of them full. Somewhere down there, she knew, was a ward for soldiers who came back wrong, and a medicus who kept a list on his wall that had gone round the corner and along the next wall and round the next corner. She had read his study. It was in her report, on leaf two hundred and nine. It was the only part of the report that had made her stop working for a while.

She turned away from the window.

"Book me a cable to Quarterdeck Nineteen," she said to her clerk. "For the fifteenth. I want to see the returns at source before the new command arrives."

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN, THE COMMAND RING. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 18**

News of his dismissal made little impression on Lord-General Castorre.

Vasska saw him receive it. She had arrived on Quarterdeck Nineteen on the fifteenth cycle with three clerks and an empty case, and she had spent three cycles in the Third's records office with Leda Corvis, and on the eighteenth cycle, at the second bell of the morning watch, she was in the command ring when the signal came.

He received it at his terminal, standing. He read it once. Then he looked up from the terminal at the command ring, at the staff officers at their stations and the map table glowing at the centre and the long window looking down on the Flats, and he said, quite calmly, to nobody in particular:

"Well. That's that."

Then he turned off the terminal and went to his office and shut the door.

Vasska stood in the command ring and watched the door close. Nobody in the ring said anything. Nobody looked at her. After a while the staff officers turned back to their stations, and the work went on.

She found out afterward what she had suspected already. A post was waiting for him on the staff of the Second Ceiling Army on Callistus, then mustering against the reformation peaks at Depth 12. It had been arranged before the Lord Marshal accepted her finding, by somebody in his family, and it would be announced in the next list. A man of his house could not fall far. He would go up the cables in a few cycles to Callistus, and sit on a staff, and in a rotation or two his name would be in a list again, and nobody would remember the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal except as a place where he had served.

It was for those beneath him to bear the weight of the failure.

---

Vasska saw that too.

The lists came down from Watchpoint Hades on the nineteenth cycle. Every officer of the Third's command who had not already found a post elsewhere was named in them, and against each name was a posting. Most of the postings were to the Forlorn Cohorts or to field commands on the Cocytus front, where the ice took soldiers and did not return them. For most of the officers on the list it amounted to a death sentence, and on the Cocytus the phrase was meant literally.

Leda Corvis was posted to the Cocytus.

Vasska found her in the records office at the end of the cycle, sitting at the long table among the case files with the posting slate in front of her and her hands folded on top of it. She had been helping Vasska's clerks with the shell returns all cycle, and the returns were stacked neatly at her elbow, finished, every column added and checked.

"Chief of Staff," Vasska said.

Corvis looked up.

"Logothete." She glanced down at the slate. "You've seen it."

"Yes."

"I'm to go down the cables on the twentieth. To the Cocytus. Field command of a Forlorn Cohort on the ice at Depth 52." She smiled slightly. "I've never commanded anything in the field in my life, Logothete. I've been a staff officer for twenty-six years."

Vasska sat down on the other side of the table. She didn't know why. She had never sat down with anyone on a list before.

"I didn't recommend it," she said. "I want you to know that. My report recommended the replacement of the command. It said nothing about where the command's officers should go. That was the Lord Marshal's office."

"I know." Corvis looked at the stack of returns at her elbow. "It's in your report, Logothete. Leaf three hundred and eighty-one. I read it. You said that the Third's chief of staff kept the most complete returns of any command you've ever audited." She paused. "Thank you for that."

Vasska said nothing.

"I kept them," Corvis said, "because somebody had to. The Lord-General never asked for them. The Command never asked for them. I kept them because I wanted to know. Every transfer. Every null. Every shell. Every regiment and every cohort and every leaf." She looked at Vasska. "I knew about the null rate in Rotation Three, Logothete. I knew it was above the ceiling. I told him. I wrote it on every summary I gave him for five rotations, on its own at the bottom. He read every one of them. He signed every one of them."

"I know," Vasska said. "I found them."

"Then you know it wasn't that nobody told him."

"Yes."

They sat for a while in silence among the case files.

"There's something I want to give you," Corvis said at last. She reached down under the table and brought up a bundle of slates tied with a cord, and laid it on the table between them. "These aren't returns. They're things I wrote down that didn't fit anywhere else. The Cohort of Nemesis at the docks. The Forsworn. Hesper Calloway. The Mast. Ricci's list, and Tribune Wren's graph. The *54th*." She put her hand on the bundle. "And Segment Twelve. The Lord-General asked me to find out where they went. Marien Pyrrhus and the two ships. I didn't. I haven't had time. I've cross-referenced it all to a file that Archmagister Varros keeps, on paper, in his own quarters, that I'm not supposed to know about. He calls it RUSK." She took her hand away. "Give it to whoever comes next, Logothete. Please. Or give it to Varros. Somebody should have it."

Vasska looked at the bundle for a long time. Then she put her own hand on it.

"I'll see that the new command has it," she said.

"Thank you."

Corvis stood up. She picked up the posting slate and looked at it once more, and then put it in the pocket of her coat.

"I'd better pack," she said. "I don't suppose I'll need much, on the ice."

---

Corvis went down the cables on the twentieth cycle of Rotation Nine, at the first bell, from Landing Shelf Kappa, in a cable car full of other officers on the list.

Vasska went down to the shelf to see her go. She didn't know why. She told herself afterward that she had gone to check the cable manifests, and she did check them.

Castorre was there. Vasska hadn't expected that. He stood on the shelf in his greatcoat in the grey light, a little apart from the others, and when Corvis came along the shelf with her single bag he stepped forward and stopped her.

Vasska was too far away to hear what they said. She saw him speak, and Corvis listen. She saw him put his hand into the inside pocket of his greatcoat and take something out, a folded letter, old and creased, and hold it out. She saw Corvis look at it for a long time without taking it, and then shake her head, and say something, and push his hand gently back toward him. He put the letter back in his pocket.

Then he did something Vasska had never seen a Lord-General of the Empire do. He took off his cap, and stood bareheaded on the shelf, and saluted his chief of staff.

Corvis returned it. Then she picked up her bag and went into the cable car, and the doors closed, and the car went down the cable into the grey toward the Cocytus.

She was not heard of again by the Third.

---

**WATCHPOINT HADES. ROTATION NINE, CYCLES 18 TO 38**

With the Third's command replaced, a vacancy opened at its head, and there were powerful voices ready to fill it.

Vasska sat through every one of the meetings. She had no choice. She was the Directorate's voice at Watchpoint Hades for the Third, and the Directorate had made it clear to her, by courier, every cycle, that it expected her to be heard.

The Pontifical College was heard first. It had sent its own delegation down from Olympia Sacra, a Flamen-Prior and two Flamen-Inquisitors and their clerks, and they sat on one side of the long table in the council chamber below the command deck in their white and crimson and declared that the war had failed for lack of faith. The reduction of the Arsenal should be a crusade, they said, to free the Katabasion and the bones of the First Descent from the apostate. Orrhae had been one of the College's own. It was the College's forces that should bring him to justice. The College could send its temple legions to steady the line on the Flats and avert defeat, but only on condition that it named the Third's commander.

Vasska had read about the temple legions. The College kept eleven of them on the ceiling worlds, about four hundred thousand soldiers in all, recruited from the faithful of the holds and armed from the College's own foundries, and it had never once committed one to the Tartarus front. Their soldiers carried implants. Their devotion was beyond question. Their training and equipment were another matter, and the Lord Marshal's staff judged them fit to hold a quiet stretch of trench and little more. Their real value, Vasska thought, watching the Flamen-Prior across the table, was political. A College that had sent its legions to the Flats would have a stake in the outcome of the siege and a voice in its conduct, and the College intended to have both.

Enforcement backed the College. Its Lictor-Superiors sat beside the Flamens and proposed to place the whole of the Third under Enforcement's authority, with a Lictor at the head of every regiment. Vasska looked at them, at their black coats and their still faces, and thought about Pallas Quiro's report on the Tabularium, which she had read in the Third's archive, and the fifty-eight Lictors who had woken at Kappa after the Tabularium fell.

When it was her turn, she was brief.

"The Third depends on the Directorate's supply lines and the Directorate's stores," she said. "Every shell it fires and every soldier it puts back into a body comes through us. The Directorate will name the commander. If the Directorate cannot name the commander, the priority of the war will have to be reviewed." She looked down the table at the Flamen-Prior. "And whoever takes the Third without the Directorate's support may find that shells are scarcer than before, and that the vats at Hades have other fronts to fill."

The Flamen-Prior looked back at her with an expression of perfect courtesy and perfect hatred.

It went on for twenty cycles. The lobbying in the halls of Watchpoint Hades was ferocious. Vasska had never seen anything like it, in thirty years. The Third had become a prize that many wished to hold, and the soldiers on the Flats went on dying while the prize was argued over.

In the end the Lord Marshal settled it. She reserved the right to name the commander in the field, as commander of the theatre. She allowed the College and Enforcement to place their own officers on his staff and in his administration. Few were satisfied. The College in particular went on pressing its case, and went on withholding its temple legions until its influence should be sufficient to justify sending them.

And on the thirty-eighth cycle, the Lord Marshal named the man who would take up the poisoned cup.

---

**THE COMMAND DECK, WATCHPOINT HADES. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 38**

Marshal Theron Kastellan of Pyrrhus Command was seventy-nine years old, and he had been a soldier for sixty-one of them, and he stood on the black glass of the command deck in front of the Lord Marshal of the Tartarean Theatre with his cap under his arm and his back perfectly straight, and listened to her tell him that he was to take command of the *3rd* Siege Army, and did not show by so much as the flicker of an eyelid what he thought of it.

He thought it was a poisoned cup. He knew it was. He had read Vasska's report on the way down the cables from the Cocytus, all four hundred and six leaves of it, with the tables, and he had read the Répertoire's figures and the College's petitions and Enforcement's proposals, and he knew exactly what he was being given.

He had commanded the *63rd* Trench Regiment of the *2nd* Siege Army through the twelve-rotation siege of Hold Charybdis on the Cocytus, and it was his victory there that had recommended him for this. He had served for forty years under Stephanos Pyrrhus. He was a patrician, of a house with a long history in the upper ranks of the theatre, and he was a man of strict routine and fixed ideas about how a war should be fought, and he believed, privately, that the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal could still be taken, and that he was the man to take it.

He would never have said so aloud. It would have been vulgar.

"You'll have a staff that reflects the bargain, Marshal," the Lord Marshal said. "I won't pretend otherwise. Your chief of staff will be a Directorate officer seconded from the Logothete's inquiry. The College will place a Flamen-Inquisitor in your headquarters with authority over every chaplain on the Flats. Enforcement will place a Lictor-Superior with a cohort of Lictors and the right to try any soldier of the Third for heresy." She paused. "Neither of them will have any authority over the conduct of operations. Both of them will report to their own masters on Olympia Sacra by every courier."

"I understand, Lord Marshal."

"Do you?"

"Yes, Lord Marshal. I will treat them with every courtesy. And I will tell them nothing I have not already decided."

The Lord Marshal looked at him for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled.

"Stephanos said you'd say that."

She handed him his letter of appointment. Then she handed him a second letter, sealed, under the Pyrrhus seal, and he took it and knew from the weight of it in his hand what it was before he broke the seal.

"Read that one alone, Marshal," the Lord Marshal said quietly.

---

He read it in his quarters on the eleventh tier that night, alone, under his reading lamp, with his personal flamen saying the evening office in the next room.

It was in Stephanos Pyrrhus's own hand. He knew the hand. He had read a great many letters in it over forty years. It was short.

*Theron. You will have heard about Segment Twelve. They tell me she went in pulling soldiers out, and that the ships went in after her because Sector Command ruled they could not leave her, and that the Répertoire has struck them from the books. I will not strike her from mine. Find out, if you can, what became of my daughter. If she is dead, I want to know how. If she is not, I want to know where. You are the only man in the theatre I would ask. S.*

Kastellan read it three times. Then he folded it carefully and put it in the inside pocket of his tunic, over his heart, where he kept nothing else, and sat for a long time looking at the lamp.

In the next room, his flamen finished the evening office and began the prayer for the dead.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 41**

He took command on Quarterdeck Nineteen on the forty-first cycle of Rotation Nine.

Castorre was there to hand it over. Kastellan had not expected that either. The old man had stayed three cycles past his posting to do it, in his greatcoat, with his bag already packed and standing by the door of his office, and he met Kastellan in the command ring and walked him round it, station by station, and told him who everyone was and what they did, and then took him into his office and shut the door and sat down behind the desk for the last time.

"There's not much I can tell you that isn't in Vasska's report," Castorre said. "She was thorough. She was right, too, mostly. I want you to know I think so."

"Thank you, Lord-General."

"Not any more." Castorre smiled faintly. "Aurelian, if you like." He reached into the inside pocket of his greatcoat and took out two folded letters, old and creased, and laid them on the desk between them. "These are the only things I'm leaving you that aren't in the report. One is the letter the Archflamen sent me in Rotation Zero, before the first barrage. The other is from Callix of the Second, in Rotation Four, telling me the Saturation would fail. It did." He pushed them across the desk. "I carried them for five rotations. I'd look at them when I had to sign something. I don't know whether they helped."

Kastellan looked at the letters. He didn't touch them.

"Why are you giving them to me?"

"Because I can't give them to anyone else," Castorre said. "I tried to give one of them to my chief of staff, on the shelf at Kappa. She wouldn't take it. She said I should keep my own debts." He looked at the letters for a moment. "She was right. But I find I don't want to take them to Callistus."

Kastellan picked them up after a long moment. He put them in his tunic pocket, beside Stephanos Pyrrhus's letter.

"There's a bundle of slates," Castorre said. "Vasska has it. My chief of staff gave it to her. It's everything that didn't fit in the returns. Read it. All of it. Especially the part about Segment Twelve." He stood up, and picked up his cap from the desk, and looked round the office once. "I asked her to find out where they went. Marien Pyrrhus and the ships. She didn't have time."

Kastellan stood too.

"I'll find out," he said.

Castorre looked at him for a long moment, at the old straight back and the still face and the hand that had gone, without his seeming to notice it, to the pocket over his heart.

"Yes," he said. "I expect you will."

---

Kastellan's first orders on taking command were defensive.

All attacks along the arc were cancelled. The Ordnance Phalanxes were ordered to conserve their shells for counter-battery work and the breaking up of enemy attacks. And every regiment of the Third was required to report its nulls separately from its transfers, at every watch.

It was the first time the Third's command had ever asked for the figure.

The staff on Quarterdeck Nineteen looked at the order when it went out with an expression Kastellan recognised from Hold Charybdis, the expression of officers who had been told to do something obvious that nobody had thought of before and who were trying to work out whether they should feel ashamed. He didn't help them. He instructed the Répertoire to take the null figures and map them leaf by leaf across the whole of the Flats, and to have the map on his desk in five cycles.

It was on his desk in four.

He looked at it for a long time. His new chief of staff, a thin precise Directorate officer named Iason Dray who had come down the cables with Vasska and had been her deputy on the inquiry, stood beside the desk and waited.

The nulls weren't spread evenly over the Flats. They lay in two places.

One was a ring about the Acropolis. A clean circle, faint at its edges and darker toward its centre, drawn round the spur at a distance that Kastellan measured on the map with his dividers and found to be exactly the distance that the Répertoire's own survey gave for the reach of the Echo Mast.

The other lay in the south. A pale wash, spreading out from the Lethe Cut, where the grey had begun to come off the river and lie on the Flats.

Kastellan put the dividers down.

He didn't understand the first yet. He had read Ricci's study, and Tribune Wren's graph, in the bundle of slates Vasska had given him, and he understood what they said, but he didn't yet understand what it meant, and he was too old and too careful to pretend that he did.

He had already seen enough of the second.

"Dray," he said.

"Marshal."

"How long would it take to bring the Forge into this?"

---

**THE FORGE OF HEPHAESTUS, THE DEEP CRADLES. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 90**

First Cradle-Lord Doros Varne was two hundred and eleven years old, and he had been joined to the Brontes-pattern Behemoth *Anvil of Talos* through the old Hephaestean cradle links for a hundred and forty of them, and when the lords of the Forge sent for him in the ninetieth cycle of Rotation Nine to tell him that the Forge Cohort Talos was going to Depth 40, he had known it for three cycles already.

The *Anvil* had told him.

The *Anvil* had no words to give. He had lain in his cradle in the deep cradles of the Forge, joined, with the cradle links seated in the sockets at his temples and the base of his skull and the *Anvil*'s great slow mind moving round his own with the slow pull of deep water round a swimmer, and he had felt something change in it. A restlessness. A turning, deep in the engine's mind, toward something far down and far off, that it had not turned toward in all the hundred and forty years he had been joined to it.

He knew what it was. Every cradle-lord of the Forge knew. It was the river.

The Forge had built the Behemoths to walk the deep holds and crush what rose from the rivers, and for eight thousand years they had done it. And over the millennia, the rivers had taken some of them. In floods. In lost battles. In the long dark wars at the bottom of the holds that nobody above Depth 30 had ever heard of. A Behemoth that went down into the Phlegethon didn't die there. The river kept it, with its crew in the cradle. And in time the crew stopped being the crew, and the engine walked for the river.

The *Anvil* knew. It had felt them come up. It had felt the drowned engines walk out of the *Lament of Ixion* onto the Shale Plateau in Rotation Eight, nine of them, and stand up, and turn north. It had been restless ever since.

"The Third has asked for us," said the eldest of the lords of the Forge, in the great cold hall above the deep cradles. "Its new Marshal has asked. The drowned Behemoths must be met and destroyed, and only Behemoths can do it." He looked at Varne. "You've never been below Depth 30, Cradle-Lord."

"No, Lord."

"No cohort of the Forge has ever been below Depth 30."

"No, Lord."

"Will you go?"

Varne thought about the *Anvil*'s restlessness, the slow deep turning of its mind toward the river.

"Lord," he said, "the *Anvil* is already going. I'm only asking to go with it."

---

The rites took nine cycles.

Varne had seen them twice before in his life, for lesser deployments, and he had never seen them done in full. They were done in full now. The twenty-two engines of the Forge Cohort Talos stood in the deep cradles of the Forge in two long rows, ten Brontes-pattern engines eighty metres tall and twelve lighter, faster Steropes-pattern hunters, and the Hephaestean servicing companies went over them from head to foot, every plate and every ward and every gun. The cradle-crews were briefed and blessed. The flamens of the Forge sang the old office over each engine in turn, the long slow litany of the hammer and the anvil that the Forge had sung over its engines since before the Empire had a name.

And on the ninth cycle, the lords of the Forge came down into the deep cradles with the ichor.

It was carried in a basin of bronze, from the Forge's own altars, where it had been kept since the god himself had walked in the Forge. Varne had never seen it. Nobody living had. It was gold and it moved in the basin with a slow thick light of its own, and when the eldest of the lords of the Forge dipped his hand in it the light ran up his arm to the elbow.

He anointed the feet of every Behemoth. One by one, down the two long rows, the great iron feet of the engines that stood taller than the hall's pillars: a single stroke of gold across each, as a sign that the engines would lay the river's dead at the Imperator's feet.

When he came to the *Anvil*, Varne was in his cradle, joined. He felt the ichor go on.

He would never be able to describe it. The *Anvil*'s whole vast mind, which had been restless for three cycles, turning and turning toward the river, went still and fixed, a hound's stillness when it has the scent, and through the cradle links, at the deepest floor of the engine's mind, where Varne had never been able to reach in a hundred and forty years, he felt something answer, something old beyond reckoning and hot as the Forge's own heart.

He came out of the cradle afterward shaking, and sat on the steps of the deep cradles with his head in his hands, and the eldest of the lords of the Forge sat down beside him.

"You felt it," the old lord said.

"Yes, Lord."

"Good," the lord said. "Then it's awake. Take it down to the river, Cradle-Lord. And bring back as many as you can."

---

The Forge Cohort Talos was embarked in its forge-lighters for Tartarus on the hundredth cycle of Rotation Nine. With it went nine regiments of Hephaestean sappers, a hundred and eight thousand engineers trained to dig beneath fortresses since before the Empire had a name for them, and forty boring engines, and the servicing companies that would keep the Behemoths walking.

They were not the whole of the convoy. They were only its heart.

---

**THE RELIEF CONVOY, DESCENDING THROUGH THE CEILING CRACKS AT DEPTH 36. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 300**

In all, the relief force assembled for the Arsenal over the first three hundred cycles of Rotation Nine was the largest single convoy sent to the Tartarean theatre in a century, and the Directorate let it be known on the ceiling worlds that it expected a return on it.

Varne lay in his cradle aboard the forge-lighter *Hammerfall* and felt it descend.

He could feel the whole convoy through the *Anvil*'s augurs. The engines in their forge-lighters, twenty-two of them in three great ships. The transports with the eleven new Trench Regiments raised from the drafts of the ceiling worlds, one million eight hundred thousand soldiers to refill the Line Phalanxes. The two armoured regiments of Thureos tanks and the company of Colossi for the Fourth Assault Phalanx. The four ordnance regiments, two of them divine batteries keyed by children of Apollo and Ares from the holds of the Fire Steps. The nine hundred thousand tonnes of shells and nectar and stores. And around all of it the escort: the twin heavy cruisers *Lysander's Oath* and *Kallisto* and a full squadron of eight Lantern frigates, and above them, high in the ceiling geometry, the two Wrath-pattern strike wings of the Tartarus Fleet, interceptors and bombers, which would contest the ceiling over the Flats that the drowned had taken.

The *Lysander's Oath* led. The *Kallisto* came last. The transports and the forge-lighters were in the centre.

The Fleet had sent three Lantern destroyers of Picket Ithaca down through the ceiling cracks ahead of the convoy to find the drowned fleet. They had found the wrecks of the first battle of the low geometry, drifting in the geometry over the Flats: the *Themis Unbowed*, the *Long Labour*, the monitors and the frigates and the fire barges, a long scatter of dead hulls slowly turning in the dark. They had met no enemy. They had reported all quiet.

The convoy came down through the ceiling cracks into the low geometry and passed over the wrecks.

And the *Anvil* woke.

Varne felt it before the augurs did. The great slow mind round his own went suddenly, violently still, the stillness of a hound that has heard something in the dark, and through the cradle links he felt it turn, the whole engine's attention swinging round toward one of the drifting hulks below the convoy, a long dark shape among the others that the augurs read as dead.

The hulk was alive.

"*Lysander's Oath*," Varne said aloud, into the relay, before he knew he was going to. "*Lysander's Oath*. Below you. Among the wrecks. Something's..."

The *Eye of Light* struck.

---

She had lain there for most of a rotation. Varne learned that afterward. She had lain among the wrecks of the first battle with her reactors banked and her drowned crew at their stations, giving off nothing the Fleet's augurs could read, and waited. The Forsworn had served aboard the Empire's ships for six hundred years. They knew what a convoy escort looked for. They knew it didn't look at wrecks.

Her first broadside destroyed two of the Lantern frigates. Varne felt them go through the *Anvil*'s augurs, two bright sparks going out. Her second disabled the *Lysander's Oath*'s starboard thrusters and her steering, and the heavy cruiser at the head of the convoy began to slew round, slowly, broadside to the battleship, out of control. A transport loaded with Phaethon shells was hit and crippled and left drifting.

The cruisers replied. The *Kallisto*, at the rear, turned out of the convoy's line and fired a wide spread of torpedoes and closed on the battleship. The *Lysander's Oath*, out of control, went on firing anyway with every gun that would bear.

And the transports ran for the cables at Kappa.

Varne lay in his cradle aboard the *Hammerfall* and felt the forge-lighter's engines go to full power under him, and felt the *Anvil*'s mind straining toward the *Eye of Light*, wanting to fight, wanting to walk, and he could do nothing. The *Anvil* was in a hold. It couldn't fight from a hold. He lay in the cradle and felt the convoy run, and felt the remaining frigates place themselves between the transports and the *Eye of Light*'s guns, one after another, and felt two more of them crippled doing it.

At close range, even a battleship couldn't ignore two heavy cruisers.

The *Eye of Light* took the *Kallisto*'s torpedoes as she turned her guns on them. Varne felt them strike. Fires broke out inside her along the old wound in her hull where the Cocytus ice had been packed, and through the *Anvil*'s augurs he saw the ice glow red, and crack, and steam. Her batteries left the *Lysander's Oath* burning and venting atmosphere. But the *Kallisto* held her course, and came in close, and tore open the battleship's flank below the burned face of the god.

The *Eye of Light* broke off. With her prey escaping to the cables and her own damage mounting, she withdrew among the wrecks over the spur with two drowned escorts, slowly, trailing fire and steam.

The *Kallisto*, alone, didn't pursue.

---

The *Lysander's Oath* was beyond saving. Her captain ordered her abandoned and left the fires on her lower decks to burn her out, which took several weeks. Her surviving crew crossed to the *Kallisto*, which went on down to Kappa. The low geometry over the Flats acquired one more wreck.

Four escorts lost. Hundreds of the *Lysander's Oath*'s crew killed. But the convoy got through.

Varne lay in his cradle aboard the *Hammerfall* as the forge-lighter came down onto its cables over the Flats, and felt the *Anvil*'s mind slowly settle round his own, the stillness going out of it, the restlessness coming back.

"She's not dead," he said aloud, to nobody, to the *Anvil*. "Is she."

The *Anvil* didn't answer. It never did. But he felt, deep in the engine's mind, a long slow turning, back toward the wrecks over the spur, where the *Eye of Light* had gone.

The Fleet's officers argued for most of a rotation afterward over whether the *Kallisto*'s torpedoes had finished her. Varne never argued. He knew.

---

**THE FOOT OF THE CINDER STAIR. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 304**

The *Hammerfall* settled on its own cables onto the Flats at the foot of the Cinder Stair on the three hundred and fourth cycle, and opened its holds, and Varne walked the *Anvil of Talos* out onto the ash.

He felt the ash under the *Anvil*'s feet. It was the first thing he felt. Soft and deep and grey and endless, giving under every step, and under it, far down, through the soles of the engine's feet and up through the cradle links into his own body, the heat of the river.

Behind him, the other engines came out of the forge-lighters one after another. Ten Brontes-pattern engines eighty metres tall, and twelve Steropes-pattern hunters, lighter and faster, in their honour banners bearing the eagle of the Empire and the bronze hammer of Talos. They came out onto the ash in a long line and stood, and the ash rose round their feet in great slow clouds, and then, at Varne's word, they began to walk.

They walked north, along the foot of the Stair, toward the front.

The soldiers of the Third came up out of their trenches to watch them.

Varne saw them through the *Anvil*'s augurs. They had been ordered to stay in their trenches. Every regiment on the Flats had been ordered to keep its soldiers below the parapet while the engines passed, in case the drowned ships in the low geometry saw the movement. They came up anyway. They came up out of the trenches along the whole of the line as the engines went past, thousands of them, onto the parapets and the parados and the tops of the dugouts, and stood there in the grey light of the ash cloud with their rifles in their hands and their faces turned up, watching.

Nobody prevented them. Varne saw officers among them, centurions and optios and once a colonel, standing on the parapets with their soldiers, watching too.

He walked the *Anvil* past a trench where a cohort of the *44th* stood on the parados in a long grey line, and as he passed he saw a centurion among them, a thin, weathered woman with her helmet in her hand, looking up at the *Anvil* with an expression he would remember for the rest of his life. He could never have named it, though it sat somewhere past joy and hope, older and more tired than either.

He found out later, from the *44th*'s roll, who she was. Centurion Marrow, of the *6th* Cohort. She had been on the Flats since Rotation One.

For many of the soldiers who had been on the Flats for nine rotations, it was the first time they had believed the Third might win.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION NINE, CYCLES 304 TO 330**

The convoy's arrival transformed the Third's position.

Kastellan watched it happen on the map table, cycle by cycle. Soldiers came down the cables at Kappa by the thousand, tanks by the hundred and shells by the thousand tonnes, and everything was sent forward to the front at once. Within thirty cycles the Line Phalanxes had been brought back to strength, and the gun lines had been restocked to a level they hadn't held since the Saturation, and the Ordnance Phalanxes were allowed to fire freely for the first time since the drowned had cut the convoys.

He did one thing on his own authority that nobody on the staff understood. He posted the new soldiers to the Line Phalanxes by cohort, not as whole regiments, so that every cohort on the line received its share of drafts alongside its veterans. The Third had abandoned the practice in its first rotation, for administrative convenience. Dray objected to restoring it. It would make the returns harder to keep, he said.

"Then keep them harder," Kastellan said.

He had read Corvis's bundle of slates by then, all of it, three times. He had read about the *54th*, raised from the ration rolls and sent to the Flats as a whole regiment, bakers and clerks and dockhands together, none of them ever having been to Depth 40, none of them ever having transferred, with nobody among them who had. He had read the list. He kept a copy of it in his desk drawer, as Corvis had.

The Quiet Ones laid a second rail line from the foot of the Stair to the southern gun lines. The Hephaestean servicing companies built a cradle-yard for the Forge Cohort on the old outer belt below the Drum, where the Behemoths would be rearmed and repaired for the rest of the siege.

And Kastellan began to plan.

He sat at the desk that had been Castorre's, in the office that had been Castorre's, with the two letters that had been Castorre's in the inside pocket of his tunic beside the letter from Stephanos Pyrrhus, and he drew up the plan for the next attempt on the inner belt. It would take most of a rotation to make the battered regiments ready. It would begin, he decided, on the two hundred and fortieth cycle of Rotation Ten.

He called it nothing. The Répertoire called it the Kastellan Offensive.

On the evening of the three hundred and thirtieth cycle, his chief of staff came into the office with the first returns of the restocked gun lines and found him sitting at the desk with the map of the nulls in front of him, the clean ring round the Acropolis and the pale wash in the south, and the dividers in his hand.

"Marshal?"

"Dray," Kastellan said, without looking up. "Tell me the price."

Dray had the figures ready. He had been Vasska's deputy for nine years and he always had the figures ready.

"For the offensive as drafted, Marshal, the Répertoire estimates between one million and one million, four hundred thousand transfers over the first ten cycles. Null rate between two and three percent on most of the front. Higher near the Mast." He paused. "Much higher on the edge of the Grey."

Kastellan put down the dividers.

"Thank you," he said. "Now stop telling me."
