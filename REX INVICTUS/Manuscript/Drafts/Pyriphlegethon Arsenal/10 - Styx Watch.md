## Chapter 10: Styx Watch

**THE KATABASION, BENEATH THE CINDER CONE. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 411**

The Archflamen was praying when Dace came down to tell him, and Dace waited at the foot of the stair for an hour and a half rather than interrupt. He had learned long ago that there was no point in telling a man anything until he was ready to hear it.

The Katabasion was the tomb of the First Descent, and it lay under the cinder cone at the heart of the Arsenal, at the bottom of a stair of red marble that went down from the palace for three hundred steps. At the bottom of the stair was a hall. It was long and high and narrow, cut into the living basalt of the cone and faced from floor to vault with red marble quarried from the cone itself, so that the whole hall glowed a deep dull crimson in the light of the reliquary lamps, and along both its walls, in niches cut into the marble, lay the bones of Legio Prima Katabasis.

There were four thousand two hundred of them. The first Imperial legion to go down into Tartarus, eight thousand years ago, before there were holds or vats or a Répertoire, when the Empire had first decided that the deep was a thing to be held and not merely feared. They had gone down the Phlegethon with torches and spears and no way back, and fought their way down to the bend, and died there, all of them, holding a spur of black basalt over the river against things that had no names. The Hephaestean orders had found their bones when they came to build the Arsenal two hundred years after, and laid them here, in niches, each one with a small lamp of the Hearth burning beside it that the Vestals of the House had kept alight ever since.

At the far end of the hall, in a niche larger than the others, lay the bones of their Legate. Nobody knew his name. The Hephaestean records called him only *Primus*, the first, and the College had long ago declared that he had been the Lord Imperator himself, in one of his earliest descents, though there was no evidence for this and a good deal against it.

The Archflamen knelt before his niche on the bare marble, barefoot, with his hands open on his knees and his eyes closed, and the forty Vestals of the Hearth stood around the hall in their white robes with their lamps on their spears, and nobody made a sound.

At the end of the hour and a half, the Archflamen opened his eyes and said, without turning his head, "Come in, Ilan."

Dace came down the hall.

---

He told him standing, as a clerk reported to a magistrate, with his notebook open in his hand, and the Archflamen listened, still kneeling, with his kind tired face turned up toward him in the crimson light.

He told him about the thirty-one. He told him about Mirren, and the fifty-four seconds. And then he told him what it meant.

"Seven seconds," he said. "That's how long a soldier of the Third is in the open, Holiness, between the moment he dies and the moment the relay takes him. Seven seconds in the dark, alone. Then he's safe, and the noosphere carries him home to the Watchpoint, and nine days later he wakes in a vat with nectar in his mouth and goes back to the line. He never feels the seven seconds. He never knows they were there." He closed the notebook. "Mirren held one for fifty-four. If she can hold one for a minute, she can hold one until it's empty."

The Archflamen was silent for a long time.

"Empty," he said at last.

"Until there's nothing left to go home." Dace crouched down, so that his face was level with the Archflamen's. "Holiness. You know what the Third is. You've preached about it. You've stood on the palace steps and told the faithful that the Empire's dead come back and ours don't, and asked them to consider which side the Imperator has chosen. You know what the Empire's soldiers think death is. A lift, between the line and the vats. A bad fortnight. They go up and they come down and they go up again, and they've forgotten what it is to go down into the dark."

He gestured at the niches, at the four thousand two hundred.

"These went down," he said. "They went down into the dark with torches and spears and no way back, and they knew what dying was, and they did it anyway, for him. And he went down before all of them, alone, and came back up through it." His voice was quiet and very steady. "That's the gospel, Holiness. It's your gospel. You've preached it a thousand times. The Imperator went down into the dark and came back up through it. And he wants his soldiers to remember. He wants them to know what the dark is again."

The Archflamen looked past him at the niche of the nameless Legate.

"And the river," he said.

"There are others in the river," Dace said, "who want the same thing. They've been waiting a long time to be asked. I asked them." He paused. "I told you, two rotations ago, that the river would answer if asked. You didn't believe me."

"No," the Archflamen said. "I didn't."

He said nothing else. He knelt in front of the nameless Legate's niche with his hands open on his knees and looked at the bones in it for a long time, and Dace waited, crouched beside him, and the Vestals stood around the hall with their lamps, and none of them made a sound.

At last Dace stood up, and bowed, and went back up the three hundred steps of the stair to the palace, and the Archflamen did not call him back.

He wrote the conversation in his notebook that night, in his small neat hand, as he wrote everything. Under the Archflamen's last words he wrote: *No reply.* The Hunt's interrogators found the notebook nine rotations later in the Haruspex's quarters at the Tabularium, among sixty others, and the page is the only record of the night the Kindlers' creed was first spoken aloud.

**THE HEAD OF THE ORE ROAD, THE MORA CUT BELOW ORE HEAD. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 415**

Optio Tobias Rell of the *26th* Trench Regiment had been posted to the head of the old ore road for six weeks, and in six weeks nothing had come up it but rats, and he'd started to think of it as his road.

It came up the face of the Mora Cut in a long series of switchbacks cut into the wall of the chasm, a smaller and meaner cousin of the Cinder Stair, from the bottom of the Cut eight hundred metres below. Before the siege it had carried ore up from the old Hephaestean workings in the depths of the Cut to the lift-heads at Ore Head, and the ore-lift gangs had lived in the forty thousand prefabricated huts of Ore Head at the top of it. The *5th* Line Phalanx had shelled Ore Head flat in the last month of its advance and taken it almost without a fight, and since then the road had been used by nothing at all. It went down into the Cut and away to the south, toward the confluence of the Phlegethon and the Styx at Depth 44, and the Third's maps marked its lower reaches with a red line and a single word: *Impassable*.

Rell's picket was eleven soldiers in a sandbagged post at the head of the road, with a heavy rifle and a relay set and a slate for the log, and their orders were to watch the road and report anything that came up it. Nothing had. They spent the long cycles playing dice, and looking down into the Cut, and arguing about which of them would get to go back to the support line first.

At the second bell of the four hundred and fifteenth cycle, one of the sentries said, "Optio. There's something on the road."

Rell went to the parapet. He looked down.

There was a column coming up out of the Cut.

It came up the switchbacks below him in a long dark line, soldiers on foot, four abreast, in good order, at a steady walk, and the head of it was already on the fourth switchback from the top, three hundred metres below him. He couldn't see the tail of it. It went back down into the darkness of the Cut further than the glow of the Phlegethon reached. In the light of the river he could see that they were wearing grey.

"Stand to," he said. "Heavy rifle on the road. Nobody fires without my order."

The picket stood to. Rell took the long glass and put it on the head of the column.

They wore the Empire's grey trench armour, the same pattern as the Third's, scratched and dented and black with age. Over it they wore long black cloaks, hooded, that came down to their knees. They carried rifles, slung, and at their hips, every one of them, a short sword in a black scabbard. At their head walked a tall soldier with a centurion's transverse crest on her helmet, a black crest, and a black cloak, and nothing in her hands at all.

"Styx Watch," the sentry beside him said. "Those are Styx Watch cloaks, Optio. I've seen them. My cousin's in the *9th* Styx Legion. They hold the confluence. That's Styx Watch."

Rell kept the glass on the head of the column.

"Challenge them," he said.

---

The challenge went out from the picket's relay set on every band. The column's transponders answered within the second, and the answer came up on the slate in Rell's hand, in the Répertoire's amber.

*Transponder response: 9TH STYX LEGION. 4TH COHORT. CENTURION. AUTHENTICATED.*

The head of the column reached the top of the road ten minutes later. It stopped, without any order Rell could hear, twenty metres from the sandbags, and stood there, four abreast, in silence, as far back down the switchbacks as he could see.

The centurion walked forward alone. She stopped in front of the sandbags and pushed back her hood.

She was tall. Her face was long and pale and lined, with deep grooves from the nose to the corners of the mouth, and her hair was cropped close to the skull and white. Her eyes were grey, a light, cold grey, almost colourless, and they looked at Rell over the sandbags without any expression at all.

"Optio," she said. Her voice was low and slightly hoarse from disuse. "*4th* Cohort, *9th* Styx Legion. We were cut off at the confluence in Rotation Two, when the river rose. We've been fighting our way up the Cut for four rotations toward the sound of the guns." She paused. "We'd be grateful for a place to rest."

Rell looked at her. He looked past her, at the column, standing motionless on the road.

"How many of you?" he asked.

"Four hundred and twelve."

"From a cohort of six hundred and forty?"

"From a cohort of six hundred and forty," the centurion said, "four rotations ago."

Rell looked down at the slate. The transponder response was still glowing on it. *AUTHENTICATED.* He keyed a query, and sent it, and waited eleven seconds.

*Query received. 9th Styx Legion, 4th Cohort. Status: contact lost, Rotation Two, cycle 87, confluence, Depth 44. Centurion's code valid. Ave Imperator.*

It was all true. Everything she'd said. The Répertoire had it.

"You'll want to report to the *5th* Line Phalanx," Rell said. "I'll log you through. There's reserve trench on Leaf 52-33, behind the *44th*, that's empty. You can rest there while the Command decides what to do with you." He hesitated. "Do you need rations? Water? Medici? We can send..."

"No," the centurion said. "Thank you, Optio. We have what we need."

She turned, and raised one hand, and the column began to move again, past the picket's post, four abreast, at the same steady walk.

Rell stood at the sandbags and watched them go by.

He would think about it a great deal afterward, and tell it to the Hunt's interrogators eight rotations later, in a cell on the ceiling, and they would write down every word. He would tell them that the column had gone past him for forty minutes and that not one of the four hundred and twelve had looked at him. He would tell them that none of them had spoken, not one word, in forty minutes, which was not something he had ever known soldiers do. He'd say that they had carried no packs, and no rations, and no water that he could see, after four rotations of fighting up a chasm that the Third's maps marked impassable. He'd say that their armour was black with something that wasn't soot, and that when they went past him, close, he had smelled something cold, the bitter air off a river in winter, though there was no winter anywhere in Tartarus and never had been.

And he would tell them about the neck.

One of the soldiers in the column, near the end, had stumbled on the last switchback as he came up past the post, and his hood had fallen back. Under it, his hair was cropped short to match the centurion's, and on the back of his neck, at the base of his skull, where the implant sat in every soldier of the Third, there was a scar. It was old and white and puckered, a long straight line across the nape, where somebody had once cut something out of him with a knife and left it to heal over the centuries.

The soldier had pulled his hood back up at once, without looking round, and walked on.

Rell had logged the column through on his slate, as ordered. *R5 c415. 9th Styx Legion, 4th Cohort, 412 all ranks. Centurion's code authenticated. Directed to reserve trench, Leaf 52-33.* He'd sent it up to the *26th*, and the *26th* had sent it up to the *5th* Line Phalanx, and somebody at the *5th* had read it and filed it.

By the next cycle, the column was gone from the reserve trench on Leaf 52-33. The *5th* Line Phalanx's staff assumed it had been sent up the Stair to rejoin its Legion. Nobody checked.

**THE KATABASION. ROTATION SIX, CYCLE 2**

Hesper had not been underground in a place built by men for eleven hundred years, and she found the Katabasion beautiful and slightly absurd.

She came down the three hundred steps of the red marble stair alone, at the hour the Haruspex had named, with her hood back and her hands empty, and stood at the foot of the stair and looked down the long crimson hall at the niches of the dead and the forty Vestals of the Hearth with their lamps. At the far end of the hall, a grey-haired man in white vestments was standing in front of the largest niche, barefoot, waiting for her. Beside him stood the Haruspex in his grey coat, with his notebook, and a scribe of the Tabularium with a slate.

She walked down the hall between the niches. The Vestals watched her pass. Their lamps flickered as she went by, every one of them, stirred by a cold draught that had come down the stair with her, and then steadied.

She stopped in front of the Archflamen.

"Holiness," she said.

"You came up the river," the Archflamen said. He was looking at her with great attention, his kind tired face gone still. "Ilan says you came up from the Styx."

"From the confluence. By the Cut."

"Who are you?"

She'd known he would ask. She had thought, on the long walk up the Cut, about how to answer. In the end she had decided to tell him the truth, all of it. She had nothing to lose by it, he would find most of it out anyway, and after eleven hundred years she was tired of not being asked.

"My name is Hesper," she said. "Hesper Calloway. I was a Legate of the *9th* Styx Legion. I swore its oath on the Styx when I was nineteen years old, as every soldier of the *9th* swears it, standing in the river at the confluence with the water up to my waist. I swore to hold the confluence until the Imperator came back up through it."

She paused.

"I held it for six hundred years," she said. "I held it against things you don't have names for, Holiness, and against things you do. I held it with the *9th*, and with my own hands, and I died on it a hundred and eleven times and came back up the relay a hundred and eleven times and went back down to it. And then, in the six hundred and first year, the Command ordered the *9th* to give up a third of the confluence to save a hold upriver that was falling."

"And you refused."

"I refused. I'd sworn an oath. The Command hadn't sworn anything." Her voice didn't change. "They relieved me. They sent a new Legate down the relay with a writ. And I took the cohorts that would follow me sooner than break the oath, nine of them, five thousand seven hundred soldiers, and I walked down into the Styx with them, at the confluence, where I'd sworn, and kept going until the river was over our heads."

The scribe of the Tabularium had stopped writing. The Haruspex had not.

"The river took us," Hesper said. "The Styx keeps oaths, Holiness. It keeps an oath broken from above, and it takes the person who swore it, and it holds them. We were the only soldiers left who still held the *9th*'s oath. The ones who'd stayed behind had broken it, when they gave up the third of the confluence. We hadn't. So the river kept us." She looked down at her hands, which were long and pale and very still. "The *9th*'s records will tell you we were entered as null. Five thousand seven hundred of us. They'll call us the Forsworn."

"Are you?" the Archflamen asked. "Forsworn?"

"No," Hesper said. "We're the only ones who aren't."

---

She told him the rest. She told him that the Forsworn had cut out their implants a long time ago, with their own knives, at the bottom of the river. She told him why.

"The Empire's soldiers go up when they die," she said, "and come back down, and they believe it's a gift. A mercy. The great mercy of the Empire, that nobody who serves it ever has to die. We believed it too, once, the *9th*. We went up and came back down for six hundred years and thanked the Imperator for it." She looked at the niches of the dead along the walls. "Then we went into the river, and we saw where the uploads go on the way."

"Where do they go?" the Archflamen asked.

Hesper looked at him for a long moment.

"Up," she said. "Mostly. Seven seconds in the dark, and then the relay, and home. But not all of them. Not always. There are things in the deep that hear them going past, Holiness. There always have been. Old things. They've been listening to the Empire's dead go up past them for ten thousand years." She paused. "Your Haruspex asked the river for help. We heard. Others heard as well. We were closest."

"What do you want?" the Archflamen said.

"What you want," Hesper said. "The Empire's soldiers are to remember what dying is."

The Archflamen said nothing for a long while.

"What do you offer?"

She told him. Four hundred and twelve of the Forsworn, the ones she'd brought up the Cut, and two thousand more who would come up when she called them. Training for his thralls, whose fighting she had watched for a cycle from the ore road below Ore Head and judged poor. Her own six hundred years of trench war on the Styx, and eleven hundred of war under it. And one thing more, which she would not describe in front of his clerks.

The Archflamen looked at the scribe, who stopped writing, and at the Haruspex, who didn't.

"Ilan," he said. "Will you take the Legate to see whatever she needs to see?"

"Of course, Holiness."

"Then I accept," the Archflamen said, and turned back to the niche of the nameless Legate, and knelt, and closed his eyes.

**THE GUEST QUARTERS OF THE TABULARIUM. ROTATION SIX, CYCLE 2, DARK WATCH**

The Haruspex took her across the bridge to the Tabularium after dark, and Hesper stood in the doorway of the guest quarters and looked at the strays for a long time.

There were five hundred of them, in the guest quarters and the old Enforcement barracks, asleep in their whitewashed rooms on their real beds under their real blankets. Some of them weren't asleep. Some of them were sitting up with their eyes open in the dark, listening, and some were rocking, and two or three were crying quietly, and in the last room at the end of the corridor a thin pale girl of thirteen was sitting cross-legged on her bed with her hands on her knees and her eyes closed, utterly still.

"Mirren," the Haruspex said softly. "This is the Legate Hesper. She's come up from the river."

The girl opened her eyes.

She looked at Hesper for a long moment, and Hesper looked back at her. Then the girl's face changed. She drew back on the bed, slowly, until her back was against the wall, and pulled her knees up to her chin.

"You're cold," she said. "You're all cold. All of you." She looked past Hesper at the door, and through it, toward the column on Leaf 52-33. "You're the birds that don't go home. The ones that get caught. But you're walking around."

Hesper didn't move.

"Yes," she said. "That's about right."

She stepped into the room and crouched down beside the bed, as the Haruspex did, so that her face was level with the girl's. She didn't touch her. The girl watched her the whole time, pressed against the wall.

"The Haruspex tells me you can hold them," Hesper said. "The birds. When they go up. He tells me you held one for fifty-four seconds."

The girl nodded slowly.

"I can teach you to hold them longer," Hesper said. "As long as you like. I know how. I learned it at the bottom of the river, from the things that have been doing it for ten thousand years." She paused. "And I can teach you where to put them, after, when they're empty. So that they don't hurt you anymore."

The girl stared at her.

"Where?" she whispered.

Hesper glanced up, through the window of the little room, across the gorge, at the long black spire of the Echo Mast standing up over the inner ward in the dark, its relay vanes turning slowly in the heat.

"Down," she said.

**THE ECHO MAST. ROTATION SIX, CYCLES 4 TO 12**

On the Archflamen's authority, the Forsworn took over the Echo Mast two cycles later.

It had been dark to the noosphere since the night the Tabularium fell, five rotations ago. There had been no reason to reopen it. Nothing the Archflamen could receive from the Empire's noosphere would help him, and nothing he could send up it would be believed, and the Mast had stood over the inner ward for five rotations drawing power from the casting lines and turning its vanes and saying nothing to anyone. The Third's signallers had noted that it was still drawing power and transmitting on bands the Third didn't use. They had been noting it for five rotations. Nobody had asked them what it meant.

Hesper's signallers knew what it meant. They knew the Mast's bands from the *9th* Styx Legion's own archive, which they had carried in their heads for eleven hundred years. The Mast had been built eight thousand years ago, as the noosphere relay of a forge-hold, and as a beacon. In the days when the Arsenal supplied the holds below Depth 40, in the days when there had been holds below Depth 40, the Mast had spoken to them on downward bands, down the rivers, through the rock, and when the holds were abandoned the downward bands had fallen silent and been forgotten. The Hephaesteans had stopped using them seven thousand years ago. The Répertoire had never known they existed.

Hesper's signallers climbed the Mast to its relay heads on the fourth cycle of Rotation Six and turned it downward.

They broadcast for nine cycles.

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SIX, CYCLE 6**

Leda Corvis first heard the carrier tone in the middle of a meeting with the Dionysian Logistics Directorate about three Trench Regiments that did not, so far as anyone could tell, exist.

The Lord-General had asked for them at the start of the rotation. He'd asked for a new Line Phalanx for the Cinder Flats, three Trench Regiments, to be committed on the western Flats against the second belt, and he had told the Command, in writing, that with one more phalanx the Third would break the second belt before the end of the rotation and bring the campaign back onto its timetable. Corvis had drafted the letter. She had believed every word of it.

The request had gone through the ordinary channels, which meant the Directorate, and the Directorate's clerks had begun to search the depots of the theatre for three Trench Regiments to which no other theatre had a claim. They had been searching for six cycles. Corvis had spent four hours a cycle on the relay with them, and had come to understand that the Directorate's clerks were not looking for three Trench Regiments so much as for a reason to give them to somebody else.

"The Lethe holds," the senior clerk was saying, a smooth fat man named Antiochus whose voice came through the relay thick and wet around whatever he was eating. "The Lethe holds have asked for six regiments, Chief of Staff. Six. To replace the divine batteries they can't feed. The Acheron stairs have asked for four. The Cocytus line has asked for nine. And the Third, which was given four rotations to take the Arsenal and has used six without reaching the inner belt, and has consumed more vat capacity at the Watchpoint than every other front in the theatre combined, is asking for three more." He paused. "You can see the difficulty."

"I can see that the Third is the only front in the theatre that can end the shell shortage, Clerk Antiochus," Corvis said. "The Lethe holds are short of shells because the Arsenal is in the Archflamen's hands. Give the Lethe holds six regiments and they'll hold for another rotation. Give the Third three, and the Lethe holds won't need any."

"So you've said. Several times."

"I'll say it again, if it helps."

"It doesn't, particularly," Antiochus said. "There are officers in the Command who feel that the Third should be held where it stands, Chief of Staff. That the Arsenal should be left to starve inside its belts and the soldiers sent where they'd do more good. I'm sure you're aware of that view. On the other hand, there are members of the Pontifical College who feel very strongly that the Archflamen should be removed with all possible speed, while the faithful still regard him as a priest who's gone astray and not as a martyr. The Lord-General's family has been very active on the College's side of the question. Very active. And the Command..."

Corvis's relay set made a sound.

A tone came from the set on her desk underneath Antiochus's voice, low and steady, a single held note with no message in it and no burst of transmission behind it, sounding from a long way off. She looked down at it.

"...and the Command, as I say, is divided," Antiochus went on. He hadn't noticed. "So you can understand why the Directorate..."

"Clerk Antiochus," Corvis said. "Can you hear that?"

"Hear what?"

"There's a tone on the relay."

There was a pause.

"I don't hear anything, Chief of Staff."

She looked out through the door of her office into the corridor. The duty signaller at the end of it, a young woman at the relay console, was sitting perfectly still with her hand on her headset, frowning. Further down, in the relay vault, two of the night watch were standing up from their stations. And on every set Corvis could see, on every console and desk and pane, the same low steady tone was sounding.

"Clerk Antiochus," she said. "I'll have to call you back."

---

It was on every set on the Flats.

By the end of the watch the reports had come in from every regiment of the Third, and from the gun lines, and from the depots at Kappa and the railheads, and from the relay pylons across the Flats, and all of them said the same thing. Every relay set in the *3rd* Siege Army was carrying a carrier tone, low and steady, on no band the Third used, underneath its ordinary traffic. It didn't interfere with the traffic. It was simply there. And beneath the tone, if you listened carefully, with the volume turned all the way up and your ear pressed to the speaker, there was something else.

"It sounds like a voice," the signals officer of the *44th* reported. "Far away, somebody talking in another room. I can't make out words. I don't think they're words. But it's a voice."

Corvis took it to Varros. Varros took it to the Répertoire. The Répertoire answered in eleven seconds.

*Query received. Carrier tone, Cinder Flats, all sets. Source: Echo Mast, Pyriphlegethon Arsenal. Band: non-standard, archaic, downward. Content: unreadable. Direction of transmission: down. Assessment: the Archflamen is transmitting on the Mast's archaic bands to forces below Depth 40. Probable intent: appeal for aid. Probability of aid: low. Ave Imperator.*

"Down," Corvis said. "It's broadcasting down."

"So it says." Varros sat in his cognition chair with the reply on his pane and a glass of the dreadful ceiling brandy in his hand, and looked at the word *down* for a long time. "There aren't any holds below Depth 40, Chief of Staff. There haven't been for seven thousand years. The Mast is talking to nobody."

"Then who's it for?"

"I have no idea." He drank. "But it's not for us. We're just overhearing it."

---

The Mast broadcast for nine cycles.

On the ninth, it stopped, and the carrier tone went off every relay set on the Flats at once, as suddenly as it had come, and did not come back.

The Répertoire had been listening the whole time. It couldn't read the broadcast, but it could follow it, and it tracked what answered. It logged movement at Depth 43, where the Lethe met the Phlegethon, in the deep below the Lethe holds. It logged movement under the Cocytus ice at Depth 47. It logged movement along the lower Phlegethon, below the Arsenal's old docks, in formations the Third's spotters had never recorded moving together before. Corvis read the logs when they came up to the Quarterdeck. They were long, and technical, and full of numbers she didn't understand, and at the end of them, in the Répertoire's amber, was the assessment.

*Assessment: movement consistent with an ordinary reformation cycle of the deep. Assigned to the holds downriver. No action required of the 3rd Siege Army.*

She read it twice. Then she filed it, and went back to the Directorate and the three Trench Regiments that didn't exist.

She would remember, afterward, that she had read it twice. She would remember that she had wondered, for a moment, at the end of the second reading, whether it was a coincidence that the deep had begun an ordinary reformation cycle in the same nine cycles that the Echo Mast had been calling down into it. And she would remember that she had decided that the Répertoire knew more than she did, as it always had, and filed it.

The connection was made in Rotation Eight, when what had answered reached the Cinder Flats.

**THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE 44TH, LEAF 52-33. ROTATION SIX, CYCLE 19, SECOND BELL**

The *44th* had been planning the attack on Leaf 52-33 for a month, and Ilsa Marrow thought it was the best attack the Third had made on the second belt since the Saturation, and for the first hour, it was.

The plan was simple. The best ones always were. Thirty-one Hoplon carriers would go in under a short, heavy barrage at the second bell, in a wedge, timed so that the barrage lifted as they reached the wire. There'd be no long bombardment to warn the Arsenal, and no rolling barrage for the garrison to wait out in their shelters. The barrage would come down hard for twenty minutes and lift exactly as the Hoplons hit the wire, and the Hoplons would cross the wire and the forward trench on their tracks without stopping, and drop their ramps on the support trench behind it, and put fifteen hundred breachers into the second belt in four minutes, before the garrison had time to come up out of its shelters.

Marrow's *6th* Cohort would be in the first four Hoplons. She'd asked for that. Her colonel had looked at her for a long time and then said yes.

"Because you're mad," Brandt had said, when she told him.

"Because I've been in the second belt twice," Marrow said, "once in the Saturation and once on Leaf 59, and both times I got thrown out of it by the garrison coming up from below while I was still standing in the forward trench wondering where to go next. This time I'm not going to be in the forward trench. I'm going to be in the support trench, behind it, before the garrison's out of bed. And I'm going to be there first."

At the second bell, the barrage came down.

---

It went exactly as the plan said. That was the strangest part, afterward. For an hour, everything the *44th* had planned for a month happened exactly as it had been planned.

The barrage came down on the second belt on Leaf 52-33 for twenty minutes, hard and heavy and short, and the thirty-one Hoplons went forward out of the *44th*'s trenches under it in a wedge, grinding across the ash at full speed, and Marrow rode in the lead Hoplon of the *6th* Cohort's four with fifty of her soldiers packed in around her in the dark of the troop compartment, shoulder to shoulder in their plate, swaying, deafened, waiting.

At the twentieth minute, the barrage lifted. She felt the Hoplon hit the wire. It tore straight through it and over the forward trench, lurching, tilting, slamming down on the far side, and then she felt it stop, and heard the ramp go down with a crash, and the light came in.

"Out!" she was roaring. "Out, out, out! *6th* Cohort! Go!"

They went out. They went down the ramp of the Hoplon into the support trench of the second belt on Leaf 52-33, behind the forward trench, before the garrison was out of its shelters, as she had said they would be. Along the line of the trench on either side of her, the other thirty Hoplons were dropping their ramps, and the breachers of the *44th* were pouring out of them in their plate, fifteen hundred of them, into the second belt, in four minutes.

The thralls in the support trench were still coming up their shafts from the shelters when the *6th* Cohort reached them. They never got out of the shafts.

By the end of the first hour, the *6th* Cohort held four hundred metres of the support trench, the *44th*'s cohorts were coming across no-man's land behind the Hoplons to widen the breach, and Marrow had her relay set to her ear listening to her colonel ask the *5th* Line Phalanx for the *20th* Armoured.

"*They've got it*," she heard him say. "*The Forty-Fourth's in the second belt. We're through. I need the Twentieth. I need it now.*"

She lowered the handset and looked along the support trench. It was the Arsenal's work, revetted in steel, deep and dry, and it was full of her soldiers.

"We're through, Saul," she said. "We're actually fucking through."

Brandt, beside her, said nothing. He was looking east, along the trench, at the traverse forty metres away.

"Centurion," he said. "Friendlies. Coming down the trench from the east."

---

They came round the traverse in a column, four abreast, filling the trench from wall to wall. They wore the Empire's grey trench armour, scratched and dented and black with age, and over it, long black cloaks, hooded, that came down to their knees. They carried rifles, slung. Marrow's visor picked up their transponders and painted them green, every one of them, the soft green of a friendly unit, and the code came up in the corner of her eye. *9TH STYX LEGION. AUTHENTICATED.*

"Styx Watch," Brandt said. "What the fuck is the Styx Watch doing in the second belt?"

Marrow didn't answer. She was looking at the soldier at the head of the column, a tall soldier with a black centurion's crest on her helmet, walking toward her down the trench at a steady pace, with nothing in her hands.

There was something wrong with how they were moving.

She couldn't have said what it was, then. Later she would be able to say it exactly, and say it to her colonel, and to Callix, and to a woman in black from the Cohort of Nemesis. Soldiers in a trench moved carefully, crouching, keeping their heads below the parapet, watching the traverses. These were walking down the middle of the trench upright, at a steady pace, without hurrying and without looking round, certain that nothing in the second belt could hurt them.

The centurion at their head stopped ten metres away.

"Centurion," she said, in a low hoarse voice. "You're in our trench."

Then the Forsworn drew their swords.

---

They were short black swords, a little longer than a trench knife, black from hilt to point, and they came out of their black scabbards with a sound like ice cracking. The column came down the trench at the *6th* Cohort at a run, and it was the fastest thing Marrow had ever seen. They didn't take cover. They didn't fire their rifles. They came down the middle of the trench upright, in their black cloaks, faster than any soldier she'd ever seen move in plate, and they went into the fire bays among the breachers of the *44th* with the black swords.

Marrow fired. Every breacher in the trench fired. The rounds hit the soldiers in the black cloaks and didn't go in. She saw them hit. She saw her own round strike the chest of the centurion with the black crest, square on the breastplate, at ten metres, spark and fall away from something far harder than steel, and the centurion didn't even break stride.

And the black swords went through the Third's plate with no more trouble than through cloth.

She saw a breacher of the *3rd* Maniple, a big slow man named Dorran who'd been in the *6th* for six years, take a black sword through the chest plate and out through the back plate, and fall. She saw it happen to six others in the time it took her to chamber a second round. And she saw, with the part of her mind that had been on the Flats for six rotations and had seen a thousand soldiers fall, that something about how they fell was wrong.

When a soldier of the Third was killed, his implant fired. You couldn't see it, but you could feel it, if you'd been on the line long enough, a little jolt in the air, a flicker on your visor's relay display as the upload went past. Every soldier on the line knew the flicker. It meant the soldier beside you had gone up and would be back in a fortnight.

Dorran went down with a black sword through him, and there was no flicker.

The others went down, and there was nothing.

"Back!" she was screaming. "Back, back, get back! Back to the Hoplons! Get back over the wire! Don't let them touch you! Don't let them fucking touch you!"

---

By the end of the second hour, the breach was closed.

All thirty-one Hoplons were burning inside the second belt. The Forsworn had gone through them one by one, and the garrison had come up out of its shelters behind them, and the thralls behind the garrison, and the *44th*'s cohorts coming across no-man's land to widen the breach had been caught in the open by the counter-barrage and stopped. The *20th* Armoured never came. The *5th* Line Phalanx cancelled the request at the end of the first hour, when the *44th*'s colonel stopped asking for it.

Of the fifteen hundred breachers who had gone into the support trench of the second belt on Leaf 52-33, sixty came back over the wire after dark.

Marrow was one of them.

---

**THE COMMAND DUGOUT OF THE 44TH. ROTATION SIX, CYCLE 20**

She wrote her report that night, by hand, at the map table in the colonel's dugout, with the colonel standing over her and her hands shaking so badly she had to start the first page three times.

She wrote it plainly. She wrote that the counter-attack on Leaf 52-33 had come along the support trench from the east, and that its soldiers had worn the Empire's grey trench armour and the black cloaks of the Styx Watch, and that their transponders had shown on the Third's visors as friendly, and that their code had read *9th* Styx Legion and been authenticated. She wrote that they had moved with a speed and assurance she had not seen in six rotations on the Flats. She wrote that they had ignored cover. She wrote that they had carried short black swords, which they used in the fire bays, and that the Third's rounds had not penetrated their armour, while their swords had gone through the Third's.

And she wrote, at the end, the thing she'd felt in the trench, and couldn't stop feeling.

*Soldiers struck down by the black swords did not upload. I have served on the line for six rotations and know the feel of an implant firing. There was none. I request that the Répertoire check the transfer logs of every breacher killed in the support trench.*

She signed it and gave it to the colonel. He read it twice, and looked at her, and didn't say anything, and sent it up the line.

It went to the *5th* Line Phalanx that night, and from there to Quarterdeck Nineteen, and from there to the Répertoire.

The Répertoire checked the transfer logs.

Four hundred and nine of the fifteen hundred breachers had been killed by the black swords. All four hundred and nine were null.

---

**QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION SIX, CYCLE 41**

The Command at Watchpoint Hades read Marrow's report on the forty-first cycle of Rotation Six, and so did Corvis, and Corvis read it with the other things that had been put beside it, and when she had finished she went to find the Lord-General.

He was in his quarters, at his desk, under his reading lamp. He'd read it already. She could see that from how he was sitting.

"The *9th* Styx Legion's codes," he said, before she could speak. "The Archflamen has the *9th* Styx Legion's codes. They must have come from the column at the Mora Cut. The picket of the *26th* passed it through on the four hundred and fifteenth, and the Répertoire's identified the codes as the same. A cohort of the *9th*, lost at the confluence in Rotation Two. They must have been captured, and their codes taken..."

"No, sir," Corvis said.

He looked up.

She put the other papers down on his desk in front of him, one at a time, as she had done for nineteen years, in the order he would want them.

The picket's log from the Mora Cut. *R5 c415. 9th Styx Legion, 4th Cohort, 412 all ranks.* Optio Rell's statement, taken by the provosts that morning, about the silence and the smell of cold and the scar at the nape of the neck. The Répertoire's record of the carrier tone. And the note from the technical officers on the four hundred and nine nulls.

The technical officers had not seen a weapon do this before. But they knew of one, in the archive, and they referred the Command to it: the *9th* Styx Legion's own records of what had happened eighteen hundred years before to soldiers of the *9th* who were cut by Styx-iron at the confluence. Styx-iron was the black metal of the Styx, forged under the river, and the *9th* had carried swords of it against the things that came up the river for a thousand years. The *9th*'s records were plain. A soldier cut by Styx-iron didn't upload.

"And this," Corvis said, and put down the last sheet.

It was from the *9th* Styx Legion. It confirmed that the cohort passed through at the Mora Cut had been lost in Rotation Two. And it said that the centurion's code the column had used was not, in fact, the code of the lost cohort's centurion. It had been reissued to her from an older one, by the Legion's own records office, which reissued old codes as a matter of economy. It had been first issued eleven centuries before to a Legate of the *9th* named Hesper Calloway.

Hesper Calloway had refused the Command's order at the confluence, eleven hundred years ago. She had gone into the Styx with nine cohorts of the Legion, five thousand seven hundred soldiers, and been entered as null. The *9th*'s records had a name for the soldiers who went with her.

They called them the Forsworn.

Castorre read the sheet twice. Then he sat back in his chair.

"Leda," he said slowly. "What are you telling me?"

"Sir, the Archflamen hasn't obtained the *9th* Styx Legion's codes," Corvis said. "He's obtained the *9th* Styx Legion's dead. Soldiers who went into the river eleven hundred years ago and came back out of it. They know the Third's codes because they used to be the Empire's own. They know our drill, our bands, our habits. They've fought six hundred years of trench war on the Styx." She put her finger on Marrow's report. "Four hundred of them closed a breach held by fifteen hundred of our breachers in an hour. They carry swords whose wounds the vats can't undo. And the Forsworn hold the Echo Mast. The carrier tone was them, sir. The broadcast down the river was them."

Castorre looked at the papers on his desk for a long time.

"Down the river," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"The Répertoire assessed the movement in the deep as an ordinary reformation cycle."

"Yes, sir."

"Do you believe that?"

Corvis was quiet for a moment.

"I read it twice, sir," she said. "The first time it came up. I read it twice, and I wondered, and then I filed it. I thought the Répertoire knew more than I did." She looked at him. "I don't know what I believe now."

Castorre picked up his stylus.

He wrote to the Command that cycle. He reported the substance of everything on his desk, plainly, without adjustment, and Corvis did not draft it for him and did not try to. He renewed his request for the new Line Phalanx. And he asked for something to be done about the Mast.

---

**THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE 44TH. ROTATION SIX, CYCLE 44**

The order came down to every regiment on the Flats three cycles later, and Marrow read it out to the *6th* Cohort at stand-to, standing on the fire-step of Bay 1 with the slate in her hand.

"*From the forty-first cycle of Rotation Six,*" she read, "*every regiment of the Third is to challenge any party of Styx Watch on sight, regardless of its codes, and to fire upon any party that does not halt.*"

She lowered the slate. The *6th* Cohort looked at her from the fire bays, all along the trench, in silence. Fewer than half of them had been in the *6th* before Leaf 52-33. The rest were new, fresh down the Stair from the vats or the ceiling drafts, filling the places of the four hundred and nine who would not be coming back.

"Questions?" she said.

A trooper in Bay 3 put his hand up. He was one of the new ones.

"Centurion," he said. "How do we tell them apart? The Forsworn. From real Styx Watch."

Marrow thought about the column coming round the traverse on Leaf 52-33, walking upright down the middle of the trench, without hurrying, without looking round. She thought about the round sparking off the black-crested centurion's breastplate at ten metres. She thought about Dorran going down without a flicker.

"Real Styx Watch keep their heads down," she said. "Real Styx Watch are afraid of something."

---

In the remaining months of Rotation Six, the soldiers of the *3rd* Siege Army challenged every party of Styx Watch they saw, regardless of its codes, and fired upon every party that did not halt.

They killed eleven of their own soldiers in the rear areas under that order. Nine of them were genuine soldiers of the *9th* Styx Legion, sent down from the confluence with dispatches for the Command. Two were signallers of the Third who had borrowed Styx Watch cloaks against the seep.

They killed no Forsworn.
