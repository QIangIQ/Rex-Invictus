THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART ELEVEN. THE SCREE

"My eye to your aim. My hand to your will. My life to your hunt."
Oath of the Psiloi, said on the Flats before the first assault on the Acropolis

THE ACROPOLIS. ROTATION EIGHTEEN

All the old ore roads of the Cinder Flats ran to one place. They had linked the casting lines and the heat-wells and the magazines and the trench belts that now lay smashed behind the Third, and they all ended at the foot of the Acropolis. The cone stood up out of the Flats on a pillar of black basalt, the last and tallest of the old cinder vents, and the main road went up its side in switchbacks to the Founders' Gate. It climbed back and forth, back and forth, to a gate of cut basalt blocks that the heat of the Flats had worn smooth over eight thousand years.
Inside, above the walls and parapets of the cone, rose the dome of the Katabasion. It had begun as a single shrine over the bones of the first legion that had gone down into Tartarus and come back, and over the centuries it had grown into a vast reliquary hall, where pilgrims came down from the ceiling worlds in their thousands to touch the legion's shields. It was a huge building, carved out of the cone's own stone, and it was not the tallest thing on the Acropolis. Higher still stood the Echo Mast. The Mast had been the Arsenal's noosphere relay, the spire that carried every upload on the Flats to Watchpoint Hades, and it stood on the cone's inner ward, cut off from the rest of the Acropolis by a sheer gorge that split the top of the cone in two. Storms of the ceiling geometry played around its tip. From it, a watcher could see the whole of the Flats.
To the watchers on the Mast now, looking at the Third's regiments coming up to the foot of the cone, the Flats below had become a pit of smoke and fire as far as they could see. The guns had been a distant thunder once. Now they were one continuous noise that went all around the cone. Shells struck the slopes or burst against the wards of the Acropolis every minute. Dust and smoke lay over the Flats in thick grey banks that rolled and churned as more shells came down. Down in the fire the killing went on every watch, without stopping. Inside its walls the Acropolis was crammed with everything it needed for war. The last stronghold of the Archflamen's army was full of soldiers and guns. Outside the warded walls, the slopes were covered in gun pits and lookout posts, and every face of the cone was pocked with covered trenches and hidden marksmen's holes. The ground around the foot of the cone was scarred with bunkers and pillboxes and dugouts. The Archflamen's people had worked hard to turn the Acropolis into a rock that the Third's tide would break on, again and again. After rotations of bombing by the Wrath wings and long-range shelling by the biggest guns the Third had, the Acropolis was badly damaged and still standing. Now, in the eighteenth rotation, the end of the siege was going to be fought out.

THE HUNT'S PLAN

The plan for the assault on the cone had existed almost since the siege began. Eighteen rotations earlier, Castorre's staff had known exactly what they would face when the time came. The Arsenal was the Empire's. The Répertoire had every plan and drawing anyone could want, of every change and addition and strengthening the founders and the Directorate had ever made to it, and its records were long and careful. Castorre had planned the assault in detail, and Kastellan had gone over his plans and changed them to suit himself. Now Grace was the third commander to go through the slates and the war-table models of the cone, and the supply figures and the regimental briefings, and change the plan again. Before any orders went out, she called in her contingent and the senior Hunters for a full review of it.
The operation was enormous and intricate. A million soldiers would attack in phases, to wear the defenders down a step at a time and then take and hold the key points. The worst problem was the ground. No armour could be used in the last assaults. The slopes were too steep for tanks, and only just passable for infantry, and that would funnel the attackers into the killing grounds the Archflamen had prepared for them. However the planners looked at it, the last push into the Acropolis would be the bloodiest part of the bloodiest siege in the theatre. For most of the million, the climb up the cone would be their last battle. For many of them it would be their last battle of any kind, since the Echo Mast stood at the top of it.
The first priority was the wards. The Acropolis's wards were fed by the heat-wells under the cone, which the founders had sunk to the edge of the Phlegethon, and they were the key to its strength. The founders had built them on the same principle as a Behemoth's, and the whole cone was wrapped in them. Every stretch of wall and every tower and buttress had its own ward-stones. While the wards stood, there was little chance of doing real harm to the stone of the Acropolis. Grace had no doubts about it. She meant to pour enough fire onto the cone that there would not be one stone of it left standing on another when she had finished. The Directorate would complain and the College would protest bitterly about the destruction of the Katabasion, but it would be a necessary sacrifice to burn out something far worse that was growing on the top of that hill.
The wards could be overloaded, but only when enough hits forced the heat-wells feeding them to cut out or blow. Overloading each ward and keeping it down was the job of the siege guns. The Phaethons that the Directorate had left the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx could hammer the cone every watch with salvoes that would, everyone hoped, be too much for the ward-stones. They could only do it if enough shells were brought up, so the Ordnance Phalanx was given first call on every shell that came down the cables. The regiments' own guns would go short. That meant fewer shells for preparatory barrages, smoke, counter-battery and rolling barrages, and fewer for answering a counter-attack, all of which would leave the breachers more exposed. But while the wards stood, no assault in the world would get through the walls. The regiments would have to rely more on their lighter field guns, and on the guns of their Thureos tanks, which would have nothing else to do once the breachers started up the slopes.
The next objective was the three main gates. With the wards down and the guns turning the inside of the Acropolis into ruins, the breachers would sweep up the slopes and take the gates, and the gates would be the stepping stones for the next waves into the cone. That would be the hardest and most thankless work of all, crossing open ground under fire into the strongest defences the Archflamen had. Everyone knew the cohorts would be ground up on the slopes, against defenders dug in deep and behind walls so thick that nothing but a direct hit from the biggest shells would touch them. It would be murder, and the planners could see no other way. Someone had to climb that hill and die. Others would have to climb after them, over them, and be cut down in their turn, and so it would go on, as the Third crawled closer to the gates, one body at a time. Grace had to decide who would be given the job.
She gave it to the Twenty-Second Trench Regiment, under Colonel Imre Saldane. Saldane had come up through the ranks of the Twenty-Second over the whole of the siege, and on the Flats his name had become something of a legend. As a centurion he had led the attack that broke the outer belt, sixteen rotations before, and had been cut off and fought for three cycles without help, and been wounded five times in that fight alone. In the rotations since, he had risen to command the regiment. His predecessor had been killed in the line when a mortar round took the top of his head off. The piece of skull hung in a reliquary on the regimental standard now. Saldane had gone on leading from the front, and had been wounded another four times. He had transferred twice. A Laistrygon's slag-rake had taken his left arm in the Kastellan Offensive, when he drove the Twenty-Second's salient into the inner belt on Leaf 54-45, and he had come back from Hades with a bronze one and led the regiment through the Cinder Wall. Now his regiment would go in whole, in the first attempt on the three gates. Grace called him to her bunker and gave him his orders. He must have known at once what they meant. He did not argue. He saluted.
Grace's own note of the meeting records that she told Saldane plainly what she was asking of the regiment and did not pretend it was anything else. Saldane thanked her for that, and undertook that the Twenty-Second would take the Founders' Gate.

THE TWENTY-SECOND'S PLAN

Saldane had been handed the cup. Now he had to drink it. First he built his regiment up for the fight. More shells and rounds and fuel and soldiers were drawn from the dumps and brought forward. Then he gathered his staff and studied the models and the plans the Lieutenant had given him. From the forward trenches that now ringed the cone, the Twenty-Second would attack from three directions at once. From the north it would go for the Flamen Gate. From the west it would take the Slag Gate and destroy the works outside it, and the ceiling-lance silo that guarded the approach to the Pilgrim Gate above. The heaviest blow would come from the south-east, against the Founders' Gate. Each attack would be led by a whole cohort in fifteen Hoplons, the First Cohort from the north, the Seventh from the west, and the Fifteenth from the south-east.
The attack could only start after the Ordnance Phalanx's barrage had done its work. Then, as the barrage moved onto the cone itself, Saldane would send in the Hoplons. They would carry the leading cohorts through the last of the outer works and only put them down when the slope became too steep to drive. The breachers would go on uphill on foot, under the fire of any tanks that could find a place to shoot from at the foot of the cone. When the Hoplon cohorts stalled, a second wave on foot would be close behind them, to go through them and on up the slope. The third wave would do the same. The fourth wave would hold most of the regiment's shock breachers, and it would be these soldiers who carried the attack to the gates and fought hand to hand for the gate-towers. On the switchback road to the Founders' Gate, Saldane meant to send up Hephaestean dozer-crawlers to clear the road of mines, so that a company of Thureos could climb it in single file and support the shock breachers at the gate. Fresh cohorts would be waiting at the foot of the road to go straight up it and hold the gate once it was taken.
The Founders' Gate was the main effort and got the most soldiers. The other two gates were much harder. At each of them the attack would be a strong feint, with enough soldiers and guns behind it to hold the defenders in place, and perhaps, if the defenders wavered, to take the gate after all. But it was at the Founders' Gate that Saldane hoped to win a foothold. If he did, his task was done, and another regiment would take over the assault. By then the Twenty-Second would be finished. The transfers would be enormous. He knew that was what the Third was for.

THE SCREE. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 81

At last the end was in sight. If the cycle went well and the Twenty-Second took its objectives, the end would be within reach. The light came up off the Phlegethon, hard to see through the low smoke and the rumble that might have been the ceiling storms or the guns. Nobody could mistake the opening of the barrage. The Tenth Ordnance Phalanx, dug in on the south side of the Demus Gorge, had spent many watches preparing for this fire mission. It would be sustained and accurate, concentrated and shattering, every gun that could be found now trained on the cone and the Acropolis on top of it. As the order to fire came down, the gunners pulled their lanyards and the guns went off together. The shells went up and over, screaming. The divine mortar rounds landed first, fast and high. A few seconds behind them came the heavier rounds of the aedificant batteries, with their deep falling whine. Last came the Phaethon shells, each one coming down out of the dark under the ceiling with a noise like a loaded ore train going past. A thousand shells fell in the first minute.
Saldane and his staff watched the Acropolis and its slopes disappear into a cloud of fire and smoke. Shell after shell added to it, and the ring of fire around the cone grew thicker. It did not seem possible that anything could live through it. Around the watching officers, soldiers in grey coats were climbing into the Hoplons by section and century and cohort, while the carriers idled and filled the air with their exhaust. Each section was counted aboard in a clatter of boots on steel ramps. The centurions and the provosts pushed each soldier into place. Then the rams hissed, and the ramps came up and locked with a clang. Sealed in their steel boxes, the next thing the soldiers inside would see would be the foot of the cone and the fire on it.
On time, Saldane gave the order to attack. Seconds later the Hoplons' engines roared, and their tracks bit, and they crawled forward, picking up speed slowly, the soldiers inside thrown about against each other. It had begun, and every cohort had its part to play. Saldane had set it moving, and now he had to let it run. Behind the Hoplons came more cohorts on foot, still in ordered lines, each section spaced evenly from the next, and the Thureos tanks with their commanders riding out of the hatches to pick a route. Soon their guns would be adding to the barrage in front of them. The field guns followed among more cohorts, and Kentauros carriers bounced across the churned ground behind them. Then came the shock breachers, some in carriers, some on foot, bent under their heavy plate. The whole regiment was moving. Only the big guns stayed where they were, and they would be firing soon. Saldane had committed everything he had. He kept back three cohorts and his outriders as a reserve, and he would commit them too if a breakthrough came. It was everything or nothing.
All three attacks met the hard resistance everyone expected. Even in the thick smoke and the bursting shells, the Hoplons drew heavy fire. The leading carrier of the Seventh Cohort took a hit in the side from a hidden gun. The shell went through its troop bay and through everyone in it, and out of the far side, and left a ragged hole. A second shell hit it too, and burst inside.
The First Cohort ran into a minefield, and though its Hoplons pushed on, several were left burning. From high on the cone, a ceiling-lance fired flat down the slope, and every beam tore a long black scar in the ground. One Hoplon, then a second, then a third disappeared in a flash of light and running metal.
On the Fifteenth Cohort's front the fire was taking its toll of the Hoplons too. One had its driver's cab torn off by a tank round. Another slewed sideways as its tracks were shredded by a shell, and stuck. The soldiers inside climbed out into heavy fire and scattered into cover or lay where they fell.
When the ramps came down, every century got the same treatment. Heavy guns swept the ground. More hidden minefields blocked the way. The Archflamen's mortars came down on pre-ranged targets with terrible accuracy, and whole sections died in a storm of bursts and rounds. Centurion Petra Oss, leading the Seventh Cohort, was dead, and so was her whole command section, all of them killed when a bunker everyone thought was destroyed opened fire on them at point-blank range. Her soldiers crawled on toward the road up to the Slag Gate, leaving bodies behind them. The way forward was covered in bodies and abandoned Hoplons.
Against the First Cohort the Archflamen counter-attacked, and the first wave was thrown back in hand-to-hand fighting. It had hardly begun to climb toward the Flamen Gate. The second wave went in with bayonets fixed and turned the fight again, and the Archflamen's soldiers were cut down or fell back, but the slopes had still not been reached and two waves had been used up. The third wave won another few hundred metres in a fast rush, but it was still well short of the Flamen Gate when the guns and mortars raked it and broke it into a crowd. Centurion Dagny Holm of the Nineteenth Cohort tried to push on uphill, and her cohort went the way of the first waves. Fewer than a hundred soldiers came back out of a cohort of seven hundred and forty. Holm was not one of them. Her implant had been in reach of a relay the whole time. The Echo Mast had been closer.
While the northern and western attacks stalled, the attack on the Founders' Gate did little better. It was just as brave and no more successful. The Hoplons had taken the worst of the fire. Eight of the fifteen were wrecks now. The rest pulled back, firing up the slope until their guns were empty. The breachers were climbing into a gale of fire that came down on them from above, and all order had gone in the fighting.
On the switchback road the dozer-crawlers had ground their way up, scraping a track through the mines, until lance-fire from the walls left every one of them burning on the road and their crews jumped out of the hatches and ran back downhill. Behind them came a column of Thureos tanks of the Twenty-Second's Fifth Armoured Company, twelve tanks in line astern. They crawled up, their shells bursting against the gate-towers. One by one they were knocked out. The last of them reached the fourth switchback before its crew climbed out of their crippled tank and left it. Burning wrecks lay all the way down the road, and they would block it for any attack that came after.
Ahead of the attack the barrage had now closed onto the cone and was hammering it. The weight of shells had, as planned, overloaded the wards. It could not destroy the heat-wells feeding them, which were buried far down in the cone, under the vaults, and any harm it did would only last as long as the shells kept falling. The guns had begun to tear into the buildings.
The Twenty-Second's soldiers called the slopes of the cone the Scree, after the loose cinder that slid away under their boots when they climbed. By the dark watch of the first cycle, the Scree was covered from top to bottom in the Twenty-Second's dead.

THE GULLY OF ASH. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLES 81 TO 87

West of the Twenty-Second, the Thirty-Fifth Trench Regiment was going into its own battle under Colonel Theron Kastellan.
Its objective was the Gully of Ash, a deep ravine that cut into the western flank of the cone and ran up to the Arsenal's great vault doors, the armoured gates of the magazines under the Acropolis. The plan for clearing the gully was intricate, but every unit in it had been thoroughly briefed, had studied the models and had its orders. The first objective was a natural trench, which the maps called the Seam, that ran across the line of advance. From there the attack would divide. Two forces would go for the strongpoints at the mouth of the gully. A third would swing right and storm the ruins of a ceiling-lance silo that was believed to have been knocked out by the Phaethons months before. Its broken walls still gave the defenders good cover to fire along the main line of advance from, and it had to be taken. A fourth force would then come up and push on into the gully itself and attack the walls and the vault doors, together with the drop of Enforcement's Lictor cohort into the gully. Once the walls were broken, a new attack would clear the rest of the gully and carry the fight up to the doors. The Hephaestean sappers would take over there, to blow the armoured doors and begin clearing the magazines beyond. As each objective fell, fresh cohorts would be released to take over the attack. In all, Grace expected the assault to take three or four cycles of sustained fighting. As always, progress would be slow and hard won.
When the order came, the Thirty-Fifth went up the ladders and forward in good order. At first it went smoothly, tanks and breachers going forward into the smoke that drifted over the ground after another softening barrage by the field guns just behind. A seep-storm was coming down again, deepening the paste under boots and tracks. But the planners had missed something. On the war-table the Seam showed as a single line, and it was thought to be lightly held, a small obstacle for the first wave to overrun. What the staff had not seen was that the Seam was a real trench, a deep crack in the basalt with a steep northern wall, and in many places no tank could cross it. The Archflamen's soldiers had dug deep into its walls, where the guns could not reach them. The Thirty-Fifth's fire plan had been worked out for the gully and for the threat of plunging fire from the cone above, and it had given the Seam only a short shelling by a few batteries of light mortars. From the Seam the defenders could come out to fight the advancing soldiers and slip back into cover. They had also hidden tanks in the bottom of it, with firing positions dug at its edge. As the Thirty-Fifth's tanks came forward, the Archflamen's armour would roll up into perfect hull-down positions and fire, and then roll back and move before anyone could fire back. The Seam alone was going to be a far harder fight than anyone had planned for.
So it proved. The defenders waited and opened fire only at short range, with a sudden storm that tore the leading centuries apart. Thureos tanks blew up from flanking shots out of tanks no one could see. Five tanks were burning within seconds, and the rest backed away rather than join them. The attack stopped dead. Then the mortars began to land, the air full of fragments over the heads of the soldiers lying in the ash. The commander of the Third Cohort was killed by a heavy gun. Six of his eleven centurions died in the first volleys. Despite the losses the attack had to go on, and it did. There was no way around the Seam, so the only thing to do was to go straight at it, and it made little progress.
Kastellan had the guns brought down onto the Seam in a hurry, but the defenders were well dug in and quickly found shelter under its lip. The three Colossi with the regiment found no way across, and then lost two of their number to demolition charges thrown by men no one saw, out of the crack. More cohorts were called forward, but it was not until the second cycle that the Thirty-Fifth forced its way into the Seam by numbers alone and began to clear it with grenades and bayonets. In it they found thralls and Laistrygons and garrison soldiers side by side, and among them Frost Masons, who had turned a small crack in the ground into another killing field. On the right of the attack, the Sixteenth Cohort made the first crossing. Its task had been the attack on the ruined silo, but most of its ten centuries had been badly hurt in the fight for the Seam already. It did not have the guns to put down the strongpoint in the silo, and its attacks on it soon failed.
Only by the end of the third cycle was an objective that should have been taken in the first hours in the Thirty-Fifth's hands, with more soldiers coming up to make it a base of fire for the next move. The mouth of the gully was guarded by twin strongpoints, each a tangle of pillboxes and bunkers and trenches and gun pits. The Phaethons were already shelling them, and the tanks in the Seam were firing on them directly, but with the attack behind its timetable there was no time for a long barrage. It was time to go again, and new lines of the Thirty-Fifth's soldiers came up out of the Seam and went south.
The assault that was meant to take three cycles was in its fifth before any of the Third got into the gully itself. On the right the attack on the silo had failed again, and Kastellan had asked for a Behemoth to help with a third try. Even the arrival of the Steropes-pattern Hammer of Lemnos could not get the breachers onto it.
In the gully the Thirty-Fifth found itself in a nightmare of trenches and minefields, with tank traps as thick as trees in a forest to keep the tanks out. The Frost Masons had been at work again, and the advance toward the vault doors went a few metres at a time. The fire from the walls and the towers swept the road up the gully, which was nothing but craters now. The Archflamen's soldiers fought for every hole. Behind the Thirty-Fifth the shells were running short. The attack had outrun the shells set aside for it, and the fire that was meant to keep the cone's defenders down had to slacken. The result was murderous plunging fire from the top of the gully, and there was no cover from it anywhere. Cohort after cohort used itself up in the gully for nothing. The Thirty-Fifth's soldiers called it the Kiln.
It was the seventh cycle of the battle before Grace gave the order for the Lictor drop. It had been meant for the second cycle, and put off, and put off again. When the order finally came, Enforcement's Lictor cohort went down into the gully with four Hunters of the Director's household, in drop-caskets that came out of the dark under the ceiling ahead of a wave of fire-caskets that sprayed the landing ground. In the low smoke and with the lip of the gully blocking their view, the Thirty-Fifth's officers could not see the Lictors land, and missed the moment for the push on the walls and the doors that was meant to go in from both sides at once. The four Hunters and almost a hundred and fifty Lictors came down into a trap. They were cut off from any help, in a cauldron of fire with no way out. It quickly became clear that the Archflamen had expected exactly this and had a counter-attack waiting.
The great vault doors opened, and out of them walked a drowned Behemoth. Around its feet came a swarm of thralls and Hollowed and things out of the river, all crying out and desperate to earn their masters' reward. They flung themselves at the Lictors, who shot them down without mercy. Hundreds died, but they were only the first wave, the throwaways that let the real killers come close. Again it was the Cinderborn, and the Red Furnace, and again Draugos was at the front of them with his champions.
Against a drowned Behemoth, even Lictor plate gave little protection. Cut off in their own battle, the Lictors called for help, and at last the Thirty-Fifth was ordered to attack the doors in a desperate effort to break in to them. A Cyclopean lance-tank burned the doors through with shot after shot, but the Thirty-Fifth could not force its way inside. A charge by the whole of the regiment's outriders was shot down to the last rider. Now the last reserve for the assault was gone.
Without relief, the Lictor cohort was wiped out. All of them were lost, and the four Hunters with them. The ones who were lucky were killed by the Cinderborn. Those who were wounded or taken were dragged away by Draugos and handed to the Kindlers, who knew how to open a living body to what lived under the river. The Lictors would be offered up as hosts, and they would come back onto the Flats in a few cycles, changed past knowing, as the Kindled.
The bad news for the Thirty-Fifth did not end with the failure in the gully, or at the silo, or with the destruction of the Lictor drop. Out to the north-east, in one of the many small attacks that had gone in to support the main effort, its Seventeenth Cohort had been working through the edge of the ruined forges at the foot of the cone when it walked into an ambush. The Unremembered had come up into the bombed forges, probably by passages under the ground that nobody on the Third's side knew about, and laid a trap. They surrounded the whole cohort and wiped it out before anyone could be spared from the other attacks to help. More than seven hundred soldiers were lost. Most of them had not been killed. They had been taken back into the Acropolis alive, for whatever the Unremembered did with the living. The Third had learned what that was. It was called the Hollowed.
On the eighth cycle, the Thirty-Fifth was coming apart in the Kiln. Two-thirds of its breachers were dead, and of the dead, the relays were logging one in four as null. The Grey was coming down into the gully on every Arsenal barrage, and every soldier who died in it went to Hades empty, or went nowhere and then stood up again in the ash and turned around. The drowned Behemoth was walking down the gully toward the Seam, burning everything in front of it with its Phlegethon-cannon. Behind it came the Red Furnace. The Third's relay bunker for the gully had taken a direct hit on the sixth cycle and the regiment's fire net was down, so every battery that could still fire was firing blind. On the Scree, what was left of the Twenty-Second was being pushed off the slope. Kastellan, who had been a Marshal once, stood in the Seam with a rifle and gave the order that every commander on the Flats had learned to dread. Fall back.

THE LANTERN. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88. THE DARK WATCH

In the dark watch of the eighty-eighth cycle, a vessel crossed Depth 40 low under the ceiling, through a crack in the ceiling geometry that no chart of the Empire listed.
It was a lantern of the Dominion, a slender craft of gold lacquer and thin hull, shaped like the lamp of a Shinto shrine, and it was running for the deep crossings into Egyptian space with its own lamp lit. Its course had not been meant to bring it near the Flats. It was the only light under the ceiling that was not on fire, and the ceiling-lances on the cone found it at once. The first beam missed. The second took off the lantern's tail. The lantern turned over in the air and came down burning onto the upper Scree, slid two hundred metres down the cinder with its lamp still lit, and came to rest against the wreck of a Hoplon at the head of the Gully of Ash.
The lantern carried two passengers. They were the pair the Hunt's files had last placed on the Themis Needle in Interlude I, the boy and the girl who escaped Lieutenant Grace there. After the Needle, the Director had marked the boy with her silver crescent in Interlude II, and he had paid for the mark in days of his own memory. A Dominion lantern had then taken the pair aboard, and the Dominion's shrine-vats had healed them completely. The boy had both arms and both eyes again. His Aegis was whole. The intelligence wearing him, which the Hunt's files name Minerva, was running at full capacity for the first time since Chapter 15. The girl was Verrill Chase, a chemist and relay engineer of the Chase family.
The first witness to see them was Centurion Liane Marsh of the Thirty-Fifth's Ninth Cohort, sheltering behind a basalt slab at the top of the gully with two soldiers and a heavy gun. Her report describes a boy walking down the Scree out of the fire upright, through the Archflamen's guns, with a girl running bent double behind him. The rounds fired at them stopped in the air short of the boy and dropped smoking onto the cinder. He gave them no attention. Marsh's estimate was that he took about a second to read the ground in front of him: the Scree, the gully, the drowned Behemoth walking down it, the cone and the Echo Mast above.
The boy dropped into cover beside Marsh and asked for her relay team. The regiment's relay bunker had taken a Phaethon round on the sixth cycle, and its team was dead. Marsh pointed him to a relay set still strapped to a dead signaller in a pit ten metres down the slope. He did not remove the set. He put his bare hand on its casing and crouched there for about two seconds.
Every slate in the Thirty-Fifth's line lit at once.

THE FIRE NET. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88

The fire net of the Thirty-Fifth Trench Regiment came back up all at once, under a new authority that no battery commander could trace. It was not the regiment's, nor the Seventh Line Phalanx's, nor the Hunt's. It carried no seal. It did carry a target list, and every battery on the western front of the cone that still had shells received its share of the list in the same instant, with firing solutions worked out and a time on target. The list covered the Seam, the twin strongpoints, the gully, the ruined silo and the Behemoth, with every gun pit on the western face that had fired in the last six cycles marked to the metre. Battery commanders who asked who was firing received the same answer.

FIRE. ON MY TIME. QUESTIONS AFTER.

Most of them fired. The rest found that their guns had been laid for them, with solutions on their slates better than any they could have worked out, and after the first salvo they fired too.
Forty batteries fired together, and every shell arrived in the same second on targets that had been engaged for six cycles without effect. The twin strongpoints at the mouth of the gully were destroyed. The ruined silo, which the Sixteenth Cohort had failed to take three times, collapsed into its own magazine. Gun pit after gun pit on the western face fell silent, the shells having come down through their slits. The Archflamen's hull-down tanks in the Seam were hit in the four seconds between rolling up and rolling back, by mortar rounds fired forty seconds earlier at where they were going to be. Colonel Kastellan, standing in the Seam with a rifle, asked over his set who was firing. No one on the Third's side could tell him.
The boy then told Marsh that the regiment's retreat would stop, and that she should tell her soldiers so. He informed her that the Behemoth was his and ordered her to keep her head down.

GREY SEVENTH

Verrill Chase had stayed behind the slab with Marsh. Her attention was not on the Behemoth. She had found a residue on the cinder in a nearby crater, where the last Grey Seventh shell had burst, and she was examining it through a loupe.
Grey Seventh was a proscribed Lethe distillate. The Arsenal had held stocks of it in its vaults, and the Archflamen's gunners had been firing it since the Lethe Cut four rotations before. The Third's technical officers had concluded that it emptied the uploads of the soldiers who breathed it, and it was the main cause of the null transfers in the later rotations of the siege. Marsh overheard Chase's analysis and later gave it to the Hunt in full. Chase concluded that the Grey emptied nothing. It was a memory solvent. It dissolved the binding that held a soldier's memories in the shape of a person, so that the implant uploaded all of the person in no order at all. The uploads arriving at Hades were scrambled rather than blank.
Chase identified the counter in the same conversation. A binder that fixed a pattern faster than the Lethe could dissolve it would stop the effect in the lungs, and Mnemosyne salt was the exact opposite of the Lethe distillate. Mnemosyne salt was used for lineage-keying, the process of fixing a bloodline into a shell at the breech, which made it a standard store of any foundry casting divine mortar rounds. The Arsenal had made those rounds for eight thousand years. Minerva placed the nearest stock in a forward magazine of the Arsenal in the Seam, three hundred metres away, which the Thirty-Fifth had taken on the second cycle without knowing what it held. She asked Chase whether she could build a filter from it. Chase asked for an hour. Minerva gave her twenty minutes.

THREE SECONDS

Minerva then asked Marsh how long the Third's soldiers had to reach a relay after death. Marsh gave the standard window of seven seconds. Minerva asked about the Mast, and Marsh did not answer. Every soldier on the Flats knew that the uploads went up into the noosphere, that the Echo Mast was the nearest relay, and that the Mast took them.
Minerva's assessment, as Marsh recorded it, was that seven seconds was too long. The Third's implants were built to upload once at death through the nearest relay, and on the Flats the nearest relay was the enemy's. With her hand back on the dead signaller's set, Minerva changed the cadence. From that moment every implant in the Third sent its state to Watchpoint Hades every three seconds, continuously, rather than once at death. A soldier who died in the Kiln would already be at Hades in all but the last three seconds of his life, and there would be nothing left in him for the Mast to catch. Marsh objected that the implants were AtRep's and could not be altered. Minerva told her that the Répertoire would know in eleven seconds.
At Watchpoint Hades, thirty-seven depths above the Flats, the Répertoire du Savoir Athénien recorded every implant on the Cinder Flats changing cadence at once, through an authority it did not recognise and could not refuse.

CODE 18 – Speculum Falsum. Origin: Répertoire du Savoir Athénien, Watchpoint Hades. Subject: Unauthorised cadence change, all implants, Depth 40.
Verdict: Pending.

The vat-crews at the Watchpoint noticed the difference within the hour. Soldiers coming back out of the vats from the Flats came back whole. The relay crews on the Flats noticed it sooner. The null rate in the Kiln, which had been one in four, fell to nothing and stayed there for the rest of the siege.

THE KILN. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 88

By then the drowned Behemoth had walked a third of the way down the gully. It was one of the Forge's oldest patterns, crusted black with river growth, and its Phlegethon-cannon fired every forty seconds and set the floor of the gully burning. The Red Furnace and the Kindled came behind it under Draugos's banner. The Thirty-Fifth's breachers were running ahead of it.
Minerva walked down the gully toward the Behemoth at an ordinary pace. The fire from the walls that had come down on the Thirty-Fifth for six cycles came down on her and stopped in the air in front of her. The Third's soldiers saw her Aegis only as a shimmer, like heat over the Flats, a hand's breadth from her body. Breachers running back up the gully saw her go past. Several stopped. A few turned back.
Above the gully, on the western face of the cone, stood the Arsenal's quench-tanks. The founders had built them eight thousand years before to cool the casting lines of the Acropolis. They were cisterns of Cocytus brine the size of hab-blocks, piped down from the ice holds above Depth 40 and kept cold by their own wards. Neither side had treated them as part of the war, and the siege had left them untouched. Minerva had found them in the Répertoire's plans of the Arsenal within her first second on the Scree. She reached into the Arsenal's own machine net, which the founders had built and the Archflamen's people had never fully understood, and opened the valves.
The brine came down the western face of the cone in a white sheet and poured down the gully, freezing as it fell. The Phlegethon fire on the gully floor went out under it in a scream of steam. The river-fire in the Behemoth's cannon went out, and the Phlegethon-blades of the Red Furnace went black in their hands. The Kindled faltered. The brine ran up the Behemoth's legs and froze, and the engine stopped, held to the knees in grey ice with its cannon cold. Marsh's report notes that the brine did not run as water should have run. It ran where the boy was looking. Marsh did not include this in her first report and gave it to the Hunt only on direct questioning.
With the Behemoth's wards down, the Thirty-Fifth's batteries, now on the new net, put eleven Phaethon rounds into its chest in nine seconds. It broke open and fell back against the wall of the gully as its reactor died.
Minerva then addressed the Thirty-Fifth over every set in the regiment at once. The regimental log records the order verbatim: "Thirty-Fifth. The fire is mine now. Your dead are going home. Forward." Kastellan, in the Seam, repeated the order to the regiment on his own authority.

MNEMOSYNE

Chase had the forward magazine open in four minutes. The Thirty-Fifth had logged it as shells and casting stores and left a guard on it. Behind the casting stores lay eleven tonnes of Mnemosyne salt in sealed jars, each stamped with the lineage keys of the casting lines.
By the time Minerva came back out of the gully, Chase had set a section of the Thirty-Fifth's sappers to knocking the bottoms out of mask canisters and a line of breachers to packing them with salt and charcoal from the magazine's own stores. A pile of finished filters stood on an ammunition crate. The sappers' diary records her correcting a Hephaestean sapper-major on the packing. The filter had to be loose enough to breathe through, or the soldiers would take their masks off, and it required the fine grade of salt, the coarse grade being for the large calibres.
Chase wrote the recipe onto a signaller's slate in ninety seconds, with a drawing of the canister, and asked for it to be sent to every regiment and every captured magazine on the Flats. Every regiment had been capturing Mnemosyne salt for eighteen rotations without knowing its use. The signaller asked under whose authority it should go. Minerva gave her own, and the signaller sent it.
Within the hour, the first Grey Seventh barrage of the night came down on the Thirty-Fifth in the Kiln. The soldiers in the gully had Chase's filters in their masks. They breathed the Grey and went on fighting, and they kept their names.
The sappers' diary notes that the filters were delivered two minutes late on Minerva's twenty-minute deadline, and that Minerva pointed this out to Chase.

THE TURNING. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLES 88 TO 90

The rout in the Kiln stopped in the dark watch of the eighty-eighth cycle. By the turn of the next watch it had become an advance.
The Thirty-Fifth went back down the gully behind its own barrage, which now came down to the metre where it was wanted and moved forward with the breachers. The Kindled met them at the head of the gully, and the Thirty-Fifth found that the brine had affected them in a way the Kindlers had not planned for. They had been made with river-fire. Some were still burning. Others had been returned to something like the soldiers they had been, and these sat down in the ice and wept. The Red Furnace fought on, and Draugos's champions killed many of the Thirty-Fifth in the gully that cycle. Without their Phlegethon-blades and the fire behind them they were only warriors, and the Third had always had more warriors than the deep. Draugos pulled the Red Furnace back up through the burned-out vault doors into the Acropolis. The Thirty-Fifth followed them to the doors and held them.
On the Scree, the Twenty-Second found fire coming down in front of it again where it was needed, and stopped falling back. Saldane had lost two-thirds of his regiment. He held the lower Scree with what remained, and when the new net gave his guns a target list he had not asked for, he did not ask whose it was. Every soldier of the Twenty-Second who died on the Scree after the dark watch of the eighty-eighth cycle went home whole.
The Répertoire's figures for the assault set out the effect of the intervention.

Assault on the Acropolis, Cycles 81 to 87: 160,000 transfers. Null: 39,000.
Assault on the Acropolis, Cycles 88 to 90: 61,000 transfers. Null: 0.

The Third's command could not say who had done it. The Répertoire logged the authority on the net as UNREGISTERED, CLASS UNKNOWN, CARRYING NO SEAL, and its CODE 18 query was still unanswered at the end of the rotation. The soldiers of the Thirty-Fifth said it was a boy who had come down off the Scree out of a burning lantern. Most of the Third did not believe them.

THE FIRST VICTORY. ROTATION EIGHTEEN, CYCLE 89

On the Slag Gate spur, west of the Kiln, Grace watched the bombers go over.
Eighty heavy Wraths flew over her in waves, their engines a deep drone under the ceiling, their running lights crossing the smoke like slow sparks. As she watched, the first wave dropped its loads on the spur, which vanished at once into a cauldron of smoke and fire that boiled up off the slope. The next wave was already coming in, and more bombs followed the first. Then hundreds of shells joined in, and the spur was hammered with every explosive the Third could throw at it. Five more waves of bombers came over after the first.
As the last Wrath banked away and its engines faded, the smoke and dust over the spur began to clear. Atlanta passed her a scope. In at least four places the wall of the spur had been breached. At least two towers were piles of smoking rubble with fires still burning deep inside them. The Slag Gate itself still stood, scorched black, but standing. Above it the parapets had been smashed and the battlements had crumbled. Inside their bunkers many of the defenders had been crushed by the falling stone, and others had been deafened by the bombing. More were crawling up out of the rubble and digging their way back into the open.
It had been a terrible thing to watch, and the first wave of the Twenty-Sixth now got up to begin its assault. Its objective was still the wall, even though much of it was rubble, since enough of it was left to make a good place for the survivors to fight from. They did fight. For all the destruction, the bombing did little good. The survivors burrowed into the rubble and made new firing positions in it. The heavy guns that had lived through it were set up among the broken stone, and gunners and marksmen were soon perched on the shattered parapets. The southern bastion of the ceiling-lance silo, the strongest work on the spur, had come through mostly whole and would soon be firing again.
On the word, the Twenty-Sixth's assault cohorts went forward with tank companies and Colossi among the centuries, and with them walked Cradle-Lord Varne's Anvil of Talos and two Steropes, the Bellows of the Forge and the Wakeful. Two more Steropes were close by. Grace watched the first waves climb toward the Slag Gate spur until they disappeared into the dust and smoke.
During the assault Hunt Second Celyn twice brought Grace a signal from the Thirty-Fifth concerning the Kiln. Both times Grace deferred it.
Despite the bombing the defenders still had plenty of fight, and soon the first wave had slowed to a crawl. But the weight of armour, nearly a hundred tanks in that wave alone, and the fire of the Behemoths with their missile racks and their rotary cannon, were breaking up what was left of the walls and the defence lines. It was only a matter of time before the Third's guns overwhelmed the dug-in defenders and opened a road for the breachers. The tanks found it hard going. The bombing had cratered the ground and many of them stuck in it and became pillboxes until their ammunition ran out. From Grace's bunker, the attack seemed to be working, slowly. The first centuries were fighting in the rubble of the walls now, clearing them one stretch at a time. The Anvil of Talos pulled back to rearm, and the Bellows of the Forge supported the main push. On the right, the Wakeful led the attack on the silo's southern bastion. Then came the first serious setback. The bastion was badly scarred, but the lance buried inside it had lived through the bombing. As the Wakeful fired its missiles at the bastion, the bastion fired back. The first beam overloaded the Wakeful's last wards. The second went straight through the Behemoth's front plate into its reactor. The Wakeful staggered and stopped, and its guns went quiet. Then its reactor blew. It went up in a ball of white fire so bright that everyone who saw it carried the shape of it on their eyes for a cycle, and so hot that nothing within a hundred metres lived through it. When the cloud had cleared there was nothing left of the Behemoth at all. There was only black scorched ground for a hundred metres in every direction, and a few small pieces of melted wreck, which were all the Forge's crews could find to take home. Another of Talos's engines was gone.
Grace ordered more guns onto the bastion, and soon a barrage of Phaethon rounds was hammering it. Then, with her contingent close behind her, she left her bunker for the front line.
At the front the Twenty-Sixth's soldiers were still pushing forward. In places they had almost reached the walls, and in others they had been stopped and pinned. Grace took direct command of the attack on the silo bastion. She called off the Phaethons and ordered the leading centuries and the shock breachers with them to charge. By the dark watch they were inside, fighting in the tunnels and galleries in the rock. Grace and her contingent led the close fighting, and it was Grace who went into the lance chamber in a line of white fire and put a bolt of lightning into the lance's heart, and blew it out of the cone.
While the Twenty-Sixth drove the defenders out of their holes and took the spur wall one stretch at a time, the Archflamen counter-attacked. As in the first assault on the Founders' Gate, his soldiers came out of the gate above, the Pilgrim Gate this time, high on the cone, and came pouring down the Pilgrim Stair. Tanks and walkers came with them, and the furnace-engines of the Cinderborn, things of iron that had been made to scream, and the last of the Brethren of the Black Shale, who had bent the knee to Draugos and had something to prove.
At first their attack swept the Twenty-Sixth back from the wall, and then the survivors rallied, and fresh waves of breachers with massed tank fire behind them went up the slope of rubble again. The fight for the wall was savage. It changed hands six times as the battle went on through the cycle and into the dark watch. It was a night of hellish fighting against the Cinderborn's furnace-engines. The air was full of their screaming, which was the screaming of the people who had been put inside them. By the turn of the watch the rubble was back in the Archflamen's hands, and four of the Twenty-Sixth's cohorts had been broken in the counter-attacks. Bodies covered the rubble and filled the craters. Every one of those soldiers had gone home whole. The Twenty-Sixth's relay team told Grace that at the turn of the watch, and she did not understand it, and had no time to.
On the second cycle, as the Twenty-Sixth tried again to take the spur back, Grace called in her own reserve. Shield-Captain Roe's company and Flame-Captain Tharn's came down from the Selene's Arrow. They came down the Pilgrim Stair from above, out of the moon-ship's translation chambers, and hit the Archflamen's counter-attack from behind in a blaze of silver fire. The Sixth Moonwall had been trained for one thing for ten thousand years, and the furnace-engines were it. Their spears burned with a cold light. The flame hunters went into the engines and through them, and the things inside the engines went quiet.
Still in the thick of it, Grace gathered the survivors around her for the next push as the Moonwall struck. She went up the rubble slope yelling at the Twenty-Sixth to follow her, which it did. They came to the top of the tall heaps of rubble again. Beyond them was the sheer southern face of the cone, with the Pilgrim Stair going across it and up under the walls to the Pilgrim Gate. Up that stair the last of the Archflamen's soldiers now fell back, still firing wildly. The Moonwall's strike had tipped the balance and thrown them back. In a sudden storm of cold fire, the furnace-engines had been burned out. When Grace looked around her, the spur was a scene of complete destruction. The Slag Gate spur was nearly gone. The towers had fallen and the walls had been ground to stone and dust. Wrecked tanks and engines lay across the cratered ground, many still burning like beacons in the smoke. The dead of both sides lay everywhere. But after two cycles of fighting, what was left of the spur was the Third's. Here the Archflamen's counter-attack had been beaten, and the Third had won a place to attack the Pilgrim Gate from.
Only once the spur was secure did Grace take Celyn's slate. It held the Thirty-Fifth's report, the Répertoire's figures, the unanswered CODE 18 query and a single image from a centurion's helm camera, taken through smoke and steam, of a boy walking down the Gully of Ash in front of a frozen Behemoth. The steam hid his face. Grace ordered that the centurion be brought to her.
