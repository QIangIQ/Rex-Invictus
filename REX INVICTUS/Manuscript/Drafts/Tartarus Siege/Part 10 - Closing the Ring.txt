THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART TEN. CLOSING THE RING

"To fight the thing that eats the soul and come away whole, you keep your purpose narrow. Each of us conquers herself first. Then the rest."
Hunt-Elder Melaina Sarn, to the Hunters of the Third, before the cleansing of the Arsenal

THE MOONWALL COMES DOWN. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 101

On the hundred and first cycle of Rotation Sixteen, the moon-ship Selene's Arrow came down through the high geometry over the Flats, eleven cycles out from Luna. She was one of the fastest ships the Empire had, heavily armed for her size, and her translation chambers could put several squads on the ground at once. Only the Hunt flew ships like her. Her pilots came from the Hunt's own houses of navigators, who had been bound to the Director by oath since before the Empire had a name, and who could take a ship through the deep geometry by feel, on routes no Fleet pilot would try. With them the Hunt could put a strike force anywhere in the Empire faster than anyone else. Now the first of the Sixth Moonwall's companies had come to the Flats under Shield-Captain Antheia Roe.
The first problem Grace and her new staff had to face was the one Kastellan had been fighting for most of a rotation. Anyone could see it on the war table. The siege could not be finished while the east was open. The ring around the Acropolis had a hole in it, and while it did, the Archflamen had a way out. Even if the Third took the cone, the war might go on somewhere else, if the Archflamen's people could leave it and fight again in the deep holds. The open flank also tied down the Fifth Line Phalanx, which had to guard the back of the First Line Phalanx against attack from the east. Good regiments and good guns were sitting on the defensive.
Grace studied the problem at the war table for most of a watch. Her order was to close the ring.
The four regiments of the Fifth Line Phalanx would attack in echelon, the Twenty-Second in the north first, then the Twenty-Fourth, the Twenty-Sixth, and last the Twenty-Eighth in the south. They would swing south and then south-west and close the ring around the Acropolis by joining hands with the Thirty-Fifth at Leaf 57-44, the southernmost regiment on the Seventh Line Phalanx's front. The Thirty-Fifth was Kastellan's regiment now. The whole movement would swing like a door on a hinge, and the hinge would be the First Line Phalanx's positions. From the north the attack would have more than sixty kilometres to cover, and the outrider patrols reported that the Unremembered and the Archflamen's Lethe-blooded cohorts held the whole of it. To speed the attack up, the Sixteenth and Twentieth Armoured were attached to the Fifth Line Phalanx to give it an armoured fist for the left hook.
It would take time to organise, and until then it would be the first priority of the Third's staff. The other regiments were to hold their lines and do nothing but raid and patrol until the Acropolis was closed inside a ring of guns. The Hunt expected hard fighting, and expected the Unremembered to come out of the mist to meet the attack. Hunters would go with the offensive. Shield-Captain Roe's companies would wait on the Selene's Arrow as a fast reserve, ready to go wherever the Archflamen was strongest or wherever the things from the pit were found. The attack was set for the hundred and fiftieth cycle.
Two more operations were planned to follow it if it went well. In the first, the three remaining regiments of the First Line Phalanx would push forward to the Cinder Wall on the north side and pin the defenders against it. In the second, the Eleventh Line Phalanx would come down from the north to take the high ground there, a ridge of old cinder vents that the Third's maps called Height 202. The Eleventh was the least experienced of the Third's phalanxes. Its three regiments had been on the Flats for only six rotations. When both operations were done, the ring would have closed up to the Wall all the way round, and the Archflamen would be shut in a pocket that could only get smaller.

THE GREY. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 150

The light off the Phlegethon was low over the Fifth Line Phalanx's trenches when the attack began, and a seep-storm was coming in over the Lethe Cut. Soon it would break and turn the ground to paste again. The Fifth's soldiers were used to it. This time they would not be attacking a fixed line. There was no continuous line in front of them to break. In front of them was ground that had been fought over for rotations, covered in the wrecks of everything that had been fought over it. Old tanks and guns lay half-buried in the ash or tipped into shell holes beside the broken remains of old trenches and pillboxes, and stretches of wire that had once guarded something and now guarded nothing. Worst of all, under the ash there were mines no one had mapped and shells that had never gone off, and either could go up without warning after rotations in the ground.
On the hundred and fiftieth cycle the guns opened up. The Fifth's own batteries put down a rolling barrage with smoke mixed into it to hide the Twenty-Second's first move. The barrage came down with great force on ground that had already been shelled to nothing, and the Twenty-Second's leading cohorts went up the ladders and out. The tanks were close behind them, each company of the Sixteenth Armoured joined to a cohort to make a battle group. Behind the barrage, the advance began, steady and in good order.
At first it went well. Then the Archflamen's counter-barrage came down. Wherever a rolling barrage fell there would be an attack behind it, and the Archflamen's gunners knew it. The mortar rounds came first and then the heavier shells, bursting all around the Twenty-Second. Among the grey smoke of the bursts was another grey, paler and heavier, that did not rise. It was Grey Seventh.
The Third had known Grey Seventh since the last stand of the Fifty-Fourth on the Lethe Cut, when a regiment had died under it and a hundred and nine thousand of its soldiers had gone null and thirty-two thousand more had gone absent. It knew what the shells looked like and what the cloud looked like. It still did not know what the stuff was. Nobody in the Third could say what it did inside a soldier, except that a soldier who breathed it began to forget, and a soldier who died in it went to Hades blank or did not go at all.
The cloud lay across the Twenty-Second's front. Losses were heavy and the attack fell into confusion. Cohorts got lost in the smoke and the Grey. Sections that walked into a thick patch of it simply stopped. Their soldiers stood in the ash and looked around them, and could not say where they were or what they had come there to do, and some of them sat down. The relay at the Twenty-Second's command bunker logged the uploads from the dead in the cloud, and one in three of them arrived empty.
Through it all the tanks kept going. Inside their sealed hulls the crews were safe from the Grey, and now the tanks were the point of the attack. It was a nightmare of a battlefield. Inside the cloud a soldier could see a few metres. Tanks drove into shell holes and old tank ditches they could not see and stuck there. Several Thureos tanks hit mines. Then, through the grey, the Archflamen's cohorts counter-attacked. What followed was a string of small, messy fights inside the cloud. Arsenal tanks fired blind or came in to point-blank range. The Third could not bring tanks and soldiers and guns together inside the Grey, and the Archflamen's crews won a string of quick fights, burning whole squadrons and pulling back. But for all their successes, they did not have enough strength to stop the attack. The counter-attacks could hold it up for a while and cost it soldiers and tanks, but its weight kept it moving. Where the Grey thinned, the Twenty-Second's soldiers pushed on against scattered resistance. By the end of the first cycle they had taken ten kilometres. The next cycle the Twenty-Second would go forward again, and the Twenty-Fourth would join the attack to the south of it.
For the first few cycles the offensive went on, the Archflamen throwing in his soldiers wherever he could to hold it up, and the Fifth's armoured battle groups driving on. On the fourth cycle the resistance stiffened. The Archflamen's commanders had had time to organise a proper defence. The Unremembered had come up out of the Lethe Cut in force, with their own armour, and behind them came things the Third had not seen before on the Flats. There were Laistrygons who had been in the Grey too long, who no longer knew what they were and did not stop for anything. There were drakon-spawn out of the deep river channels, blind and pale, and other things with no name that came out of the mist and went for the tanks. All of them were thrown into the fighting.
Then the Twenty-Sixth joined the battle, and reported that it was fighting the Third's own soldiers.
They came out of the Grey in the dark watch, in the Third's grey coats, with their helmets off. They had no rifles. Some had entrenching blades and some had nothing. They walked across no-man's land in a long loose crowd toward the Twenty-Sixth's trenches and did not answer a challenge, and when the sentries fired on them they did not run or go to ground. They kept walking. They were soldiers who had been in the Grey and had not come back out of it whole. Some of them had died in it, and their uploads had gone to Hades blank, and their bodies had stood up again in the ash. Some of them had never died at all. They had been the absent of the Fifty-Fourth, the thirty-two thousand the Répertoire had not been able to account for, and now the Répertoire could account for them. They wore their regiment's flash on their shoulders. Their faces were the faces in the Fifty-Fourth's rolls. They walked for the Archflamen now.
The soldiers of the Twenty-Sixth called them the Hollowed. They killed them in hundreds. The Hollowed kept coming, careless of their losses. Some of them were cut down and lay still. Some lay still for a while and then got up again, and had to be cut down again, and again, until there was nothing of them left to get up. Others reached the parapet and climbed into the fire bays and went for the Twenty-Sixth with their hands and their teeth, and the Twenty-Sixth's soldiers found that they could not shoot a soldier with their own regiment's colours on his coat without stopping, for a moment, to look. The Hollowed did not stop.
Hunt-Elder Sarn came down to the Twenty-Sixth's front on the sixth cycle. She walked out into no-man's land on her metal legs in the dark watch with a Psiloi squad behind her and looked at the Hollowed for a long time. When she came back she went straight to the Lieutenant.
Sarn's report identified the Hollowed as Lethe-work. The Grey took the soldier out and left the body, and the Unremembered put something else in. She could not yet say what. Asked by the Lieutenant whether they could be recovered, Sarn said they could not. Asked whether the process could be stopped, she did not yet know. Grace's own note on the report set out the strategic problem. If the Third's dead fought for the Archflamen, he would have as many soldiers as the Third gave him, and no army could fight a war of attrition against an enemy that grew with every loss it inflicted. Sarn agreed.
Soon the Twenty-Fourth, and then the Twenty-Second, were reporting the Hollowed on their fronts too. The whole battlefield had come up under the Fifth's feet.
After eight cycles of fighting, the Fifth Line Phalanx's offensive had stuck in the grey. Progress had stopped. The ground taken in the first cycles was held, but the Twenty-Second's long hook had only reached halfway to its goal. The enemy in front of the Fifth was now nearly equal to it in numbers, and more Hollowed came out of the Grey every watch. The offensive was deadlocked. To break the deadlock the Hunters with the Fifth authorised the Third's own proscribed stocks. The Third had kept a reserve of Styx-salt shells since the Second Grand Offensive, sealed in a deep magazine at Kappa and listed as destroyed, and the Hunters had them brought up and fired. Styx-salt burned the Lethe out of anything it touched, and the Hollowed it touched lay down and did not get up. It also poisoned the ground, and the air above the ground, for centuries. Leaves 59-45 and 60-45 were drowned in it. The Répertoire's estimate was that they would not be fit to stand on for four hundred years.
The cohorts of the Ninth Assault Phalanx that had helped the drive could now be pulled out to refit and move. The Ninth would go to support the First Line Phalanx's offensive, and the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx's assault batteries were already in place to support the Eleventh Line Phalanx's attack on Height 202. Both attacks would begin on the two hundred and thirtieth cycle, and the Hunters would lead both.

HEIGHT 202. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 230

The Sixteenth and Twentieth Armoured needed a complete refit after the hook, so the First Line Phalanx put its offensive back. On the two hundred and thirtieth cycle the Eleventh Line Phalanx went in alone. Its three regiments attacked along a front of fifteen kilometres. The Sixty-Eighth had the hardest of it. Its sector took it up the northern slopes of a range of low vents, old cinder cones that had gone cold before the Arsenal was built. At the southern end of the range, the last and biggest of the old cones, stood the Acropolis. The Sixty-Eighth's first objective was Height 187. After that it had to take three ceiling-lance silos, all hardened, all built to take a direct hit from anything the Third could fire. Then there would be the last short climb to Height 202. From there the Sixty-Eighth would be looking down at the Cinder Wall.
The Seventieth had further to go, but over easier ground. Two lines of trenches stood across its path, and it would have to storm both before it reached the Wall. The Tenth Ordnance Phalanx had three hundred and fifty tanks ready to support the attack, with thirty Colossi. If they were needed, the Behemoths of Talos were standing by.
The sappers in every regiment's sector had been at work for a rotation under the Archflamen's works. The first cycle of the battle would open with their mines going up and their saps breaking out behind the strongpoints they had marked.
In a seep-storm, the leading cohorts of the Sixty-Eighth began to climb. After a few hours the advance had been stopped all along the line by strongpoints dug deep into the slopes, each with a clear view down the hill. Rounds and shells came down on the climbing soldiers in gusts. Many of the cohorts coming up behind were cut to pieces by guns hidden safely behind the Wall.
To get the tanks up to support the climb, the Hundred and Ninth Sapper Company had been ordered to build a road. The sappers came up behind the first waves and worked all cycle under fire, blasting the rock and clearing a track that could be surfaced for the armour waiting at the foot of the slope. The work went painfully slowly, and without the tanks to put down the gun pits, the soldiers on the slope could do little but die. The Sixty-Eighth's climb to Height 202 was going to be another long, bitter grind.
On the left of the Eleventh's attack, the Seventieth opened its advance with a charge by its outrider squadrons. The plan was to catch the Archflamen's cohorts unready with a fast attack, and the outriders gathered behind the forward trenches on their ash-bikes, four hundred and fifty of them, and went to their start line. There was no barrage to warn the defenders. The staff thought the guns would do little against the dug-in cohorts and would only tell them an attack was coming. The charge depended on surprise. The squadrons went off along the old ore road as though they were on parade, and at first it worked. The leading squadrons broke through along the road, and some got as far as Leaf 58-45, but most were shot off their bikes before they got anywhere. At the end of the cycle about sixty riders answered the roll.
The Eleventh's offensive went on slowly, and the usual bill came in for soldiers and tanks. The First Line Phalanx's delay was costing the Eleventh dearly. With nothing happening on the First's front, the Archflamen could move soldiers from there to stop the Eleventh, and the grind that had to wear down the soldiers in front of it would take far longer. The Eleventh's commanders signalled the Lieutenant again and again to get the First Line Phalanx moving and take the weight off them. Grace's answer was the same every time. The First would go when it was ready and every tank was back in place, and not before. For more than a week, the Eleventh bled away its strength alone.
After the fourth such signal, Grace sent word to the Eleventh that the First would attack in eight cycles.
On the two hundred and sixty-first cycle the First Line Phalanx attacked. Three regiments struck out south-west to cover fifteen kilometres to the Cinder Wall. Like the Eleventh, they had massed tanks and Colossi with them, and the armoured spearheads were soon tearing into the Archflamen's lines. There they met the survivors of the Archflamen's cohorts that the Fifth's hook had driven back into the pocket. The Unremembered fought fanatically and fired more of the Grey. The Wrath wings were flying every watch now, bombing and strafing. The Archflamen's swarms and drowned Lanterns were seen less and less, and it seemed the Third had at last won the air under the ceiling. The heavy Wraths dropped load after load on the Cinder Wall on Leaf 58-45 and carpeted the ground behind it. The weight of all the attacks together was grinding the defenders down.

THE KINDLING OF DRAUGOS. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLES 260 TO 320

As the fighting went on, cycle after cycle, and the Third's regiments pressed forward, even the Legates of the deep rivers could not save the Archflamen from the weight of what the Empire had set moving. For the first time the Arsenal's soldiers were short of shells and food and fuel. Some cohorts were hoarding what they had. Others watched their stocks go down every cycle.
The morale of the Arsenal's own soldiers was falling. Some still fought as hard as ever, out of madness or a faith nothing could shake, but others gave up. They threw down their rifles and went over to the Third, or ran, though the Lictors of the Flame shot anyone they caught. For the first time the Archflamen's army faced the danger that a collapse in one sector would set off the next, and the next, until the whole of it fell apart. The Archflamen's commanders could not let that happen. There were some among them, soldiers or things that had been soldiers once, who would not let the war end yet. The Legates of the rivers would never break. They had come up to the Flats to kill, and they had not come to see the war end before they had done all the killing it could give them.
One of them was Draugos the Kindled, war-chief of the Cinderborn. The reports that cohorts had run from the Third, and that the Third was almost at the Cinder Wall, brought a swift and bloody answer from him.
It was only during the Hunt's long investigation into the siege afterwards that anything of what went on at the top of the Archflamen's army came out. At the time the Third's commanders knew nothing of what was happening inside the Wall. Only the questioning of prisoners, and the work of the Hunt's sister-augurs on the minds of those prisoners, would later put the story together.
Draugos meant to stop the rot before it brought the Arsenal down, and he meant to keep the war going. He had come up the Phlegethon to burn things for the river, and he did not serve the Archflamen or recognise him. His Cinderborn, like the other bands of the deep, were loyal to nobody but their war-chiefs and their river. That made them dangerous allies. They were terrible in battle, but as the front collapsed and the stores ran short, the bands of the deep began to turn on their allies and on one another. Draugos wanted the burning to go on until there was nothing left on the Flats to burn. As far as he was concerned the war was nowhere near over. He meant to take the Arsenal for himself.
First he went after the other fire-bands, to bring them under him. That would make his the largest faction of the Phlegethon on the Flats. Then, with them behind him, he would take the whole of the Archflamen's army, and that meant getting rid of the Archflamen and his household.
Perhaps Draugos had always meant to do it, or perhaps something below him had told him to. He had waited for his time, and now he took it. The fire-bands respected nothing but strength in a fight, and few on the Flats could match his. He was enormous, and he led by example and by fear. He challenged the tribune of the Red Furnace, the most savage of the Phlegethon's bands after his own, to single combat, for the command of both. Refusing would have been a dishonour to the river, and the Red Furnace's own warriors would have pulled their tribune down for it anyway. The bands of the river liked nothing better than a fight between their champions.
So, in the light of the casting pits at the foot of the cone, with their armour off and forge-axes in their hands, the two of them fought to the death. Draugos won. He took the tribune's head and held it up to the crowd and claimed the Red Furnace by right of conquest. Anyone who did not like it could fight him now. Nobody did. The Red Furnace would fight for him.
Next he brought in the Kindlers. He made them an offer. If they swore to follow him for the rest of the war, he would give them everything they needed to open a door. The Kindlers were the priests of the deep fire. They had been working for most of the siege to open the way between the Flats and what lay under the Phlegethon. It had been the Kindlers who performed the reformation ritual on the riverbank in the fifth rotation, when the Eye of Light came up. They knew how. Draugos would give them what they did not have, the tens of thousands of lives a door that size would cost. He also gave them a promise and a name. The promise was that what came through the door would be the greatest of the old keepers of the pit. The name was Kampe.
The Kindlers could not refuse. They swore to him, to bring up the warden of Tartarus herself, the jailer of the Hundred-Handed, who had kept the pit for the Titans before the Olympians were born. The fire-bands were all Draugos's now.
With the strongest faction on the Flats behind him, Draugos turned it on the other bands. His terms were simple. Join him or burn. To show that he meant it, he fell without warning on the war-chief of the Brethren of the Black Shale, an old band out of the deep Phlegethon, and killed him and all his champions in their own camp. The rest of the Brethren gave up after a short fight. The other bands followed them, all but two. The Forsworn would not swear to Draugos, and neither would the Unremembered.
Hesper Unsworn made a deal of her own to keep the Forsworn out of Draugos's way. Of all the war-chiefs of the deep, she was the only one the Archflamen trusted. She knew he was no more than a banner now. She had been running much of his war for him for rotations. But the Arsenal's people still thought of him as the holy man who had freed them, since he had said he was. Hesper agreed to sell him. She would stand aside and let Draugos take him, and in return the Forsworn would keep their own command.
She kept her word. In secret, the Forsworn let Draugos and his champions into the Archflamen's rooms in the Acropolis. The Archflamen's household guard who had not already been quietly killed by the Forsworn were cut down by Draugos's champions, who went through the rooms leaving a trail of butchery behind them. Haruspex Ilan Dace and a few of his guards got away, and went down to the Lethe Cut and the Unremembered, with the girl Mirren carried in a blanket between two of them. Archflamen Sabinus Orrhae was taken alive. Draugos lifted him out of his chair with one hand. The Archflamen had played his part on the Flats, and the deep fire had only one use left for him. He would go into the Kindlers' door with the rest of the tens of thousands. Draugos had him and his surviving household thrown into the deepest cells under the cone to wait for it.
Draugos's coup was done. By blood and by treachery he had made himself master of the Arsenal. The war was his to fight now. Millions would march at his word, and there was a door to build.

GIBBET HILL. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 330

On Quarterdeck Nineteen, the Third knew nothing about the fall of the Archflamen or the rise of Draugos. Grace watched the First and Eleventh Line Phalanxes' offensives day by day. The push toward the Wall had gone slower than she had hoped at first, but now the Archflamen's army had cracked, and the ground taken every cycle was growing. The number of prisoners had soared. The quartermasters' reports showed great stocks of abandoned tanks and captured equipment. In some sectors it looked as though the Archflamen's soldiers had stopped wanting to fight.
The First Line Phalanx's Third Trench Regiment was within striking distance of the Cinder Wall now, and Grace ordered it to take the Ore Gate on Leaf 57-45, or to force a breach somewhere near it and hold that. Either way, the First's offensive would end only when it had a road through the Wall. The Behemoths would go in with it.
On the Eleventh's front, progress had also been slow. The Hundred and Ninth's road had held back the whole attack. Cohorts had clung to the slope waiting for tank support that could not reach them. Hundreds of sappers had died under the shelling, and the tanks had made little progress.
Even without the tanks, one by one the ceiling-lance silos fell, in hard battles at close quarters. From the last of them the soldiers of the Sixty-Eighth could see what the Archflamen's soldiers had done on top of Height 202. They had taken prisoners from the Third and built gallows on the crest, and hung the prisoners from them against the red light of the river cloud, as a warning. Before they hung them, they had cut out their implants. Every soldier on those gallows had died slowly, with nothing to carry them back to Hades, and every one had gone null. The Third's soldiers had a name for the place inside a cycle. They called it Gibbet Hill. They went on with the attack.
On the three hundred and thirtieth cycle, the Sixty-Eighth's Fifteenth Cohort was close enough to the crest to try for it. A mortar barrage came down on the top first, tumbling stones down the slope in sprays of fire and dust, and then a flare went up and the cohort went. Its centuries were down to twenty soldiers or fewer after the fighting on the slopes. They scrambled up through the loose cinder, slipping and firing as they climbed. Grenades came bouncing down among the rocks and burst in grey smoke. Lance-fire cracked past them. A marksman's round went through the cohort commander's helmet, and he lay dying on the slope for several hours, out of reach of a relay he could not get to, until the relay team crawled up to him and he uploaded at last. On the crest a brawl began with bayonets and blades. The Archflamen's soldiers threw the first sections back, but more came up behind them.
Three times the Fifteenth Cohort went up and three times it was thrown off in the fighting at the top. It went again under cover of the dark watch, and after a confused fight in the dark it took Gibbet Hill. A single optio was left to command the defence until reinforcements came up. The counter-attack everyone expected never came, another sign that the Archflamen's army was weakening. The soldiers of the Sixty-Eighth cut the bodies down from the gallows, and carried them down the hill, and brought the regiment's standard up and planted it on the crest. They had their objective. Below them was the Cinder Wall. Beyond it, close now, was the cone of the Acropolis.

THE ARCHFLAMEN'S DOOR

"Every engine has its limit. So does every soldier. Find it before the enemy does."
Cradle-Lord Doros Varne, Forge Cohort Talos

A TEST OF LOYALTY. ROTATION SIXTEEN, CYCLE 360 TO ROTATION SEVENTEEN, CYCLE 90

On the three hundred and sixtieth cycle of Rotation Sixteen, the Dionysian Logistics Directorate sent the Third's command Directive 40-PA-0907. It ordered the Third to pull six regiments out of the line and make them ready to move. The Directorate had reviewed the theatre's needs, and found that the Third's strength on the Flats could no longer be supported and was no longer needed. Its supply and transport would be reduced to match. The reduction of the Arsenal was being cut in priority. With the losses the Third had inflicted on the Archflamen, the Directorate's calculations showed that the regiments left would be enough to finish the siege, with an acceptable delay and the extra transfers that would follow. The six regiments would go to Lethe Hold, where the Unremembered had been raiding the vat-lines and where Commander Thaleia had been asking for help for two rotations.
The directive came straight down to Grace's forward bunker on the Flats. Her staff saw at once what it was. The College had been beaten in the Conclave. Now it was getting back at the Hunt. Thwarted in its attempt to take the war for itself, it had been working quietly ever since to weaken the Hunt's hand on the Flats and get a share of the Third's regiments for its own purposes. The Hunt's staff assumed the College had promised the Directorate's masters on the ceiling worlds its votes and support in other matters in return.
Grace read the directive standing up. She read it twice. Then she swore for a while, in a dialect of Old Gaian that most of the bunker did not understand.
Hunt Second Celyn objected that the Directorate could not act against the Director's seal. Grace's view, recorded in the bunker log, was that the Directorate had found a way around it. It was not taking the Third. It was taking six regiments of the Third and sending them to defend a hold against the same enemy the Third was fighting. Grace considered it a clever move.
She sent envoys of her own up the cables, two Psiloi officers and a clerk of the Hunt with the Director's seal on her bag, to go around the Directorate and put the case to the Director herself, and through her, if need be, to the Council of the Twelve. The Director sat on that Council. She could overturn the directive, or at least put the Hunt's case to the Directorate's own master, and if that failed, to the Council. The Directorate supplied the Third and moved it. But the Third was under the crescent now, and the Directorate had gone over the Hunt's head to cut it without a word to the Lieutenant.
The envoys went up to the ceiling worlds and joined the crowd of petitioners in the long galleries outside the Director's audience hall, all of them waiting to be heard. Each petitioner would be heard, in time, and the Director would give her judgement and set her own officers to put it right. It would not happen quickly. But with the Director behind it, almost anything could be done, and only the other members of the Council could stop her. In the meantime Grace could only go on fighting the war.
When the answer came, it was bad. The Director had put the case to the master of the Dionysian Logistics Directorate, and the master had asked for his own patron's support, and Dionysus had given it. In the Council, the Lord of the College had backed them both. It was a powerful alliance. Others, among them the Forge and Pyrrhus Command, had abstained. The word came down that Directive 40-PA-0907 would stand, though the cuts had been softened a little in the Hunt's favour. The Third must shrink as ordered. The Council had decided. There was nothing Grace could do about it. The Director's own seal had been overruled by the highest authority in the Empire, and its word was law.
She could refuse. She could sit on the Flats with the Third and the crescent on her pauldron and tell the Directorate to come and get its six regiments if it wanted them. That would be called a revolt at once, and the College would see her arrested and the war handed to it. With the Third behind her she could hold off any attempt to arrest her, perhaps for long enough to take the Arsenal. That road led to her name on a CODE 31 and another war, against Enforcement this time. Others had gone down it out of pride, and Grace had seen several of them go. An officer who believed she knew better than the Council of the Twelve what the Empire needed had a code number of her own.
The bunker log records that Grace held the directive for most of a watch before signing the orders for the six regiments to pull out.
On the ninetieth cycle of Rotation Seventeen, the First Line Phalanx's three remaining regiments left the Flats, and with them the three Phaethon regiments of the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx, which halved the Third's heaviest guns. The Ninth Assault Phalanx was pulled out too, but Grace's case had saved the Twentieth Armoured, which was moved into the Seventh Line Phalanx. Half the independent batteries that had been used to stiffen the guns at the point of every offensive went as well. They went up the cables to Lethe Hold, and after that they were Commander Thaleia's. Grace's job was to finish the siege with what she had left.
The news got worse. On the way back down the cables, her envoys were found dead in their compartment on the supply car, all three of them. Their heads had been taken off cleanly, at the top of the neck, below the implants, and the implants had gone with the heads. All three were null. The killers were never found or named. Nobody needed them named. It was a message. The College had been watching her, and had tested her loyalty, and she had passed.
Grace had the bodies sent up to Luna. She did not say anything to anyone about it for a cycle. Then she went down to the Seventh Line Phalanx's front and spent a watch on the parapet with the Thirty-Fifth, and when Kastellan found her there she was shooting at the Wall.

THE ORE GATE. ROTATION SEVENTEEN, CYCLE 210

The quarrels inside the Empire could not stop the siege. Grace had fewer soldiers and tanks and shells now, but the Archflamen's army was showing signs of breaking at last. The remaining phalanxes were given new places on the line. The task of taking the Ore Gate was handed to the Second Line Phalanx, which was ordered to give one regiment at full strength for it. It gave the Forty-Third Trench Regiment, which moved up and began to make ready for an assault on the Cinder Wall. It took weeks to bring the guns into place, and more weeks for the sappers to dig their saps, but on the two hundred and tenth cycle the regiment was ready.
Colonel Mara Thyssen of the Forty-Third planned the assault. Provost-General Silas Varro would lead it from the front. As always the tanks and the breachers would go in behind a heavy barrage. The Steropes-pattern Behemoths Hammer of Lemnos and Bellows of the Forge would support the regiment. Their task was to hit the Wall and the gate with their heaviest weapons and blow the breaches the breachers and tanks would need.
The barrage was going over as Varro climbed into the turret of his Thureos, the Ash Walker, from which he meant to lead the attack. He watched the Wall through his scope. It was hidden now in smoke streaked with fire as the barrage rolled over it. He gave his driver the word to advance and waved the tanks around him on, then pulled the hatch down over his head and dropped into his seat. Within seconds the first squadrons were moving, pitching and rolling over the shelled ground.
As the Forty-Third went forward, the Behemoths' missile racks fired salvo after salvo at the gate. The closer the Forty-Third got, the heavier the fire against it became. Inside his tank, Varro could hear rounds rattling off the front plate and bigger shells whining past. His gunner and loader worked without stopping, sending high explosive at the parapet to keep the gunners on it down. All along the line other tanks were doing the same. Despite all their fire, the Archflamen counter-attacked. A crowd of soldiers came out through the gate as its armoured doors rolled back, infantry and tanks and guns and the spider-legged walkers of the casting lines, all rushing out at once. The Archflamen's shells were falling around the Forty-Third now and adding to the noise. Varro felt the Ash Walker lurch forward as its gun came down onto the new targets. Then it was hit. There was a flash. A shell came through the front of the turret. It took the gunner's head off and burned the loader to nothing in an instant, and went past Varro's shoulder. He had the hatch open and was out of it before he had thought about it. As he jumped down, the turret blew up behind him. Its magazine had gone, and an orange fireball went up into the smoke. The blast threw Varro away from the tank with a smoking fragment through his left shoulder blade. He lay on the ground calling for help, and then he blacked out.
Black smoke was still pouring over him when he came round. The wreck of the Ash Walker was burning beside him. The battle was still going on all around. Soldiers were fighting, firing in every direction. Tanks loomed up out of the smoke and disappeared again. Varro had lost his signal set. He had no idea how the battle was going. He got up slowly. His uniform was scorched and soaked with blood and caked in ash. Then the enemy came out of the smoke. The counter-attack must have driven the Forty-Third back as far as him. Varro felt for his sword and found it still in its scabbard, and pulled it out. The first enemy to reach him was huge. He made Varro look like a child. His armour was the red of fresh blood and glowing at the seams, and there was fire behind his eyes. He had a forge-axe in one hand and the other was a gauntlet of iron that hissed and smoked. He saw the wounded Provost-General and came for him.
Varro raised his sword as the war-chief charged. He was weak from losing blood and he was slow. The first swing of the axe knocked his parry aside and threw him on his back. The enemy stood over him, laughing behind a mask of iron teeth. Varro tried to roll away and could not. He lay there and looked up into his enemy's face. It was old and thin, the skin burned tight over the skull, the eyes two coals. Varro's implant was in reach of the relay at the Forty-Third's bunker. He knew that. He also knew what the Cinderborn did to the soldiers they caught. Then the iron hand came down and closed on his head.
The relay logged him null.
Varro's death did not end the battle. The sudden counter-attack, and the arrival of the Red Furnace and Draugos's champions in their furnace-plate, had driven the Forty-Third back, but its numbers began to tell and more tanks drove for the gate again. Meanwhile, the Hammer of Lemnos had hit the gate with its forge-lance over and over until the gate was gone and the parapet above it had fallen in. The towers either side of it were being knocked to rubble by the guns and the tanks.
Over the smoking rubble the fight went back and forth. In the smoke and noise the Forty-Third's soldiers rushed what was left of the gate with bayonets fixed, and were thrown back by the Cinderborn. The warriors of the Red Furnace went into the fight with their forge-axes, leaving a wreck of bodies wherever the two sides met. Draugos was among them, a tower of a warrior, his axe running with blood. With him came his champions, each a veteran of a hundred fights in the deep. All cycle and through the dark watch they fought, with the shells of both sides falling among them in one long barrage.
For two more cycles the Forty-Third threw itself at the gate, and each time it was thrown back with heavy losses. Rents had started to open in the air over the gate, thin bright lines that hung there for a few seconds and closed. They were not accidents. There was a will behind them. The Hunt's augurs concluded that the Archflamen's people were already opening the way for the things under the river. The Cinderborn had that power. Grace asked Hunt-Elder Sarn to find out who, or what, was doing it.
Sarn was glad to. It was her work, later, that would bring out the story of Draugos's rise and of the Kindlers' door. Meanwhile, the Hunt answered too. The Director sent a second company of the Sixth Moonwall down to the Flats to join Shield-Captain Roe's. It arrived on the three hundredth cycle, and Flame-Captain Dione Tharn took her place on the line beside Roe.

UNDER THE CONE. ROTATION SEVENTEEN

While the war around the Wall boiled, the sappers were pushing deeper toward their target. The fighting under the ground was as bitter as above it, and as slow, but there was progress. Galleries were coming at the cone from the north and the east and the south and the west. The Frost Masons counter-mined, but they did not have what the Hephaestean sappers had, the borers and the hundreds of tonnes of explosive and what seemed like soldiers without end to dig with.
Unable to counter-mine fast enough, the Archflamen's people started letting things loose in the galleries. There were drakon-spawn out of the river channels, pale and blind and starving. There were things of the deep that had no names, that came up through cracks in the rock. There were Laistrygons that had been in the Grey until there was nothing left in them but hunger, and they were sent down the shafts and left there. The sappers found themselves fighting nightmares in the dark, half a kilometre from the nearest relay. Shotguns and charges brought the things down, but every fight held up the digging and cost more sappers, and every sapper who died down there went onto the Deep List. Sapper-Lieutenant Ansel Rook's company lost a third of its strength in Rotation Seventeen, almost all of it to things that were not soldiers.
Grace questioned the sapper commanders about their progress and pushed them on. The battle around the Wall was eating soldiers and tanks faster than anything she had ever seen. Already whole regiments had been smashed in the attacks. A way in from below might be the best way into the Acropolis. But the sapper commanders could not promise a gallery under the cone's undercroft for at least another half a rotation. The wasteful battles above ground would have to go on until then, if only to keep the Archflamen from moving his people down to dig against them.
