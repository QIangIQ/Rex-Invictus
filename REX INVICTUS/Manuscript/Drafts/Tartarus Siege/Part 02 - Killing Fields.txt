THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART TWO. THE KILLING FIELDS

"Dig, then shoot. Shoot, then dig. The ground keeps whatever you give it."
Centurion Ilsa Marrow, Sixth Cohort, Forty-Fourth Trench Regiment

THE CINDER STAIR. ROTATION ZERO, CYCLE 70 TO ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 38

The Twenty-Second Trench Regiment came down the Cinder Stair on the seventy-fourth cycle of Rotation Zero, a hundred and eighty-six thousand soldiers in column of cohorts, and the Third's war at the Arsenal began with a walk.
The Stair was a switchback road of cut basalt, nine hundred metres high, which ran from the ceiling relay at Landing Shelf Kappa down the face of the shelf wall to the Flats. It had been built by the Third in its first century and rebuilt eleven times since. It took a cohort four hours to go down it with its packs and its picks. The Twenty-Second went down it for three cycles without stopping, and by the end of the third cycle its forward cohorts had dug a perimeter of rifle pits across the foot of the Stair and its reserve was laying wire behind them.
The Archflamen's gunners did not fire on the Stair. The Twenty-Second's officers waited for it all through the descent, with the regiment strung out on a road a cohort wide and nine hundred metres up the face of a cliff, and nothing came. The Répertoire noted it and drew no conclusion. The Twenty-Second's centurions drew their own, which was that the enemy had the range of the foot of the Stair and wanted the army on the Flats before he used it.
They were right about the range. They were wrong about the reason. The Archflamen had forbidden his gunners to fire on the Third's soldiers until the Third fired first. He meant to be the one fired upon. Dace's preachers read the order aloud at every shift change in the Arsenal for six weeks, and by the end of it there was not a thrall on the spur who did not know that the Empire would strike the first blow against the Imperator's own priest.
Behind the Twenty-Second came the rest of the army.
The Third's transports came down through the ceiling in seventeen convoys, relay barges and ore-haulers and the long troop lighters of the Tartarus Fleet, escorted by a squadron under Navarch Iole Saturnin from the heavy cruiser Pallas Ascendant. They did not land. Nothing landed on the Flats while the ceiling-lances stood. The convoys held station in the low geometry eleven kilometres north of the Arsenal's lance envelope and lowered their cargo down the relay cables to the shelf at Kappa, a pallet at a time, and the Stair carried it down from there.
For five months the cables ran without a pause. They carried rations and water casks and nectar for the forward vats. They carried timber for revetments and steel for dugout roofs, and wire, and picks, and the Third's mortars broken down into their parts. Most of all they carried shells. The Third's quartermasters had been ordered to stock the Flats for a war of four rotations. They stocked it for six. Quartermasters do not trust a plan. The Répertoire allowed it, since the shells were the Arsenal's own and the Third had taken them from the Arsenal's lists before the Mast went dark.
The cables could not carry the guns. A Phaethon heavy mortar weighed eleven hundred tonnes in its cradle. The Tenth Ordnance Phalanx brought its guns down the Stair on rail, on a line the Third laid down the face of the shelf in nineteen cycles, and to lay that line and the ones that followed it, out across the Flats to the gun lines, the Command sent down four hundred and twenty thousand labourers from the debt-rolls of the Lethe Hold.
They were not soldiers. They were bondsmen working off debts to the Hold's ration board, and they had no implants. When they died on the Flats they stayed dead. The Third's soldiers called them the Quiet Ones, and gave them their rations when they could spare them, and the Quiet Ones laid four thousand kilometres of track across the Cinder Flats over the next ten rotations and buried sixty thousand of their own beside it. There is a memorial to them now on the ceiling at Landing Shelf Kappa. The Répertoire carries their work under logistics.
It took the Third eleven months of front time to put the whole army on the Flats. When it was done, the Third's forward line ran in an arc of a hundred and sixty kilometres, about forty kilometres out from the Arsenal's outer belt, from the edge of the Mora Cut in the north to the rim of the Demus Gorge in the south-west. Behind it, in ranks of emplacements eighty kilometres deep, stood the guns.

THE CAMPAIGN PLAN

The plan was the Répertoire's, and Lord-General Castorre signed it.
The Répertoire did not propose to break the Arsenal at a point. A thrust at one place on the Flats would let the Archflamen move his reserves to meet it along the inside of his own belts, which were shorter than the Third's line and better roaded. Instead the Third would press along the whole front at once, and keep pressing, and let the Archflamen's army bleed at every point of the arc until it broke somewhere, and then go through where it broke.
The chasms settled the shape of it. No army could cross the Mora Cut or the Demus Gorge, so the Third would come from the north and the west, and the Archflamen had known that, and dug his belts accordingly.
In the north, the Second and Fifth Line Phalanxes would advance on the outer belt where it ran along the edge of the Mora Cut. The Fifth would then swing east along the Cut, around the end of the outer belt, and come down on the northern spur. The northern spur was a limb of the second belt that the Arsenal had begun to dig along the Cut a century before and never finished, and if the Fifth could get around the outer belt fast enough, it might go straight into the second belt from the north without fighting for the outer belt at all.
In the south and west, the Seventh and Ninth Line Phalanxes held the line from the Lethe Cut to the rim of the Demus Gorge. Their orders were to break the outer belt on the western Flats. With the outer belt broken in the west and turned in the north, the arc would close inward. The Third's front would grow shorter as it closed, and a shorter front could be held with fewer soldiers and shelled with more guns to the kilometre.
Once the Line Phalanxes stood before the second belt, they would slow down. They would sap forward, probe, and wait for the guns. Once the second belt was broken, the ring would close on the inner belt, and the Arsenal's Acropolis would come for the first time inside the range of the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx's Phaethons. Then the Third could sit and shell. The Archflamen would be inside a ring of six hundred-year-old trenches with nowhere to go, and the Third's guns would turn the cinder cone into gravel around him.
The Répertoire estimated the cost of the outer belt at nine hundred thousand transfers. It wanted the outer belt broken fast, at whatever price, so that the Archflamen's army could not use the time to finish the second.
Castorre approved the estimate. He also approved, the same cycle, a request from the College to hold a thanksgiving liturgy at the Pallas Ascendant's chapel for the Third's victory, on a date in Rotation Four.

THE FIRST BARRAGE. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 41

The Répertoire dates the beginning of the siege to the forty-first cycle of Rotation One, at the change of the second watch, when the Third's guns opened fire on the outer belt.
There was no dawn at Depth 40. The Third used the watch bells. At the second bell, nine hundred and forty guns along the arc fired together, and then fired again, and did not stop.
The trench mortars of the forward regiments opened first, on the wire. The divine batteries came in a minute behind them. The batteries of the children of Apollo threw solar shells that burst a hundred metres up and dropped a curtain of white fire onto the Archflamen's forward trenches, and the Flats lit up gold for the first time in six hundred years. The batteries of Ares fired shells that burst in a ring of iron and did not seem to stop bursting. The aedificant batteries fired on the bunkers. Their builder-shells, keyed by the children of Athena and Hephaestus, landed without much noise, and the concrete around them folded in on itself like wet paper.
Behind all of it, from eighty kilometres back, came the Phaethons of the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx. Each shell took forty seconds to arrive, and soldiers on the forward line learned to count them in. The Phaethons fired on the resistance nests of the outer belt, the hardened points where the Archflamen's thralls had poured six metres of concrete over a gun pit. The first Phaethon shell of the war landed on a nest on the western Flats designated Gamma 14 and took eleven metres of basalt out from under it.
The barrage went on through the second watch and the third. By the fourth watch, the barrels of the forward divine batteries were glowing red, and the crews were pouring river-water from the condensers over the breeches to keep them from cooking off the keyed shells as they went in. A keyed shell held what the battery's demigod had put into it, and a keyed shell that cooked off in the breech put it into the battery.
The Arsenal answered at the start of the fifth watch.
The Archflamen's gunners had divine mortars of their own, three hundred of them in the outer and second belts, from the Arsenal's own vaults. They had been waiting nine months for the order to fire. They had their guns ranged on every marker stake from the outer belt to the foot of the Stair. They did not know where the Third's guns were, and they fired by the flash.
The first enemy shells fell short. The next fell wide. The ones after that came closer, and the crews of the forward batteries, who could not hear them coming over their own guns, did not know it.
At Battery Twenty-Six of the Third Ordnance Regiment, at the fifth bell, an enemy shell keyed by a child of Hephaestus landed in the ready pit behind Gun Four. It folded the pit in on itself and set off the forty keyed rounds that had been stacked in it. Gun Four and its crew of eight, and the battery's keyer, a seventeen-year-old daughter of Apollo named Corinne Haldt who had keyed the rounds that morning, were turned to light. The fire burned in the pit for six hours. It was solar fire and the river-water would not put it out.
Haldt and the eight went up. They woke in the vats at Watchpoint Hades eight days later, in good order, and were posted back to Battery Twenty-Six by the end of the month. The gun was not replaced until Rotation Two.
It was a lucky hit at extreme range, and the Third's counter-battery officers knew it. The Répertoire noted it differently. It noted that the Archflamen's gunners had found a battery thirty-one kilometres behind the Third's line in four hours, by flash, in their first exchange of the war, and it revised its estimate of the artillery duel from a matter of weeks to an open question.
While the guns fought, the Twenty-Ninth Trench Regiment walked forward.
It went in at the second watch with the barrage, on a front of nine kilometres in the north-west quarter of the Flats, a hundred and seventy thousand soldiers in open order with their rifles slung and their picks in their hands. They did not attack. They had been told the guns needed weeks before anyone went at the outer belt, and they had been told what they were for, which was to walk out into the ground the guns were covering until the centurions blew the halt, and then to dig.
The barrage kept the Archflamen's forward troops in their shelters and his mortars on the Third's guns. The Twenty-Ninth's cohorts walked for six hours and covered fourteen kilometres, and the centurions blew the halt with the outer belt twenty-six kilometres ahead. The soldiers knelt and dug. A soldier of the Third could dig himself into the cinder in half an hour, and by the end of the seventh watch, along nine kilometres of the Flats, the first rifle pits of the siege were dug.
The Archflamen's gunners got the range of the Twenty-Ninth before the watch was out. They killed three hundred and eighty of its soldiers in their pits that night. The Twenty-Ninth's centurions logged the transfers and kept digging.

DIGGING IN

The next cycle, each soldier of the Twenty-Ninth dug a narrow trench from his pit to the next soldier's pit. The trenches joined a section's pits into one trench, and the section trenches joined into a maniple trench, and the maniple trenches into a cohort trench, and then into the cohort trenches on either side. By the fifth cycle the Twenty-Ninth had a single line of trench nine kilometres long, a metre and a half deep, and the regiment behind it had begun on the second.
The Third had been digging on the Cinder Flats for six hundred years, and it had a manual for the work that had not changed in four of them. Fire bays every eight metres, with traverses between so that a shell in one bay did not kill the next. Firesteps of tamped cinder. Revetments of timber where there was timber and of basalt blocks where there was not, and duckboards on the floor against the seep. Dugouts were cut six metres deep, roofed in steel and basalt. Communication trenches ran back to the support line at a zigzag, and a cohort trench every hundred metres had a sump. At night the wire teams went out in front with their coils and their stakes and laid three belts of wire, and the heavy weapons teams came forward and dug in their guns where the cohort's optio told them, and by the morning the guns were part of the ground.
The Flats fought the work. The cinder ran when it was dry and slumped when it was wet. The seep-storms came down every few cycles and filled the trenches to the knee, and the soldiers baled them out with their helmets and dug the sumps deeper. Heat veins ran under the ash. A trench cut across one boiled. The Twenty-Ninth lost a maniple of its Fourth Cohort on the eleventh cycle when its sappers cut into a vein six metres down a dugout shaft and the vein came up the shaft at a thousand degrees. The Third's engineers walked the line with heat-rods after that, and the Third's trench maps from the Arsenal campaign carry the veins in red.
The Archflamen's army did not come out to stop it.
It stayed in its own trenches on the outer belt, twenty-six kilometres off, under the barrage that came down on it every watch. It fired on the Third's working parties with its mortars, and its marksmen shot the careless at four kilometres from nests in the slag heaps. That was all it did. While the Third dug its trenches, the Archflamen's thralls manned theirs, the long belts they had spent nine months digging across the Flats, and the two armies faced each other across twenty kilometres of ash that neither of them walked into.
The outer belt was a hard thing to look at through a periscope.
It was nine kilometres deep where it was finished. The Archflamen's thralls had dug it as the Arsenal's builders taught them. They had used the Arsenal's own manual, which was older than the Third's and better, and the Arsenal's stores, which were bottomless. Its front was a belt of tank traps cast from slag, a hundred metres deep. Behind the traps lay an anti-tank ditch eight metres wide and five deep, and behind the ditch, razor-wire laid in belts too thick for a shell to clear. Under all of it lay the mines. The Répertoire's estimate, from the Arsenal's own inventory, was that the thralls had laid between three and four million of them in the outer belt alone. They had laid them by hand, on a plan, and the plan had been made by the Arsenal's ordnance clerks, who had been making mines for eight thousand years and knew where a soldier put his foot.
Behind the wire were the trenches, and under the trenches the shelters, cut down into the basalt. The thralls did not stay in the trenches under fire. They went down, sixteen and twenty metres, and came up when the barrage lifted. In places the trench lines had been gathered into resistance nests, hardened points of poured concrete under six metres of fill, built so that every nest could fire into the ground in front of the next. A soldier walking across the outer belt would be in the fire of two nests at any point, and often three.
And the belts grew harder as they went in. The outer belt was the youngest and least finished. The second was older, deeper and better. The inner belt was the Third's own work, six hundred years old, and its plans were in the Third's archive, and the Third's engineers, reading them, did not like what they read.
Each belt would have to be broken. Each would cost more than the last. The Répertoire priced them, and the Third dug forward.

THE FIRST ASSAULT ON THE OUTER BELT. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 79

The Répertoire chose the place for the first assault. It chose Leaf 22-14, on the western Flats, where the outer belt crossed a low cinder rise called the Drum. The thralls had built it in a hurry and it had the fewest nests per kilometre of any leaf on the belt. The Répertoire assessed that it might be broken there.
The Thirty-Seventh Trench Regiment of the Seventh Line Phalanx would make the attack. The Twenty-Second on its left and the Thirty-Third on its right would make strong attacks of their own on either flank, to pin the Archflamen's troops in front of them and draw his reserves away from the Drum. Behind the Thirty-Seventh, the Fourth Assault Phalanx brought up its tanks and waited. If the Thirty-Seventh got a breach in the outer belt, the Fourth would drive through it with the Sixteenth Armoured Regiment and go for the second belt before the Archflamen could stop it.
The plan for the assault went in three waves over two days, with four hundred and sixty thousand soldiers across the three regiments.
The first wave was not expected to get far. It would go in as strong probes along the whole front of the three regiments, to find where the outer belt was weak. The second wave was the Thirty-Seventh's breachers, the heavy assault maniples in their plate, who would follow the first wave through the weak places and get into the outer belt and hold there until the second day. On the second day the third wave would go through the breachers and widen the breach. When the breach was wide enough, the Sixteenth Armoured would come out of reserve and go through it.
Before any of that, the guns.
The Third brought every gun it could move to the western Flats. The forward regiments' trench mortars stood in the fire bays. In the second line, behind them, stood the heavy mortars and the quad-launchers, ranged on the enemy's heavy weapons where the spotters had found them. Behind those, the Kyklops siege carriages, twelve-wheeled crawlers each carrying a single short-barrelled howitzer, would fire directly on the bunkers and the nests. Behind the Kyklops were the divine batteries, which would lay a rolling barrage ahead of the infantry to keep the thralls down in their shelters, and then lift onto the Archflamen's guns. And at the back were the Phaethons, which would add their weight to the rolling barrage, and then lift to the nests deeper in the belt.
The quartermasters had stockpiled three million, six hundred thousand shells for the attack. It was the heaviest barrage the Third had ever fired, and it would fall on three leaves of the Flats.
The Second Ordnance Phalanx opened fire on the seventy-fourth cycle to support the regiments' own guns. It fired for five cycles without stopping. The ground between the Third's forward line and the outer belt was turned over until no one on either side could have said where a road had been. The Archflamen's forward trenches on the Drum were struck so often that their outline disappeared from the spotters' periscopes. On the evening of the fourth cycle, the Thirty-Seventh's commander, Colonel Petra Anselm, reported to the Seventh Line Phalanx that she did not expect her first wave to meet much resistance.
On the seventy-ninth cycle, at the third bell, the first wave was in its trenches.
The soldiers stood packed along the fire bays, shoulder to shoulder, with the scaling ladders leaned against the parapet in front of them every two metres. Overhead the barrage rose to its peak. The divine batteries and the Phaethons fired together, and the explosions on the Drum came so close together that the soldiers stopped hearing them as explosions. They heard a single sound, like the Phlegethon in flood, and felt it in their teeth.
At the fourth bell the barrage lifted to the rear.
The silence after it was worse than the sound. In the Thirty-Seventh's trenches, soldiers who had stood through five cycles of the guns without flinching put their hands over their ears. The order came down the relay to every optio on the line. The optios blew their whistles.

LEAF 22-14, THE FORWARD TRENCH OF THE THIRTY-SEVENTH. FOURTH BELL

Private Jory Venn went up the ladder with his rifle in his right hand and his left hand on the rung, and the wind came over the parapet and hit him in the face. It smelled of hot iron.
He stood up on the parapet. Up and down the line for as far as he could see, the Fourteenth Cohort was standing up out of the ground. Then it was walking. They went through the gaps in their own wire, which the wire teams had cut in the night, and out into the craters.
Optio Desh was in front of him, a dozen metres ahead and to the left, with his whistle in his teeth and his arm up, keeping the line. The Fourteenth walked in line the way it had walked on the parade ground at Kappa. The craters made it hard. Venn went down into one and up the far side and the cinder ran under his boots, and when he came up the line was ragged and Desh was waving it straight.
The outer belt was a dark line two kilometres ahead. There was nothing moving on it.
Venn had walked about twenty metres when it opened up.
It came from everywhere at once, from the left and the right and in front, from places on the outer belt that the spotters had marked destroyed. The thralls had sat out five cycles of barrage twenty metres down in the basalt, and when the barrage lifted they had come up their shafts with their guns. Machine guns from the nests on the Drum crossed in front of the Fourteenth at waist height. Venn saw the line ahead of him stop as if it had walked into a wall. Desh went down without dropping his arm.
Then the shells came. The Archflamen's gunners had the range of every crater on Leaf 22-14. They had marked it with stakes in Rotation Zero. Their shells came down among the Fourteenth in a pattern that walked from the forward line to the wire and back again, and the cinder went up in fountains taller than a building. Venn saw a section of his own maniple lifted off the ground. He saw them come down.
He kept walking. Everybody kept walking. The Third had trained them to walk, and they walked into the fire in line, with their rifles at the port, and the fire took the line apart in front of them. As the fire got heavier, men and women started going to ground in the craters. The Archflamen's mortars dropped their shells into the craters. An optio of the Fourteenth stood up from a crater on Venn's right and shouted at his maniple to come on, and they came on, and he was cut in half. Behind them, in the forward trench, the next maniples were fixing bayonets and waiting their turn.
Venn made forty metres. Then something hit the ground beside him and he went up.

THE VATS AT WATCHPOINT HADES. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 87

He woke in a tank of nectar with a tube in his mouth and a light over him, and someone was saying his number. He was naked and he was warm. His hands were his hands, but they were new, and the calluses he had worn on them in a year of digging were gone.
The attendant who pulled him out of the vat checked his eyes and his teeth and asked him his name and his cohort, and his mother's name, and the motto of the Third, and he gave them. She wrote him up. He asked her what had happened at the Drum. She did not know. He was the six hundred and forty-first soldier of the Fourteenth Cohort through the vats that week, and in her view that was what had happened.
He was posted back down on the ninety-fifth cycle. The Fourteenth Cohort had a new optio and four maniples of replacements from the depot at Kappa, and two hundred and seventy of its own dead who had come back from the vats before him. Its veterans had a word for the walk back into the line after a transfer. They called it the second ladder. Venn had gone up the first one once, and he was afraid of the second one, and he did not tell anyone.

THE FIRST ASSAULT, CONTINUED

Six hundred and forty soldiers of the Fourteenth Cohort went up the ladders in the first wave at Leaf 22-14, in nine maniples. Five hundred and seventy-one of them were transferred or wounded by the end of the first watch. Every officer was transferred. At roll that night, the cohort's survivors in the forward trench numbered sixty-nine, which was fewer than the strength of one maniple. No soldier of the Thirty-Seventh reached the enemy wire on Leaf 22-14 that day.
Elsewhere on the Drum, a few sections got there. They went through the wire on their bellies where the guns had thinned it, with grenades in their hands, and dropped into the outer belt's forward trench and fought the thralls hand to hand in the fire bays. The thralls fought hard. They were forge-workers who had never held a rifle until the year before, and they had no implants. A thrall killed in the outer belt was gone. They fought as if they knew it, and they held their bays until the last of them were dead, and then the Archflamen's mortars fell on the bays, and the Third's soldiers who had taken them went up with the thralls.
In the support trenches and the communication trenches behind the Thirty-Seventh's forward line, the second wave waited its turn. The breachers came forward through the crush in their plate, two hundred kilograms of steel and ceramic on a soldier, moving slowly in single file. They knew what had happened to the first wave. They had watched it through the periscopes. They were ordered forward, and at the sixth bell they went over the parapet on cue and walked out across the same ground. It killed them the way it had killed the first wave. The plate stopped rifle rounds. It did not stop the shells, or the heavy guns in the nests.
The attack went on through the day. At the end of the seventh watch, it was stopped.
All that night the wounded came back. They crawled over the lip of the forward trench in ones and twos, and soldiers in the fire bays reached up and pulled them down. Every few minutes the Archflamen's gunners fired a flare over Leaf 22-14, and in the white light the ground between the lines was covered in figures, some still and some moving. Some of the Thirty-Seventh's sections were still out there, in craters a hundred metres short of the outer belt, and they stayed there through the night. In the dark the firefights broke out again where they tried to go forward.
The Thirty-Seventh had a medicus-centurion named Hanno Ricci who went out into no-man's land that night eleven times with a stretcher. On the eleventh time he did not come back. He woke at Watchpoint Hades and asked to go back down, and was refused. The vats had flagged him for a third transfer in a rotation and the Répertoire's rule was a month's rest. He went back down anyway on a supply cable with a crate of nectar, and nobody stopped him.
The cost had been terrible and the gains were nothing. At the fourth bell on the second cycle, the third wave went in. It paid the same price as the first and second and gained no ground. The breach the Répertoire had planned for did not happen. The outer belt on the Drum had been finished after all, in the eleven months while the Third was putting its army on the Flats, and the thralls had put more nests into it than were in the Arsenal's plans.
The Sixteenth Armoured Regiment never left its reserve.

THE GARRISON COUNTER-ATTACKS. ROTATION ONE, CYCLE 85

Six cycles after the first assault, the Arsenal's Garrison Cohorts came out of the outer belt on the Drum to finish the Thirty-Seventh.
Prefect Ostorian had watched the three waves die on his wire. He judged that the Thirty-Seventh had been bled white and that its forward trench was held by survivors and replacements, and he sent nine thousand of his garrison soldiers across the ash behind a barrage of his own to take it. The garrison came on in good order, behind a screen of Hoplon carriers.
He was right about the Thirty-Seventh's forward trench. He was wrong about its guns. The Thirty-Seventh's heavy weapons teams had never gone over the parapet. Their guns were still dug in where the optios had put them in the nights after the regiment came forward, with their ammunition stacked beside them, and their crews were the same crews. The garrison walked into the same kind of crossed fire that the Thirty-Seventh had walked into, and the heavy mortars and quad-launchers of the Thirty-Seventh's second line lifted onto them in under a minute. By the end of the watch the garrison's attack was over. The garrison dead lay on top of the Thirty-Seventh's dead between the lines, and the garrison's dead did not get up.
The two armies were back where they had been. The artillery duel went on. The fury of the first assault was over, and it had cost the Third one hundred and eighty-four thousand transfers in seven cycles, of which two hundred and six were null.
Two hundred and six soldiers did not reach the vats. The Répertoire could not account for them. Their implants had fired, and the uploads had gone into the noosphere, and the noosphere had not delivered them to Watchpoint Hades. The Third's technical officers put it down to the Archflamen's jammers, which had been broadcasting on the noosphere bands since the first barrage, and to the Phlegethon, which had always eaten a few uploads a rotation at Depth 40.
They were half right. Haruspex Dace had been listening on those bands since the Tabularium fell.

LIFE IN THE TRENCHES

Up and down the arc, both armies dug in deeper.
A maze of trenches spread across the Cinder Flats over the following months, in a band that grew from three kilometres to eleven. It looked as if neither side meant to move for a long time. For months at a time the line did not move. The sentries in the Third's forward bays looked across the ash at the outer belt, and the Archflamen's sentries on the outer belt looked back.
The line stopped. The war went on. Every regiment took its turn at the patrols and the raids. At night the patrols went out into the ground between the lines to lift the enemy's mines and lay their own, and to find gaps in the wire for the raiders. Raids for prisoners went out twice a week from every regiment on the line, a maniple at a time, with knives and grenades and entrenching tools. The Archflamen's raiders came the other way. They came most often from the Sworn, in black and ash, and they came for the Third's sentries and took their heads.
The Third lost about two thousand three hundred transfers a cycle along the arc, without any regiment making an attack. The Répertoire planned for it. The vats ran day and night, and the cables brought down replacements every cycle, a third of them soldiers coming back down after their own transfers, and the line stayed full.
A soldier's life on the line came down to the cycle and the watch. There were six watches to a cycle, and a soldier in a forward cohort stood two of them in the fire bay, one on fatigues, and slept in a dugout for the other three, or tried to. The fatigues were digging and carrying. Rations came up from the support line twice a cycle in hay boxes. They were hot when they left and cold when they arrived, and they were the same thing every time, a grain paste with lentils and salt meat from the ceiling worlds. The water came up in cans and tasted of the cans. The Third's soldiers drank it boiled. Water from the Flats that had not been boiled sent a soldier to the vats inside a week, and a soldier sent to the vats by the water was put on a charge when he came back.
The seep-storms came every four or five cycles and turned the trenches into streams. Between storms, the ash got into everything. It got into the rifles and the food and the eyes. The cohort's armourers went down the line every watch with oil and brushes. The rats came in the second month. They were Phlegethon rats, the size of a dog, and they had come up the river with the heat, and they ate the dead in no-man's land and the rations in the dugouts. The cohorts kept dogs against them. The dogs did not go up. The cohorts kept them anyway, and named them, and buried them in the parapet.
The Archflamen's divine mortars came in at random. They came at any watch, two or three shells at a time, at the same cohort or a different one. The soldiers of the line learned the sound of each kind of shell, the scream of a solar round and the flat cough of an aedificant, and learned which ones gave them time to get into a dugout, and which ones did not. A soldier who did not learn went up, and came back from the vats, and generally learned the second time.
The ones who came back too often were the trouble. The vats could put a soldier back in a body, but they could not take away what he remembered of the dying. Most of the Third's soldiers came back from their first transfer ready to work. By the fourth or fifth, many of them came back slow, or with a tremor in one hand, or unable to sleep without a light. The cohort's medicus logged them and the optios posted them to the support line for a month. There were soldiers in the Third who had been up forty times. The line called them Old Ladders. Their cohorts kept them out of the forward bays and put them on the wire teams, where they were steady and did not need to sleep.
The first rotation of the siege went by. The outer belt had held against the first assault. Both armies settled down to wear the other out, and the Flats between them filled up with the dead of one side.

THE FORTY-FOURTH TRENCH REGIMENT, LEAF 31-09. ROTATION TWO, CYCLE 302

Six months after the first assault, with nothing moving along the arc, the Second Line Phalanx was ordered to make a probing attack along its whole front.
The Second's commander, Phalanx-General Oren Callix, did not expect much from it. Neither did the Répertoire. The order was written to keep the Archflamen's troops in their shelters while the Third's engineers worked forward on a new sap in the north. The regiments of the Second each chose a few points on their front and sent their breachers at them under a heavy barrage, in smoke, and waited to see what came back.
The Forty-Fourth Trench Regiment chose Redoubt K-19.
K-19 was a resistance nest on the outer belt, four kilometres north of the Drum, where the belt ran down into a slag gully and back up again. It was built of the Arsenal's best concrete, and it had been struck by the Phaethons nineteen times without being cracked. It was the anchor of the outer belt across eleven kilometres of the Flats. The Forty-Fourth's officers had been looking at it through periscopes for a year.
The Forty-Fourth gave it to its Sixth Cohort, under Centurion Ilsa Marrow.
Marrow was a mortal-line soldier of the Third, twenty-nine years of age, drafted from the ceiling world Tamaris at thirteen. She had been transferred twice. The second time, she had come back with a stiff right leg that the vats said was in her head, and she walked with a stick on the support line and without one in the bays. She did not talk much. Her soldiers said that she had dug a better trench than any centurion in the Second.

THE GULLY BELOW REDOUBT K-19. SECOND BELL

The barrage came down on K-19 at the first bell, and at the second bell it lifted to the rear and smoke came down in its place.
The Third's smoke shells burst in grey wool that hung over the ash at chest height. In the smoke the gully below K-19 disappeared, and so did the redoubt. Marrow took the Sixth over the parapet into it. They went fast, at a crouching run, with the breachers in front and the maniples behind them, and nobody in the redoubt saw them until they were in the wire.
They cut the wire. The Archflamen's gunners had been firing blind into the smoke since the barrage lifted, and their shells were falling on the gully and on everything else, on the Sixth and on the redoubt's own forward trenches. Marrow lost a maniple in the wire. She went through it with the rest of the cohort and into the outer belt's forward trench under K-19, and the cohort fought along the trench to the left and the right, and at the left-hand end of it they found the entrance to a personnel shelter.
It was a shaft with a stair, three metres wide, going down. The thralls who had been on that stretch of trench had gone down it when the barrage started. They had not come up. Marrow put a section on the shaft and sent the rest of her people along the trench to find the next one.
The relay was dead. The Archflamen's jammers had been on the Second's bands since the barrage started, and in the smoke the cohort's signallers could raise no one. Marrow wrote a note on a slip from her field book and gave it to the youngest soldier she had, a sixteen-year-old private named Teodora Asch. The note said that the Sixth Cohort held the forward trench under K-19 and the head of a shelter, and needed breachers and engineers.
Asch went back over the parapet into the smoke. It took her four hours to get across eleven hundred metres of ash. She was shot twice, and she walked into her own regiment's wire, and the sentry nearly killed her. She delivered the note to the Forty-Fourth's command post at the eighth hour of the watch. The colonel of the Forty-Fourth sent it on to the Second Line Phalanx by runner, and it reached Phalanx-General Callix at his headquarters at the start of the next watch.
By then the Archflamen's gunners had found the forward trench under K-19. They knew it was taken. They had been ordered to shell any part of the outer belt that the Third took, with their own people in it if need be, and they shelled the Sixth Cohort's trench for nine hours.
Marrow's people went down into the shelter.
They went down the shaft fighting. At the bottom was a gallery, and off the gallery ran other galleries, a whole warren cut into the basalt under the redoubt, lit by heat-lamps, with bunks and stores and a chapel to the Imperator with the Archflamen's mark painted over the altar. There were thralls in the galleries. They fought for every metre. The Sixth fought them with grenades and bayonets and entrenching tools, in passages so narrow that a soldier could touch both walls, and won the first gallery, and held its end against the thralls in the second.
They held it for a cycle and a half.

REDOUBT K-19. ROTATION TWO, CYCLES 303 TO 304

Callix sent his breachers. On the second cycle of the probe, the Forty-Fourth's own heavy maniples went over the parapet with every breacher the Second could spare from its neighbouring regiments, and worked their way up the gully toward K-19, trench by trench, against the redoubt's guns. Under the ground, the Sixth fought on in the galleries.
On the third cycle, the Fourth Assault Phalanx came forward.
It sent three companies of the Sixteenth Armoured Regiment, forty-two Thureos tanks, and a single Colossus super-heavy, the Lady Hestia, which had come down the Stair on the Third's rail in Rotation Zero and had been waiting nine months in a revetment for something worth shooting at. With them came the Mattock engineering crawlers, broad-tracked machines with dozer blades the width of a road, to fill the anti-tank ditch in front of K-19 under fire so that the tanks could cross it.
The Mattocks went in first. Their crews knew what the redoubt's guns would do to them, and they went anyway. Four of the eleven were knocked out in the ditch. The other seven filled it in eight hours, pushing the cinder and the slag of the redoubt's own glacis down into it, and the tanks went across behind them. The Lady Hestia went last, firing as she came. Her main gun was the size of a Phaethon's, on a hull that weighed four thousand tonnes, and at four hundred metres she put a shell through the redoubt's forward embrasure and out through the back wall.
Down in the galleries, the thralls tried to burn the Sixth out.
They brought up flame lances from the heat-wells. They were tools, not weapons, made to clear slag from the casting lines with jets of river fire. In the galleries they turned the air to an oven. The Sixth lost forty soldiers in an hour to the flame lances, and it would have lost the rest of them if the breachers had not got into the redoubt at the end of the third cycle. They forced the main access shaft from the surface, fighting down through the redoubt's gun decks, and took the shaft. From the shaft they went down into the galleries, passage by passage, and cleared them of the thralls.
At the end, in the dark, with the heat-lamps shot out and the flame lances still burning in the lower galleries, the breachers came round a corner into the gallery that the Sixth Cohort held. The Sixth heard plate coming and took it for the garrison. Marrow's soldiers had their rifles up and their grenades in their hands when one of the breachers called out the Forty-Fourth's challenge. Marrow called back the response. There were eleven of the Sixth left standing in the gallery, and she was one of them.
She sat down on an ammunition crate. Her right leg would not straighten. When the breachers' centurion asked if she needed the medicus, she asked for water and then the map. She had found a gallery under the second gun deck that ran east and was not on the Arsenal's plans.

THE ROLLING UP OF THE OUTER BELT. ROTATION TWO, CYCLES 305 TO 380

The Second Line Phalanx put more soldiers and more tanks through the hole at K-19, and the Archflamen's army could no longer hold the outer belt.
It had built the outer belt around its nests, and K-19 was the anchor of eleven kilometres of them. With K-19 taken, the Third's breachers could go along the outer belt's trenches from the inside, nest by nest, taking them from the rear where their guns did not point. Prefect Ostorian sent his reserves north to stop them, and the reserves came out of their shelters on the Drum and in the Lethe Cut sector to do it. The Third's spotters saw them on the march. The sectors they had left were thinly held.
The Fourth Assault Phalanx went through those sectors. Over seventy-five cycles it went down the inside of the outer belt from K-19 to the Lethe Cut, with the Sixteenth Armoured in front and the breachers behind it, and rolled the belt up. The Thureos tanks drove along the trench lines and fired into them. The Hoplon carriers put their breachers onto the parapets behind their ramps. The nests that held out were left to the Kyklops carriages, which drove up to them and fired into their embrasures at fifty metres.
The Archflamen's army left the outer belt. It went back in good order, thrall cohorts covered by the garrison, to man the second belt, thirty kilometres in, which was older and stronger and still being dug. The Third's outrider squadrons went after them across the ash on their armoured hounds, and caught the slower cohorts in the open, and cut them down. Some of the Archflamen's cohorts were left behind in the nests on the Drum, cut off by the roll-up. The Seventh Line Phalanx took nine cycles to clear them out.
On the three hundred and eighty-first cycle of Rotation Two, the Répertoire logged the outer belt of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal as reduced.
It had cost the Third nine hundred and sixty thousand transfers, against the Répertoire's estimate of nine hundred thousand, and eighteen months of front time against an estimate of eight. Four thousand one hundred and forty of the transfers were null. The Répertoire's technical officers raised it with the Command. The Command noted it.
Centurion Marrow of the Forty-Fourth was given the Corona Muralis, the wall crown, for K-19. It was the first of the siege. Private Asch, who carried the note, was given a field commendation and posted to the Sixth Cohort as its signaller. She was transferred twice more on the Flats before Rotation Four, and woke both times in good order, and went back down both times.
The Archflamen held a liturgy for the dead of the outer belt in the nave of the Katabasion. He read their names. It took three cycles. At the end of it he lifted his hands over the bones of the First Descent and told the Arsenal that the Empire's dead came back and the faithful's did not, and asked them which side the Imperator was on.
Then he sent Dace to the pens under the Tabularium, to ask the strays what else they heard on the noosphere bands.

THE SECOND BELT. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 1

The first objective of the siege was won. On the first cycle of Rotation Three, the Third's regiments came up to the second belt along a front of a hundred and ten kilometres, and dug in again, and began on the next.
The Répertoire published a revised timetable that cycle. It added two rotations. Lord-General Castorre signed it on Quarterdeck Nineteen and sent it up to the Command at Watchpoint Hades with a covering note. The note said that the Archflamen's army had been driven from its first line of defence in good order, with heavy losses, and that the Third would be before the Acropolis by the end of Rotation Five.
He moved the College's thanksgiving liturgy to Rotation Six.
