THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART SIX. BREAKING THE DEADLOCK

"There is no after. There is the Flats, and then there is the next segment of the Flats."

THE GREATEST OBSTACLE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 160

When the Fifty-Second broke the second belt, the dam went. With the Third coming forward along the whole arc, the Archflamen's cohorts on the second belt had to go back or be surrounded, and they went back, leaving rearguards in the nests to slow the Third down, to the inner belt, where they could stand again.
The inner belt ran around the spur on a front of sixty-one kilometres. It was not a line. It was a zone of works eight kilometres deep, thicker than the outer and second belts together, and it was the oldest thing on the Flats after the Arsenal itself. The Third had dug it. Six hundred years before, when the Arsenal was the Third's own anchor at Depth 40, the Third had fortified it against the Phlegethon, and every Archflamen and every Steward since had added to it, with ever larger schemes, as the vaults went deeper and the Arsenal grew. The Third's engineers had its plans in their archive, and the plans were the worst thing in the archive.
It was trench lines, and bunkers, and pillboxes, and gun pits sunk into the basalt and roofed with it, and redoubts and supporting works, all sited so that every approach was a killing ground swept from three sides. Under it ran galleries and shelters so deep that the Phaethons could not reach them. In front of it lay the wire, and the tank traps, and the mines. And unlike the outer and second belts, the inner belt was covered by the ceiling-lances. Sixty of them stood in their silos across the inner belt and the spur. They had been built to keep things out of the ceiling geometry, but the Arsenal's gunners had learned in the first rotation of the siege that a lance could fire flat across the Flats as well as up, and a lance-beam fired flat could cross a regiment's front from end to end in a second. The Third's Phaethons could reach the lance silos now. They could not break them. The silos had been built to stand up to anything that came down out of the ceiling, and nothing the Third had on the ground was as heavy as that.
The Archflamen's army had had eight rotations to work on it. Every gun had been ranged. Every metre had been swept.
Before the Archflamen's revolt, the Arsenal had been thought impregnable, and it had been built to be. Only the Empire, with the vats and the endless soldiers of the ceiling worlds, could have thought of attacking it head on. Even the Third had taken eight rotations and twenty-three million transfers to come up to the inner belt. Many of the Third's officers thought they now faced the worst of it. Inside the inner belt was the Cinder Wall, and inside the Wall the cinder cone and the Acropolis. The Wall and the Acropolis were strong, but they could not move, and the Phaethons could grind them down in time. The inner belt was not one target. It was ten thousand. It would have to be taken a few hundred metres at a time, by attrition, at high cost. Once the Third was inside it, the staff believed, the Wall and the Acropolis would go quickly.
The staff on Quarterdeck Nineteen still believed it on the hundred and sixtieth cycle of Rotation Eight, before the drowned came down.

SMALL GAINS. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 160 TO 260

Lord-General Castorre had been given twelve rotations to retake the Arsenal, with the full backing of the Dionysian Logistics Directorate for shells and soldiers. His plan had been the Répertoire's plan, plain and careful, with great attention given to the supply of the army and little to the price. He had four rotations left. Nobody on his staff thought twelve could be kept to. Nobody knew what would happen when the twelve ran out. Most of the staff assumed it would mean failure. The Directorate would cut the Third's priority, regiments would be taken away for other fronts, and the Arsenal would recede, with the Third stuck in front of it in a war it could not finish.
If the Archflamen held for four more rotations, he would have won, whether he knew it yet or not. His army had suffered as much as the Third, and more, since its dead stayed dead, but it had held for eight rotations. It had fought a defensive war and bled the Third while saving its own strength where it could. It still had the stores of the vaults, and soldiers, and the will to go on. It had the river now as well.
Castorre's problem was simple. He was short of time, so he needed a new offensive at once. His commanders on the ground told him that the Big Push had worn their regiments down to nothing. The losses had been staggering, and the vats at Watchpoint Hades would be weeks clearing them. The Archflamen's cohorts were still fighting hard, some of them like zealots. To go at the inner belt now would be useless, and would only cost transfers for no gain. The Third's regiments needed time to dig in, to fill up, and to rebuild their strength. Until then they would harass the inner belt with guns and raids and small attacks to wear it down, but there could be no general offensive. Every soldier on the Flats knew that a lull gave the Archflamen the same time to rebuild his own.
So the Third went forward the slow way, digging. Its regiments came up to the inner belt and dug in facing it, and the Flats between the old second belt and the inner belt filled up with saps and dugouts and communication trenches running all the way back to the second belt. The trenches let the Third keep off the old dock roads, which the Archflamen's guns had ranged so carefully that a column on a road was dead before it heard the shells. Harassing fire came down on the working parties every watch. The work went on.

THE FIFTY-SECOND TRENCH REGIMENT, LEAF 55-47. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 270

On Leaf 55-47, the Fifty-Second Trench Regiment of the Eighth Line Phalanx held the right flank of the Eighth's line, where the line ran up against the south-western shoulder of the inner belt. It had a problem.
Its front ran along a shallow valley in the ash. Above it, two kilometres off, rose a low ridge, and the ridge was part of the inner belt, and everything on it looked down into the Fifty-Second's trenches. It was no place to dig in for long. The Fifty-Second's forward cohorts were under plunging fire every watch. Colonel Lowe could pull them back out of range, and leave a wide no-man's land that would have to be crossed again later, or she could go up the ridge and take it.
The Eighth Line Phalanx urged her to go up it and get a foothold. From Leaf 55-47, a series of low ridges ran south-east toward the spur, and each would have to be taken in turn, since each one gave the Archflamen the best observation and firing positions for kilometres. The Fifty-Second's soldiers named the first of them Gallows Rise, for the wire on its crest, which had bodies on it.
The Fifty-Second could not make a set-piece regimental attack. It was down to half strength after the Big Push, and the vats were still sending its soldiers back a few hundred at a time. The attack would have limited objectives. Lowe was not trying to drive deep into the inner belt. She meant only to throw the Archflamen's cohorts off the high ground above her. The regiments on either side would make diversionary attacks to keep the Archflamen from moving troops along the inner belt to meet her.
She would attack on a front of four kilometres, with five cohorts in the first wave and two in reserve. Two more cohorts would make a supporting attack on the left. None of them were at full strength, and some were below half. The attack was set for the two hundred and seventy-eighth cycle. The Fifty-Second's own tank company would come up just before it began. The company had four Thureos tanks fit for action. Lowe had asked for more. The key to the attack was the guns. Every gun the regiment had would fire, most of them in a box barrage around the ridge to keep reinforcements out, three hundred and sixty guns in all. Most were short of shells and in poor repair after the Big Push, with worn barrels and failing recoil pistons, and the heavier pieces were throwing their shells wide.
After the barrage, the mortars would lay smoke, and the cohorts and the tanks would go up the bare slope behind it. As they went up, the heavy guns in the forward bays would fire over their heads at a high angle, to drop rounds onto the Archflamen's heavy weapons on the back of the ridge.
The Archflamen's works on Gallows Rise were strong. There was a forward trench and, behind it, a solid line with pillboxes for heavy guns. There were dugout shelters under it and hardened bunkers in it, and wire and tank traps and mines on the slope in front. On the left of the attack, where the ridge turned, stood a knot of works and gun pits that the Third's maps called Bastion G-70. In the centre was a second strongpoint that the Fifty-Second called the Gallows, for the wire. On the far slope of the ridge were the Archflamen's guns, ready to fire a counter-barrage into no-man's land.
In the line stood the Fifty-Second's Second, Fourth, Seventh, Eighth and Eleventh Cohorts. The Second Cohort had the Gallows. The Eighth had the hardest task, Bastion G-70, and the four Thureos tanks to help with it, and two Colossus super-heavies that the Eighth Line Phalanx had released from its reserve for the strongpoint.
The Eighth Cohort's centurion was Ostap Lerche. He was forty-one. He had been on the Flats since Rotation Three with three regiments, and had been transferred eleven times, and had a reputation in the Eighth Line Phalanx for coming back from the vats angry.

LEAF 55-47. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 277 AND 278

In the hours before the attack, the forward trenches filled with the assault maniples. The quartermasters came along the fire bays with extra grenades and ammunition, and took a last roll, and fell in behind to follow the attack and bring back what they could.
The barrage opened on the two hundred and seventy-seventh cycle. Overhead the shells screamed going out, and behind them came the deep sound of the Phaethons, climbing high over the Fifty-Second's trenches and coming down on the Archflamen's bunkers. Lerche looked over the parapet through a periscope and watched Bastion G-70 disappear in flame and smoke and ash. The barrage rolled over the bastion like a storm and on over the far slope, churning the ground and throwing rock high into the air. It went on as the Fifty-Second's mortars began to fire smoke into no-man's land, shell after shell. The smoke thickened into a blanket that mixed with the ash into a grey fog. It was time.
The optios blew the whistles, and the boots went up the ladders and over the parapet into no-man's land. Hunched figures went forward at a steady walk and disappeared into the smoke. The tanks started their engines and churned out after them.
As the Fifty-Second went forward, its guns went quiet and the Archflamen's guns opened. From the rear, nobody could see what was happening in the smoke.
On the right, the Eleventh Cohort had gone furthest, past the Gallows, but it had walked into a crossfire of heavy guns from its left. Its soldiers were in the shell-holes. Its centurion was transferred in the first salvo. The Second Cohort was going for the Gallows itself under the heaviest fire on the ridge. The Archflamen's mortars began to land among the Second, among them incendiaries that burst in orange and yellow and threw a gel of river-slag that burned through armour and skin. The fire had been ranged long before. The Second's leading maniples went to ground. In the centre, the Seventh was doing no better. Its forward maniples were still trying to get on, crawling on their bellies toward the Archflamen's trenches with the rounds going over.
The Eighth Cohort was in front of Bastion G-70, where the barrage had been heaviest. The ground was nothing but craters, and one of the Thureos had already bogged down in one. It could not move, so it became a pillbox, and fired shell after shell from where it sat up the slope at the bastion. The Eighth's soldiers crowded around the tanks for cover and crept forward. When they came out of the smoke, the Archflamen's works were waiting. The pillboxes and bunkers opened up. The tanks weaved through the craters to fire back. The first Colossus was hit again and again, rounds and lance-bolts whining off its hull, as it fired back with its twin main guns and swept the trenches with high explosive. The second Colossus tried to find a way forward, and a heavy shell landed beside it and took off its right track. It sat in no-man's land, four thousand tonnes, and could not move. The ground was killing the armour. A second Thureos went over a mine and burned. Its crew got out, and a heavy gun on the bastion cut them down.
On the left, the supporting attack was climbing the steepest part of the ridge, slowly, under mortar fire. Behind the attacking cohorts, the two reserve cohorts came forward into the forward trench to wait their turn. Behind them, a squadron of outriders waited on their hounds for a breakthrough to ride into.
The battle went on all cycle. The guns of both sides fought each other overhead without a pause. In the centre, the Seventh Cohort reached the Archflamen's forward trench and cleared it with grenades and bayonets. The Archflamen's cohorts here seemed to have pulled back into their strongpoints. More maniples were sent forward to hold what the Seventh had taken against the counter-attack that would come. The Seventh needed soldiers and heavy guns if it was going to hold. A battery of quad-launchers was ordered across no-man's land to support it, and a barrage caught the battery on the way, destroyed its tractors, and left the guns scattered across the craters.
The counter-attack came out of the inner belt behind the ridge in a wave of thralls and Sworn. They came through the Seventh's fire screaming and jumped down into the trench. In the fight that followed, in the fire bays, with swords and picks and bayonets and hands, the Seventh's centurion was run through the chest with a casting-hook and went up. The Seventh's soldiers who did not get out of the trench were killed or taken, and the trench was the Archflamen's again.

BASTION G-70. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 278

Lerche was in cover behind the wreck of a burning Thureos, trying to direct his forward maniples and to find targets for his own mortars. The smoke had lifted. The ash still lay over the slope, and a seep-storm was building under the ceiling, black and heavy. A round came off the tank's hull and hit his helmet and knocked him over. He got up. He watched the remaining Colossus put a shell into one of the bastion's bunkers, and the concrete went to dust and the bunker's wall opened. The Colossus jolted forward again, its secondary guns firing, while its crew reloaded both main barrels for the next shot.
He waved his leading sections on and stood up out of cover with his command section behind him. He drew his pistol and waved the Eighth on and went first. Metre by metre, crater by crater, the Eighth closed on its objective. Soon his sections were throwing grenades into the bastion's gun pits and trenches. In the bunkers and pillboxes, the Archflamen's people held on.
Lerche went into a shallow crater where five of the Eighth were already lying dead, torn up by fragments. He was close. With a few more sections he could take the bastion. He called for his signaller and turned in time to see her stand on a mine. It took off her leg below the knee. He crawled to her where she lay in the paste and took the relay set off her back. He called the Fifty-Second's command dugout. He told them that if they sent the second wave now, Bastion G-70 would fall.
Back in the Fifty-Second's trenches, the second wave was still being sorted out and loaded with ammunition when the order came. The optios got their maniples on their feet and over the parapet, and out across ground that every gun on the ridge was sweeping.
A few of Lerche's soldiers had reached the bastion by then. They were dug into its forward trench, guarding their flanks, the furthest forward of an attack that was stalling, like so many others, in no-man's land.
The second wave came across the ash in a ragged grey line, low, through the shells. It went on past the soldiers who fell. It reached Lerche's position and went past it and up the last of the slope with bayonets fixed. Many of them died going in. More of them got into the bastion's trenches, or over its sandbags, and drove the Archflamen's people back with the bayonet. Lerche led the attack on one of the bunkers himself. It was the one the Colossus had opened, and he could see figures inside it still holding out. He got the soldiers around him together and went along an enemy trench toward it, pistol in hand. He had the soldiers behind him throw grenades around every traverse and down every dugout stair they passed. In range of the bunker, he called up a flame-section and had it pour fire into the breach. Then he ordered everybody to follow him in.
The flame went over the bunker and burned, and a second jet went in through the breach and lit everything inside. Lerche went straight into the breach through the fire with his pistol going, into the smoke inside the bunker. His soldiers came in behind him and killed the few defenders left inside, hand to hand. In the fight, a round went through his right wrist and he dropped the pistol. Bleeding, burned, black with smoke, he set up the defence of the bunker one-handed and refused to go back.
The first bunker of Bastion G-70 had fallen. The others fell after it, each after a fight as bad as the first. As reinforcements came up, the last Colossus drove into the bastion and became part of its wall. The Eighth Cohort had its objective, and for now it had the strength to hold it.
Elsewhere along the line, the attacks had mostly gone to ground in the craters. When the word came down the relay that the Eighth had the bastion, they started again. With the Eighth clearing the trenches ahead of them from the flank, the fire from the ridge had slackened. Before long the Seventh and the Second reported their objectives taken. The forward works on Gallows Rise were the Fifty-Second's.

THE DROWNED LAND. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 278. THE FIFTH WATCH

As the Fifty-Second's cohorts took their objectives, the seep-storm broke.
It came down on Gallows Rise in a torrent of hot black water. The ash on the ridge turned to grey paste in minutes. And down through the storm, out of the low geometry where the Eye of Light and the Cinder Dawn had been holding station since the battle with the Fleet, came the drowned ships' landing craft.
The drowned ships fired on the Flats first. In every trench and dugout across the southern Flats, the Third's soldiers felt the guns of a battleship. The Eye of Light's batteries were Apollo's batteries, built to fight the Princes, and they fired solar lances that turned the ash to black glass where they struck and kept burning on the glass. Her heavy shells, each one the size of a Phaethon, tore craters in the Flats the size of the Arsenal's heat-wells. The Third had nothing to answer with. No counter-battery fire could reach a ship in the low geometry. Trenches were destroyed by single hits. Phaethons were flipped in their pits like toys and smashed beyond repair. The Third's soldiers ran for the dugouts.
Centurion Lerche watched the bombardment from the half-wrecked bunker on Bastion G-70, with his wounded hand pushed inside his tunic and the blood soaking through. He saw a long yellow streak of fire come down through the storm toward the Flats, a single shell from the Eye of Light's main battery. It landed eleven kilometres behind him, and a column of fire stood up off the Flats before the sound reached him. Fire was falling everywhere. The Fifty-Second's own forward trench at the foot of Gallows Rise took a direct hit, and the shock came through the ground and shook the bunker like a quake.
Through the storm came the landing craft, burning from the river fire in their old hulls, each one a streak of flame coming down at a slant out of the black toward the Flats. The sky over Gallows Rise was full of them, coming down through the seep, riding the storm down onto the Flats. They hit the ash and burst open, and out of them came the Cinderborn.
The bombardment stopped as they landed. Most of them had been aimed at the Third's forward line in front of the inner belt. The Cinderborn of the Red Furnace went into the Third's trenches wherever they found them, and killed everyone they found there. This was not an attack across no-man's land, and the Third was not ready for it. It had almost nothing to fire at the ceiling with. Every regiment had its strength in the forward bays, facing the inner belt. All at once, the Third's gun pits were being overrun from behind. Supply trenches were cut. Command dugouts were attacked, and the Cinderborn left them full of the dead. The Third had built its whole war on trench lines and set-piece attacks, and none of it helped soldiers who were being attacked from every direction at once. Reports came in from sectors far behind the line, that nobody had thought the Archflamen could reach.

BASTION G-70. THE FIFTH WATCH

On Bastion G-70, Centurion Lerche and his soldiers were attacked by a warband of the Cinderborn.
They came out of their burning landing craft and went into the trenches around the bastion, clearing them hand to hand, piling up the dead of the Eighth Cohort as they came. They did not burn. They had been made in the river, and the river's fire ran in them. Slag-gel and solar shells broke over them and dripped off them, and they came on through it. The Eighth Cohort did not have much chance against them. Lerche tried to rally his soldiers, but many of them were already going back down the slope across no-man's land.
He saw the Colossus firing its secondary guns as the Cinderborn climbed up its sides onto the turret. They tore the hatches open and dragged out the crew. One of them, a huge champion of the Red Furnace in armour of black slag with fire coming out of the joints, lifted the Colossus's commander clear of the turret by one hand and held him up and looked at him. Then it cut his head off with a blade of molten iron. It threw the body down the side of the tank, and roared, and jumped from the turret, and went looking for the next one.
All around Lerche, the Eighth's soldiers were firing from the bunker's last loopholes. Now it was his turn to hold the bunker, as the Archflamen's people had held it. The first of the Cinderborn to come into the breach was hit by a meltagun at point-blank range and went down as a heap of smoking slag, and more came in after it. Grenades came in through the breach, one and then another and another, and filled the bunker with smoke. Out of the smoke the Cinderborn came in.
Lerche drew his sword with his good hand and lunged at the first one. It knocked his thrust aside, and its molten blade broke his sword in half, and it put its shoulder into him and threw him across the bunker floor. The rest came in behind it. In a minute the bunker was theirs. They gathered the heads of the dead into a heap in the corner. The floor was slick, and the walls ran.
Lerche crawled through it looking for a way out. A hand closed on his collar and lifted him off the floor. He hung from it with his feet clear of the ground, barely conscious, and saw the face of the Cinderborn's helmet in front of his, with a red light behind the eye-slits, steaming in the rain. The grip closed on his throat. The molten blade came up.
His implant fired. The upload went into the noosphere under his code. The Répertoire waited for it at Watchpoint Hades and logged it, at the end of the dark watch, as null. Draugos the Kindled had blessed his warbands' blades in the river at the bend before they came up, and a blade of the Phlegethon cut what the Third sent up as cleanly as Styx-iron.
The Cinderborn threw the Fifty-Second off Gallows Rise. The landing had swept the regiment aside and given the ridge back to the Archflamen.

DEATH FROM ABOVE

With the drowned on the Flats, the Third lost the war it had been fighting. For eight rotations it had chosen where the battles were, and now it could not.
The Cinderborn warbands went through the trench lines and took the Third's soldiers wherever they found them. The Forsworn from the Eye of Light went through the Third's rear in the Empire's armour and walked into the Third's command dugouts with the Empire's codes. The Third's soldiers could not tell who was who any more until the shooting started. Bastion G-70 fell to the Cinderborn. The Fifty-Second was thrown back down Gallows Rise. The drowned had put the Archflamen's line back where it had been before the Big Push, at the points where they hit hardest, and they made sure the siege would go on.

CONFUSION. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 279 TO 300

The drowned changed the shape of the war. A slow siege of attrition had become a fluid one. The Cinderborn and the Forsworn could come down on the Third's rear wherever they liked, out of the low geometry that the Fleet had lost.
The Third recovered from the first shock. The drowned had come down without heavy guns or armour, and the Third's trenches were deep, and the Cinderborn and Forsworn raids could not hold the ground they took. In some places the front had been broken, and the Archflamen had followed through and pushed it back. In others it had held. Over the next weeks, the drowned ships brought down heavier equipment out of the low geometry, and the Archflamen's cohorts came out over no-man's land to join the attacks, against the Third's weakened forward positions, to take back ground they had lost the year before. Where there had been a clear ring of trenches around the spur, there was now a ragged line that ran forward in some places and back in others.
The worst of it was the supply. The Third's supplies came down the relay cables from the ceiling at Landing Shelf Kappa, from convoys in the low geometry that the Fleet had guarded for nine rotations. The Fleet no longer held the low geometry. The convoys could not come down. The shell stocks on the Flats were still large, the Third's quartermasters having laid in a stock for six rotations in Rotation Zero, but they could not be replaced now. Neither could the soldiers. The vats at Watchpoint Hades could still write the Third's transfers into new bodies, but the new bodies could not be brought back down the cables. By the end of Rotation Eight there were three hundred and ten thousand soldiers of the Third waiting at the ceiling depots for a way down.
Castorre's staff set to work to find out how long the Third could fight with its stores running down, from every quartermaster's return they had.
While they worked, the word of what was happening on the Flats reached the Command at Watchpoint Hades. It was alarmed. After eight rotations of war, the reduction of the Arsenal was in danger of complete failure. The Command ordered an inquiry into what had gone wrong. The Dionysian Logistics Directorate led it, under a senior Logothete named Irene Vasska, and the inquiry had the power to remove the Third's command if it found that the command had failed in its duty. The word of the coming defeat had to go up to the Directorate's own masters on the ceiling worlds as well, and from them, in time, to the Pontifical College, and to Pyrrhus Command. Their anger over the waste would end a good many careers. A good many officers would find themselves posted to the Cocytus front, or to the Forlorn Cohorts. None of the politics did anything for the Third's soldiers in front of the inner belt.

THE SHALE PLATEAU. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 300

Then the worst news came.
The drowned transport that the fire barge had sent down onto the Shale Plateau in the battle of the low geometry had lain there for ninety cycles without moving, a hundred and forty kilometres behind the Third's line in the south-west, beyond the head of the Demus Gorge. The Third's outriders had looked at it from a distance. Its holds had stayed shut. The Third's staff had marked it on the maps as the Lament of Ixion, from the name on its hull in the Fleet's old script, and taken it for a wreck.
On the three hundredth cycle the holds opened.
The crash had killed much of what was inside. A great deal more had lived. It had spent ninety cycles in the dark of the holds getting ready, and now it came out onto the Shale Plateau and formed up. It was a long way behind the Third's line. And among the drowned host, out of the Lament of Ixion's lower holds, came drowned Behemoths.
The Behemoths of the Empire had all been loyal once. The Forge of Hephaestus had built them to walk through the deep holds and crush what came up out of the rivers, and for eight thousand years they had. But the rivers had taken some of them, over the millennia, in floods and peaks and battles lost. A Behemoth that went down into the Phlegethon did not die there. The river kept it, as it kept the ships, with its crew still in the cradle, and in time the crew was not the crew any longer, and the engine walked for the river. Nine of them came out of the Lament of Ixion. The tallest was sixty metres high. Its plating had been black for a thousand years, and it walked with the Phlegethon running out of its joints, and its war-horns played something that was not the Forge's hymn.
They had a hundred and sixty kilometres of the Flats to cross, and nothing in their way. The first the Third knew of where they had gone was when they came around the head of the Demus Gorge and appeared behind the Ninth Line Phalanx, on the old second belt, coming north.
With the rear of the Ninth Line Phalanx under direct threat, the Third had to move fast. The task fell to the Sixth Assault Phalanx, which was ordered to pull out of the line at once, take what extra tanks it could from the Fourth, and go and meet the drowned.

THE DEMUS RIM. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 319 TO 358

The Sixth Assault Phalanx began to move on the three hundred and nineteenth cycle. By the time it was in place to attack, the drowned host had come another fifty kilometres north and occupied the old second belt as it came. The strategic position was bad. The Archflamen's army in front of the Third was attacking, and the drowned host behind was threatening to cut the Ninth and Eighth Line Phalanxes off from the rest of the Third. All that stood in the way was the Sixth, getting ready to attack on Leaf 61-29, on the rim of the Demus Gorge. If it failed, the Third would pay heavily.
The battle began on the three hundred and fifty-eighth cycle. The Sixth's tanks went first, by companies in line abreast, each Thureos and Ajax throwing up a column of ash as it went forward at a walking pace in parade order. With them went the Sixth's few Behemoth-hunters, the long Gigantomachy lances on Colossus hulls that had been built to kill engines like the ones in front of them, and a company of ordinary Colossi. Behind the tanks came the infantry of the Sixth's Trench Regiments on foot, and behind the infantry the field guns, still on their tractors. There were few heavy guns. There had been no time to bring the Phaethons, or the shells for them. This was a war of movement, and the Third was not built for one. There had been no time for scouting either.
Private Jory Venn of the Thirty-Seventh walked behind the tanks with the Fourteenth Cohort. The Thirty-Seventh had been lent to the Sixth Assault Phalanx for its infantry. He had been transferred nine times now. He had stopped counting the second ladders after the sixth.
The drowned host was a horde. It was the river's leavings: drowned soldiers in the rotted armour of a dozen centuries, things that had been sailors, and things out of the deep that had never been anything the Third had a word for. It was led and herded by the Forsworn, who had fought a thousand battles. Among it walked the Behemoths, shaking the ash as they came, each one broadcasting from its war-horns a dirge that drove the host on in front of it.
The Sixth's commanders had gone up a low cinder rise behind the line to watch, with their provost-optios, for a clear view of the battle. They looked out through their field glasses at ground that was already broken and cratered like the face of a moon. They watched the ash columns go forward, and scanned the far side of the Flats for the first sign of the enemy, knowing that the first things they would see would be the Behemoths.
The first strike came out of the dark overhead.
Stymphalian swarms came down out of the river cloud, thousands of them, birds of the deep with feathers of bronze that they threw like darts, and something else among them, the drowned ships' old Lanterns, flown by drowned crews. They came down on the Sixth's tanks like hornets. The tanks had nothing to fire at the ceiling. As the bronze came down, Lantern guns raked the squadrons. A tank burned, and then another. Ash spouted up in fountains as the formations broke and scattered. Nothing had come at the Third from above on the Flats since the first rotation of the siege, when the ceiling-lances had taken the Stymphalians out of the dark as fast as they came. Now the Archflamen controlled the lances, and the drowned controlled the low geometry, and the Third had lost the air over the whole of the southern Flats in an hour. When the swarms had thrown all their bronze, they went back up into the cloud as fast as they had come.
The leading tank companies went on. On the far side of the Flats, the first of the enemy were in sight now. The Third's commanders saw the flashes of the Behemoths' weapons. The leading tanks reached the drowned host's first line, where the Forsworn had dug it in waiting. To the left and right, the columns of ash showed more armour coming.
The field guns had stopped. Their crews dragged them off the tractors and into action, and the ammunition carriers came forward to each gun. Soon the quad-launchers were thumping, four shells at a time, small bursts that ran together into a cloud of ash that hid the battle from the cinder rise.
Now the Sixth's commanders had only the relay. At the front, the drowned host's first positions seemed to have been overrun, and the smoke of its wrecks added to the haze. But the Behemoths had the Sixth's range by then, and they began to take it apart. The columns of ash turned to columns of black smoke as the tanks were destroyed, one after another, by the Behemoths' guns: the Phlegethon-cannon that threw the river, and the drowned versions of the Forge's own hammer-guns, which fired shells the weight of a Thureos. The Sixth's tanks fired back. Their rounds went off the Behemoths' Aegis-wards in blue flashes and did nothing.
It was an unequal fight, even with the Colossi. One of the Gigantomachy lances found a firing position on a cinder bank and put three beams into the tallest Behemoth, and all three flickered off the wards. The Behemoth's answer hit the lance's main gun and wrecked it. Seconds later a second shot went through the hull and the engine and burned the command crew where they sat. The hull crew got out and ran, with the lance's charged capacitors crackling behind them, ready to go up. The air was full of fire. Tank rounds and lance-bolts crossed the Flats in hundreds, and shells began to fall among the Sixth.
On the left, the drowned host's own armour came into the fight: drowned Thureos and Hoplons of a pattern the Forge had not built in a thousand years, led by the Forsworn in old assault carriers. They traded shots with the Sixth's tanks. The Sixth's attack had already stalled in front of the Behemoths, and the wrecks multiplied as the Forsworn closed in.
After two hours, the relay reports said that the attack had failed. The drowned host was advancing again, with the Behemoths walking through the smoke and the explosions toward the cinder rise. The Sixth's counter-attack had been broken and was in danger of being destroyed outright. The Sixth's commanders ordered the withdrawal, and sent forward a reserve company of tanks to buy time for the rest. Of the sixteen Thureos in the reserve company, none came back.
All night the Flats on the Demus Rim crawled with wounded, and with tank crews black with smoke trying to find their own lines, while the drowned host dragged off every damaged tank it could salvage. Jory Venn came in with the Fourteenth Cohort at the end of the dark watch, eleven of them out of two hundred, carrying a centurion who had been alive when they picked him up.

THE CINDER RISE. THE DARK WATCH

From the cinder rise, the Sixth's commanders listened to the noise on their relay sets and counted the smoke columns of the burning wrecks in front of them. Fifty-five pillars of black smoke stood up over the Demus Rim in the light from the river. The ash would have hidden more, and there were the wrecks that had not burned.
The Sixth Assault Phalanx had lost seventy-four tanks on the Demus Rim, twelve of them Colossi, and twenty-nine field guns, with the tractors and the infantry not yet counted. When the infantry was counted, it came to thirty-one thousand transfers, and four thousand of them were null.
None of the drowned Behemoths had fallen.

RETREATS AND WITHDRAWALS. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 360 TO 380

After the defeat on the Demus Rim, the Third could no longer hold its ring around the inner belt.
The Ninth and Eighth Line Phalanxes were ordered to give up their positions on the south of the inner belt and swing west, and even then they could hardly hope to get out of the way of what was coming up behind them. The southern half of the ring was to be abandoned.
To save the rest of the two Phalanxes, one regiment had to stay behind as their rearguard. The order would be a sentence. The rearguard would be caught by the drowned host's advance, with its back against the Lethe Cut, which no one could cross. Its orders were to fight to the end, as a pocket that would hold the drowned host for as long as it lasted while the other regiments turned to meet it. Once it was cut off, it could not be supplied, and the drowned host would overrun it before long, sooner if it brought up the Behemoths.
The Eighth Line Phalanx chose the Fifty-Fourth Trench Regiment. It was the Eighth's freshest regiment, and its soldiers were Lethe Hold drafts, raised from the Hold's ration rolls in Rotation Two when the shells stopped coming, and had been on the Flats for less than a rotation.
The Fifty-Second and the Fiftieth left their trenches on the three hundred and sixty-third cycle and went north-west into the positions held by the Seventh Line Phalanx. The Seventh pulled back too, to a new line anchored on the ruins of Ore Head, with its front along the old ore road to the south-east, and the remains of both Assault Phalanxes were put in behind it as the Third's backstop. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers and their tanks and guns were on the move across the Flats at once, digging new lines as fast as they could while the drowned host came on toward them.
The war had had a front. Now the Third's regiments faced in every direction. The southern Flats were in pieces. Any chance of taking the Arsenal inside the Répertoire's twelve rotations was gone.
It was going to take everything the Empire had just to keep the reduction of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal from ending in defeat.
On the night the Fifty-Fourth was ordered to hold, its colonel, a Lethe Hold administrator named Corin Vadas who had been given the regiment when nobody else would take it, signalled the Eighth Line Phalanx to ask how long he was to hold for. The Eighth answered that he was to hold until relieved.
He asked when relief could be expected.
The Eighth did not answer that one. In the Fifty-Fourth's trenches on the edge of the Lethe Cut, the soldiers from the Lethe Hold looked down into the Cut, into the grey mist that came up off the river at the bottom of it, and some of them had heard what was in the Arsenal's vaults, and some of them had not.
