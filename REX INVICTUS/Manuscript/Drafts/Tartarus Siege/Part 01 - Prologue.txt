THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART ONE. THE FLAMEN OF THE DEEP

RÉPERTOIRE DU SAVOIR ATHÉNIEN. CAMPAIGN RECORD 40-PA. SUMMARY LEAF.

The Siege of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal was fought at Depth 40 of Tartarus by the Third Siege Army of Tartarus, against the forces of the apostate Archflamen Sabinus Orrhae and the things that came up out of the rivers to stand beside him. The Répertoire projected four rotations of front time for the reduction of the Arsenal. It took ten rotations and five months.
Forty-one Trench Regiments were committed over the course of the siege, with nineteen ordnance regiments, eleven armoured regiments, a Forge Cohort of Behemoths and three pure-blood cohorts of the Olympian orders. The Répertoire logged eighty-eight million, six hundred thousand transfers. Of these, four million, three hundred and ten thousand arrived at the vats blank, and were entered as null.
The Arsenal's own population of six million, two hundred thousand souls was dead or taken by the end. Depth 40 was interdicted. No hold stands on the Cinder Flats today, and no Imperial vessel holds station in the ceiling above them.
Verdict: closed. Ave Imperator.

THE NAVE OF HOLD SERAPIS, DEPTH 31. ROTATION MINUS SIX

Archflamen Helvius Strabo died at six hundred and four Gaian years of age, on a couch in the Apollonian wing of Hold Serapis, with nine physicians around him and a choir singing the Hymn of the Return in the next room. The Apollonians had kept his heart going for two centuries longer than it wanted to go. On the last night it stopped, and they let it.
The Pontifical College named his successor within the cycle. Sabinus Orrhae was forty-one, a legacy of Apollo on his mother's side and of Hestia on his father's, a Flamen of the Lord Imperator since his nineteenth year. He had served on the ceiling relays and in the Lethe holds. He had a good voice and a better memory, and he had buried more soldiers than any priest of his rank.
The office he took was the Flamen of the Lord Imperator for the Tartarean theatre. It was an old office and a poor one. The holds kept their priests to say the words over the vats and to bless the shells, and the Archflamen's work was to tour them, confirm the Flamens, and send the College an account of the faith of twenty-seven million soldiers who mostly did not have time for it.
Orrhae left Hold Serapis in the second cycle of his office with a thousand people at his back. There were preachers and acolytes, a cohort of scribes, chamberlains, cooks, a field shrine on a cargo crawler, and forty Vestals of the House of the Hearth in grey and flame-orange, sent by the House's Virgo Maxima as a gift on his enthronement. The Vestals carried short spears and Hestian lamps that never went out. The roads between the holds were not safe. An Archflamen needed to be able to defend his flock with something more than prayer.
He was gone five rotations.

LETHE HOLD, SECTOR 74B. ROTATION MINUS FOUR

Commander Thaleia met him on the duckboards of the shoreline encampment and asked him to keep his visit brief, since the Lethe fog came in at the change of watch. Her account of their conversation was given to the Hunt's inquiry many rotations later. Orrhae asked how many of her soldiers had gone over that rotation. Thaleia gave the figure as nine hundred and twelve lost into the river, and about seven thousand returned from the vats and posted back down, which the Hold treated as a rota. Orrhae then asked whether the returned soldiers knew what they were when they woke. Thaleia told him they knew their rank and their rota slot, which was what the Répertoire gave back, and that anything else they brought was their own business.
Orrhae preached that night from the parapet to the half of Sector 74B that was not on the line. He did not preach the Hymn. He told them that the Lord Imperator had gone down into Tartarus before any of them were born, alone, and that he had not come back up, and that every soldier who died in the deep and woke in the gold was walking the road he had walked. He told them the Imperator knew their names. He told them the vats were the Imperator's hand.
The soldiers had heard Flamens before. They had not heard one say that the Imperator knew their names. When he came down off the parapet, a Hermes legacy with a burned jaw caught his sleeve and asked him if it was true, and Orrhae said that it was, and gave him a medallion.
Thaleia logged the visit as Code 18. False positive. No threat.
She amended it two rotations later, after the Arsenal fell. The amendment read: Archflamen preached to my line for forty minutes. Thirty-eight of mine asked for transfer to his escort the next cycle. I refused all thirty-eight. Nine deserted anyway. They were found at the Arsenal in Rotation One, on the wrong side of the wire.

THE TOUR

Wherever the Archflamen went, the holds came out to him. He went down the Acheron stairs and along the ceiling relays, to the Cocytus pumping stations and the Styx oath-wells, and to the trench segments of the Third Siege Army on the Cinder Flats. In each of them he told the soldiers the same thing. They were the Imperator's, and the Imperator knew them, and he would come back up the road they were walking, and on that day the deep would be emptied and the vats would close.
It was a dangerous thing to tell people who had been dying and waking for years. Some of them heard it as comfort. Many heard it as a promise with a date on it.
His escort grew. Soldiers asked for posting to it and were refused, and some came anyway. Thralls from the ore lifts walked after the crawlers. Pilgrims came down from the ceiling on the supply lifts with their life savings sewn into their coats. By the third rotation of the tour the thousand had become eleven thousand, and the Archflamen's camp behind the crawler shrine had its own quartermasters and its own Lictors to keep order in it.
In the holds where he had been, the Flamens reported that attendance at the vat-rites had doubled. Some of the rites had changed. The soldiers had started to say "He knows me" over the vats, which was not in any liturgy the College had approved.
The College, on the ceiling, read the reports and was pleased. Faith was down in every theatre of the Empire. Here was a man raising it in the worst one.

THE HARUSPEX

Ilan Dace joined the Archflamen's household at Hold Serapis in the second rotation of the tour, as a haruspex and an advisor on the Phlegethon holds, where he had served. He was thirty, a legacy of Hecate and Hermes, quick, practical and well liked. He read entrails and auger-feeds equally well, and he knew the deep holds and the names of their commanders. The Archflamen came to rely on him for the things a priest did not have time to learn.
He also drank from the Lethe. Nobody knew that until much later. The Hunt's inquiry into the siege would find, nine rotations after he joined the household, that he had been drinking it in measured drops since before his twentieth year, from a flask he kept in the lining of his robe, and that the drops had emptied him out one memory at a time and left room for something else. The inquiry did not name what had filled the room. It wrote, "Tartarus is patient," and left it there.
Dace did not argue with the Archflamen. He agreed with him. When Orrhae said that the Imperator knew his soldiers, Dace said that the Imperator surely knew who did not believe it. When Orrhae said that the faithful were walking the Imperator's road, Dace asked who was standing in the road. He said that the College was full of men who had never been below Depth 3. He said that the Répertoire counted the faithful as throughput and the Pyrrhus Command spent them as ammunition, and that a man who saw what Orrhae saw would have enemies in both.
By the end of the third rotation the Archflamen trusted perhaps forty people. Dace had chosen most of them. Each had sworn to Orrhae personally, on the Styx, at the oath-wells, and Dace kept a file on each of them that he did not show to anyone.
The Archflamen began to talk, in the small hours, about a war of faith. It would be a cleansing of the deep, led from below, by soldiers who knew what they were fighting for. He said that such a war needed a hold of its own, a place apart from the College and the Répertoire, where the faithful could be gathered and armed and taught. He said it needed a shrine to give it a heart.
Dace knew the place.

THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL, DEPTH 40

The Arsenal sat on a spur of black basalt over the bend of the Phlegethon, where the burning river came down out of the Fire Steps and turned east toward the confluence. It was eight thousand years old. The Hephaestean orders had built it as a forge-hold, to draw Phlegethon fire up through ceramic heat-wells and use it to cast and quench consecrated ordnance, which needed a fire that hated the things it would be used on. Nothing on the ceiling could make shells like the Arsenal made them. Every divine mortar battery in the theatre fired its lineage-keyed shells, and every aedificant battery its builder-shells, and the Arsenal's casting lines made eighty-one percent of them.
It was also a reliquary. Under the cinder cone at the heart of the Arsenal lay the Katabasion, the tomb of the First Descent, where the bones of Legio Prima Katabasis had been carried up from the deep in the first century of the war and laid in a hall of red marble. It was the first Imperial legion that went down into Tartarus, and it had not come back up alive. The Imperial cult held the Katabasion holy. Over it the Hephaestean orders had built the Acropolis of the Arsenal, a walled hold on the summit of the cone, with a palace for the Archflamen of the theatre should he ever visit, a house for the Vestals who kept the reliquary lamps, the Tabularium where the Enforcement detachment kept its records and its cells, and the Echo Mast, the noosphere relay spire that carried the Arsenal's traffic up to Watchpoint Hades.
No Archflamen had visited in three hundred years. Orrhae arrived in the fifth rotation of his tour with eleven thousand people and forty Vestals, and announced that he would stay.

THE KATABASION. ROTATION MINUS ONE

The Steward of the Arsenal was a Hephaestean named Corran Ludd who had run the casting lines for twenty-two Gaian years and did not care for priests. He met the Archflamen at the head of the Pilgrim Stair and told him that the palace was not fit to live in and that the Arsenal had no rations for eleven thousand extra mouths. Orrhae replied that he would live in the reliquary and his people would eat what the thralls ate. He did. He slept on a pallet in the red marble hall among the bones of the First Descent, and he ate thrall bread, and he preached every night to the forge-thralls coming off shift. The thralls had never had a priest. They were indentured, most of them, debt-bonded on the ceiling worlds and shipped down to work the heat-wells for terms of twenty or thirty years, and most of them would not live out their terms. They had no implants. When a thrall died at Depth 40 he stayed dead.
Orrhae told them that the Imperator knew them too.
Dace's preachers went out among the casting lines and the ore lifts after him. They preached that the Arsenal was in danger. They said that the College was jealous of the Archflamen and the Répertoire afraid of him, and that the Pyrrhus Command would come down one day and take the Katabasion and the faithful with it. They said that the deep was full of enemies and the ceiling fuller. They said that the Imperator's hold needed defending, and that the Arsenal's charter allowed it to raise a militia of its own in time of peril.
It did. The charter was eight thousand years old and nobody had read it in three thousand, but Dace had read it, and so had the Steward's deputy, and the deputy had sworn on the Styx.
Over eleven months the Arsenal changed hands without a shot. Dace and the preachers recruited the Sworn of Orrhae out of the eleven thousand and the most devoted of the thralls, and they gave them the Arsenal's oldest armour and its best rifles. The commander of the Arsenal Garrison Cohorts, a Prefect named Galba Ostorian who had been passed over for promotion three times, was given a seat at the Archflamen's table and a promise. The thrall-masters were given another. The Steward's deputy took over the Steward's work when the Steward fell down the Number Four heat-well, which the Arsenal's coroner logged as accident.
By the end of the year only the Enforcement detachment in the Tabularium answered to anyone but Orrhae. There were sixty of them, with a Lictor-Superior named Aemilia Rusk and a feed to the Répertoire that nobody in the Arsenal could read.
Rusk had been sending reports up since the Archflamen arrived.

THE TABULARIUM. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 300

The reports went up the Echo Mast to Watchpoint Hades, Depth 3, where the Répertoire du Savoir Athénien read them along with forty million other reports that cycle, and logged them, and cross-indexed them against the Flamens' attendance reports and the desertion rolls of the holds the Archflamen had toured, and the ration draw of the Arsenal, and the oath-well records at the Styx.
The Répertoire did not hurry. It did not believe in hurry. It weighed the Arsenal's output, which was still at ninety-eight percent of quota, against the risk of an apostate in control of eighty-one percent of the theatre's shells, and against the cost of an Archflamen's death to the faith of twenty-seven million soldiers.
On Cycle 300 of the last rotation before the war, it rendered judgment.
CODE 31. JUDICIUM PETO. SUBMITTED FROM: TABULARIUM DETACHMENT, PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL.
VERDICT: ERASURE. SUBJECT: ORRHAE, SABINUS, ARCHFLAMEN. METHOD: SINGLE ROUND, PUBLIC, DURING RITE. FALSE FLAG: PHLEGETHON INCURSION. AVE IMPERATOR.
Lictor-Superior Rusk read it twice. Then she sent for Marksman Tertia Vell.

THE PILGRIM STAIR. ROTATION MINUS ONE, CYCLE 312

Vell had been in the cooling flue for eleven days.
It was a disused vent off the third terrace of the Pilgrim Stair, seventy centimetres wide, lined with ceramic that still held the heat of the wells below. She had gone in with water for fourteen days, nutrient paste, a catheter, and a VK-9 long rifle in an insulated sleeve. The rifle fired a single consecrated round of Arsenal make, which seemed right to Rusk. Vell had not asked for the round. She had asked for a cooler suit.
The flue looked down across the Stair to the Altar of the Descent, a slab of red marble on the terrace under the Katabasion doors, where the Archflamen said the vat-rite every seventh cycle for the thralls. It was a shot of three hundred and forty metres through heat shimmer and ash fall. Vell had made it eleven times in her head. The heat shimmer worried her more than the range.
On Cycle 312 the thralls came up the Stair at shift change, nine or ten thousand of them, and filled the terraces. The Sworn lined the steps in their old armour. The Vestals stood around the altar with their lamps lit. The Archflamen came out of the Katabasion doors in his orange vestments with Dace at his left shoulder, and raised his hands, and began.
He opened with the refrain of his tour, "He knows you," and the thralls on the Stair said it back to him.
Vell let out half a breath and took up the slack.
She fired on the third response. The round crossed three hundred and forty metres in a little under half a second, and in the middle of the Archflamen's chest it struck the aegis pendant he wore on a chain under his vestments, which the Virgo Maxima of the House of the Hearth had given him with the forty Vestals, and which nobody in the Tabularium had known about. The pendant took the round and threw a ring of white light out across the altar that knocked the nearest Vestals off their feet. The Archflamen staggered two steps backward into Dace's arms.
He was not hurt. The pendant was gold and Hestian work, and it was meant for exactly this.
Vell chambered the second round. Rusk had told her not to fire twice. She fired twice. The second round hit the pendant again, and the pendant cracked, and the light went out of it, and the round went no further.
By then the Sworn were running up the Stair toward the flue, and Dace was pointing at it with one hand and holding the Archflamen upright with the other, and the ten thousand thralls on the terraces had begun to make a sound that Vell had never heard a crowd make before. She left the rifle and went down the back of the flue on a cable she had rigged for this, and got as far as the second terrace below before they caught her.
Dace told the Stair that the Phlegethon had sent an assassin. Then he looked at the Enforcement flash on Vell's sleeve, which she had not removed, and told the Stair that the ceiling had.

ROTATION ZERO

The Tabularium held for nine hours. Rusk's sixty barred the keep's doors and fought from the gate-towers on the narrow bridge that crossed the ravine to the inner keep, and the Sworn came at them across the bridge with Arsenal rifles and heat-well cutters and, after the fourth hour, with a Phlegethon jet from the casting lines that the deputy Steward had trucked up the Acropolis road. The sixty died at the bridge, and in the gate-towers, and in the records hall. Their implants uploaded. Fifty-eight of them woke in the vats at the ceiling depot on Cycle 313 and reported what they had seen.
Rusk did not wake. Her transfer was logged as a null. Somebody had poured something into her mouth before she died. The Répertoire did not know yet what it was.
In the Arsenal, the Archflamen stood on the Altar of the Descent with a cracked gold pendant in his fist and declared the Arsenal the Imperator's Hold, separate from the College, from the Répertoire and from the Command. He told the thralls they were free of their bonds. He told the garrison that they were the Imperator's legion now and that the ceiling would come down to take what was his. He said the deep would rise to defend him, because the deep was where the Imperator had gone.
On the ceiling, at Watchpoint Hades, the Répertoire watched the Echo Mast go silent, and ran the numbers.
Eighty-one percent of the theatre's consecrated ordnance came from the Arsenal. The holds had eleven weeks of stock at the current rate of fire. After eleven weeks, divine mortar batteries in four hundred sectors would be firing ordinary high explosive at things that ordinary high explosive did not kill. The Répertoire calculated that the Lethe holds would collapse first, then the Acheron stairs, then the Fire Steps. After that, Tartarus would push up into the ceiling cracks the Empire used for its transit, and come out the other side into realspace, into the systems those cracks served.
It issued the first ration order that cycle.
SECTOR 72-A, DEPTH 4. AMMUNITION ALLOCATION CONFLICT. VERDICT: REDIRECT ALL 7.5MM SLUGS TO DEPTHS 88B TO 99C. LETHE SECTORS TO DEFAULT TO PLASMA INCINERATION PACKAGES. PRIORITIZE DENSITY OVER KINETIC ACCURACY.
Commander Thaleia read it on her visor at Lethe Hold and swore for some time.
The second order went to the Pyrrhus Command. It assigned the reduction of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal to the Third Siege Army of Tartarus, at that time holding forty kilometres of line on the Cinder Flats north of the Phlegethon bend, and set a projected completion of four rotations. It required that the casting lines be recovered intact, if possible. It set no figure for transfers. It set one for nulls: none above three percent.
The Third Siege Army had held the Cinder Flats for six hundred years against the things that came up out of the Phlegethon. It had never fought a hold of the Empire. It had never fought people who knew its doctrine, and had its rifles and its shells, and knew where the vats were.

THE IMPERATOR'S HOLD

The Arsenal did not settle quickly after the Tabularium fell.
For four months after the revolt the six million souls on the spur fought among themselves over what they had won. The thrall gangs settled scores with the thrall-masters, and then with each other. The Garrison Cohorts split in two over whether to follow Prefect Ostorian, and the half that did not follow him were put into the Number Four heat-well in a body, which settled it. Pilgrims who had come down from the ceiling to see a holy man found themselves in a war, and some tried to go back up the ore lifts, and the Lictors of the Flame shot them on the lift platforms.
The Lictors were Dace's idea. He took them from the garrison sergeants and the worst of the thrall-masters, and gave them flame-orange sashes and a rod of office, and set them over the rest. They kept the lines in order as the Empire's centurions did. A Lictor stood behind every company and shot whoever ran, and there was no appeal from a Lictor. The best of the fighters and the most devoted of the faithful went into the Sworn, who wore the Archflamen's mark on their foreheads in ash and had first pick of the Arsenal's stores.
Out of that, by the middle of Rotation Zero, the Archflamen's commanders had an army.
Orrhae told them that the Arsenal would be a slaughterhouse. No soldier on the spur could expect mercy from the Empire, he said, because the Empire had sent a killer to his altar in front of ten thousand people, and an Empire that did that would put the faithful into the Number Four well in their thousands. They must fight or be killed. Those who fought well would be raised. Those who failed would answer to the Lictors.
Then the thralls went back to work, for their new master, at the old work. They had always dug. They dug the Arsenal's defences now, on the Cinder Flats north and west of the spur, where the outer trench belt had been started a century before against the Phlegethon and never finished. They laid mines from the Arsenal's own stores. They unrolled razor-wire from the stores by the thousand kilometres. The Arsenal's own gunners ranged their divine mortars on the Flats, firing keyed shells at marker stakes until they knew every metre of the ground. The garrison taught the thralls to fire a rifle.
They had the stores to do it. The Arsenal held eight thousand years of the theatre's ammunition, armour, vehicles and food, against the day the deep rose. Its vaults went down nine levels into the basalt. When the Third Siege Army came, the Archflamen's army would be well armed, well fed, and dug into strong positions, and it would know exactly what was coming.

THE ARCHFLAMEN'S ARMY

There were about six million, two hundred thousand souls on the spur at the start of the siege. All of them were counted as hostile, and all of them, by the Archflamen's decree, were his soldiers. The Répertoire never had an exact figure. It had the Arsenal's last census, which was eleven months out of date, and its ration draw, and the noosphere traffic before the Mast went dark.
The best of them were the Sworn of Orrhae. They had taken the Styx oath to the Archflamen personally, and many had served in the holds before they followed his crawlers. Dace's preachers had picked them for their devotion and the Lictors had picked them for their nerve. They had the pick of the Arsenal's stores. The Répertoire guessed at forty thousand. It turned out to be nearer ninety.
Below the Sworn in quality came the Arsenal Garrison Cohorts. A forge-hold that made eighty-one percent of a theatre's shells needed a garrison against the deep, and the Arsenal's was large, about the quality of a ceiling world's planetary cohorts, built around a hard core of retired line legacies and old centurions who had been posted to the Arsenal to finish their service in a quiet place. They had never had the training of a Trench Regiment. They did not need it. The vaults gave them Thureos tanks and Hoplon carriers and divine mortars by the hundred, and they would be fighting from trenches they had dug themselves. The Répertoire assessed them as able to hold a static line and not much more. It was wrong about that too.
Then there were the forge-thralls. The Arsenal had worked its heat-wells with indentured labour for eight thousand years, and in the last thousand it had used more of it every century. There were about four million thralls on the spur when the Archflamen freed them, and they had nothing to go back to and no implants to bring them back if they died. They hated the thrall-masters and the ceiling that had sold them down. Many of them loved the Archflamen. One in four of them had already drilled with the garrison's militia company, which the Arsenal's charter required the thralls to supply. Among them were some forty thousand Laistrygonian half-bloods, the giant-blooded labourers the Hephaestean orders bred on the ceiling worlds for the heavy work of the casting lines. A Laistrygon stood three metres tall and could carry a shell casing on each shoulder. The Lictors kept them on combat stimulants and chained in their barracks between fights.
Last came the pilgrims and the Arsenal's own clerks and scribes, who had never held a rifle. Many of the pilgrims had come down because the Archflamen was a holy man, and most of them had no idea who they were fighting or why. They were told that the Empire meant to defile the Katabasion and carry the bones of the First Descent up to the ceiling for the College's museum. They joined the Archflamen's militia to defend the Imperator's dead. They got the worst rifles and the last rations.
Through all of them moved Dace's preachers, the old Flamens and acolytes of the tour, preaching the Archflamen's gospel at every shift change. Behind every company stood a Lictor of the Flame with his rod of office and a pistol.
And in the pens under the Tabularium were three hundred and eleven unregistered Hecatean strays, children and young people with the witch-blood showing who had been gathered from the deep holds over two rotations for the next Tithe Day, to be shipped up and registered. Strays without a registry implant were open doors. Things in the deep found them. The Archflamen had them brought up out of the pens and fed at his own table, and he called them his children, and said they heard the Imperator in their sleep. Some of them said that they did. Dace sat with them every night and asked them what else they heard.

THE THIRD SIEGE ARMY OF TARTARUS

The force the Empire sent against the Arsenal was the Third Siege Army of Tartarus, which had been holding the Cinder Flats north of the Phlegethon bend for six hundred years and knew them better than any army in the theatre.
The Third was a trench army. It had been built to hold a line against the deep for as long as it took, which was forever, and to break the reformation peaks that came up out of the Phlegethon every few rotations by digging up to them and shelling them flat. Its soldiers were legacies and mortal-line troops drafted from the ceiling worlds at twelve and thirteen, and a scattering of demigods by bloodline index who had been posted down for their powers. They were trained to dig before they were trained to shoot. The Third's motto was cut over the door of every one of its depots. Quod cadit, surgit. What falls, rises.
Every soldier in the Third carried an implant at the base of the skull. When a soldier died at Depth 40, the implant uploaded what he was to the noosphere through the Third's relays, up through the ceiling to Watchpoint Hades, and the Répertoire wrote him into a body grown in a vat of nectar at one of the ceiling depots. He woke three to nine days later with his rank and his rota slot, and whatever else he brought with him, and was posted back down. The Third's soldiers called it going up. The Répertoire called it a transfer. It cost the Empire four hundred litres of nectar, eleven seconds of the Watchpoint's bandwidth and nine days of vat time, and the Répertoire priced every metre of ground in transfers before it ordered an attack.
It was not free, and it was not always clean. Soldiers who had gone up too many times woke slower, and some woke strange. A soldier taken by the rivers did not upload at all, and the Third had lost more of those over six centuries than it put on its memorial walls. But a Trench Regiment of the Third could be ordered to take a position knowing that eight hundred of its people would go up taking it, and it would take it, and most of the eight hundred would be back on the line in a fortnight. The Third's officers learned to think of a regiment's dead as a kind of supply. Its soldiers learned it too.
The Third was organised in Phalanxes. Four Line Phalanxes of Trench Regiments held the line, the Second and the Fifth in the north, the Seventh and the Ninth in the south. Two Assault Phalanxes held the armour: Thureos main battle tanks, the old Colossus super-heavies, and the Hoplon assault carriers that could put fifty soldiers onto an enemy parapet behind a ramp the thickness of a bunker door. The armour was not for holding the line. It was for the breach, and for exploitation, and for nothing else. The Third did not mean to give the Archflamen the chance to fight a war of manoeuvre, and it kept its tanks back behind its trenches until there was a hole to drive them through.
The heart of the Third was its guns. The Arsenal would be a gunner's war. Each Trench Regiment had its own mortar companies, with light trench mortars in the firing bays and heavy mortars and quad-launchers in the second line. Behind them stood the divine mortar batteries, whose shells were keyed at the breech by a demigod of the battery, so that a shell keyed by a child of Apollo burst in solar fire and one keyed by a child of Artemis in lunar shards, and one keyed by a child of Demeter in thorns. The aedificant batteries fired builder-shells keyed by children of Athena and Hephaestus, which did not burst so much as rearrange what they hit, and could fold a bunker's roof down into its floor. At the back of everything stood the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx, with its siege guns and its Phaethon-pattern heavy mortars, the biggest guns in the theatre, which fired shells the size of a Hoplon carrier and could be heard landing forty kilometres away.
The Third would fight the Arsenal with the Arsenal's own shells, from its own stocks, until those ran out. Then it would fight with whatever the ceiling could make. Every soldier in it knew that.

THE CINDER FLATS

The Cinder Flats were six thousand square kilometres of black ash and cinder over basalt, on a shelf above the bend of the Phlegethon. There was no sky. Ninety kilometres up, in the dark, hung the ceiling, where the Empire's ships held station in what the navigators called low geometry. Down on the Flats the only light came from the river, which burned red at the edge of the shelf and threw the shadows of everything upward.
There was no rain at Depth 40. There was seep. Hostile vapour came up off the Phlegethon and condensed on the cold ash, and every few cycles it came down in a storm of hot black water that turned the Flats into a grey paste a metre deep. Heat veins ran under the ash where the river's fire had got into the basalt, and a trench dug across one boiled. There was no plant life. The Flats had a smell that the Third's soldiers stopped noticing after a month, and that ceiling visitors did not stop noticing at all.
To the south and east of the Arsenal the shelf broke into chasms. The Mora Cut, the Lethe Cut and the Demus Gorge ran down from the Flats into the dark, kilometres deep, and no army on foot could cross them. Any attack on the Arsenal would have to come across the Flats from the north and the west.
That was where the Archflamen's commanders had put their trenches.
There were three belts. The outer belt ran in an arc of a hundred and ninety kilometres from the Mora Cut in the north to the edge of the Demus Gorge in the south-west, and it was the youngest and weakest of them, half-built when the revolt began. The second belt ran inside it at about thirty kilometres from the spur. The inner belt had been there for six hundred years. It had been dug by the Third itself, in the days when the Arsenal was the Third's own anchor and the Third had fortified it against the Phlegethon. It was eight kilometres deep in places, and its plans were in the Third's own archive.
Inside the inner belt rose the Cinder Wall, thirty metres high, which ringed the base of the spur. Above the Wall stood the cinder cone, and on the cone, the Acropolis of the Arsenal. Under the Acropolis lay the Katabasion and the vaults, and the heat-wells, and the ceiling-lances.
The ceiling-lances were the reason the Empire could not simply drop a fleet on the Arsenal. There were sixty of them, in hardened silos across the spur and the inner belt, Phlegethon-fed beams built eight thousand years ago to keep monsters out of the ceiling geometry above Depth 40. They could reach anything holding station over the Flats. Any ship that came low enough to fire on the Arsenal would be within their reach, and the Arsenal's lances had been built to kill things much larger than a ship.
The outer belt was the place to start. It was weakest. It was not finished. The Third had eleven weeks before the shells ran out in the Lethe holds.

QUARTERDECK NINETEEN, THE CEILING. ROTATION ZERO, CYCLE 70

Lord-General Aurelian Castorre took command of the reduction of the Arsenal on the seventieth cycle of Rotation Zero, at a terminal in the command ring of Quarterdeck Nineteen, two hundred kilometres up and eleven kilometres across the ceiling from the Flats. He did not go down. He did not need to. The Répertoire had the plan.
He was a patrician of the House of Zeus, sixty-one Gaian years of age, the grandson of a Lord Commander of the Olympian Navy and a great-nephew of a member of the Pontifical College. He had studied the Tactica Olympica at the Academy on Callistus and commanded a ceiling garrison and a relay fleet without incident. His qualifications for the reduction of the Arsenal were his name, his connections in the College, which the Command needed with an Archflamen in revolt, and the Répertoire's plan, which he had read.
He had been given four rotations. The plan said four. He saw no reason to doubt it.
His first order went down to the Twenty-Second Trench Regiment of the Fifth Line Phalanx, which was to lift from its line north of the Mora Cut, march west along the ceiling relays to the landing shelf above the Cinder Stair, and hold it for the army.
Castorre signed it, Ave Imperator, and went to dinner.
