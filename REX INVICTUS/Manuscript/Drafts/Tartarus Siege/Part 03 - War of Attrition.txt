THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART THREE. THE WAR OF ATTRITION

"The Third does not count its dead. It counts the ones who do not come back. Learn the difference, and you will understand the Third."
Phalanx-General Oren Callix, Second Line Phalanx, to the Command at Watchpoint Hades

THE NORTHERN SPUR. ROTATION THREE, CYCLES 1 TO 210

When the outer belt went, the Archflamen gave up the northern spur as well.
The northern spur was the limb of the second belt that ran along the lip of the Mora Cut, and it had never been finished. With the outer belt rolled up from the south, the spur was flanked. The Second and Fifth Line Phalanxes were coming at it from the front, rested and filled up from the vats, and the Seventh Line Phalanx could cut behind it from the west if it held. Prefect Ostorian did not hold it. He pulled his cohorts out of the spur in the first week of Rotation Three, back to the main line of the second belt, and left it to rearguards and marksmen and the harassing fire of his divine mortars.
The Fifth Line Phalanx found the spur's trenches empty. It went forward along the Mora Cut against light resistance, a rearguard nest here and a marksman there, and by the ninetieth cycle its forward regiments had come around the end of the second belt and were digging in on its northern face. On the Répertoire's maps, the Fifth was closing the ring from the north, and the Archflamen's army was being folded in on its own second line.
In more than two rotations of war, the Third had been on the attack at every point of the arc. On the two hundred and eleventh cycle of Rotation Three, as the Fifth closed on the second belt from the north, the Archflamen's army came out of its trenches and attacked it, in the first planned offensive of the war.

THE FORTY-FIRST TRENCH REGIMENT, LEAF 58-31. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 210

The ground between the northern spur and the second belt had been chosen. Ostorian had given up the spur, but he had not given up the ground behind it, and his staff had picked out the stretch of the Flats south of the spur where a regiment of the Fifth would have to stop and dig on its way in. They had ranged it. They had stocked the second belt behind it with tanks from the Arsenal's vaults, and with Laistrygons.
The Forty-First Trench Regiment was holding overnight positions on Leaf 58-31, eleven kilometres short of the second belt, in trenches it had dug that cycle. Its sentries heard engines through the third and fourth watches. They reported it, and the Forty-First's colonel, Brannoc Halde, asked the Fifth's guns to put harassing fire onto the second belt in front of him to break up whatever was forming there. The Fifth's guns fired. Halde expected a local counter-attack at the change of the first watch, and he put his reserve maniples into the forward trench to meet it and ordered a stand-to along the line.
At the first bell, the forward trenches of the Forty-First were full. The soldiers stood on the firesteps with their rifles on the parapet.
The Archflamen's gunners opened with a few rounds, here and there along the Forty-First's front, the way they did every morning. Then every gun in the northern half of the second belt fired at once.
It came down on the Forty-First like the Phlegethon in flood. The Archflamen's divine mortars had the range of every trench on Leaf 58-31, and his heavy guns had the range of the support line, and the barrage walked between them for an hour and did not miss. Among the high explosive came smoke, thick white smoke that came out of the shells in a rush and lay over the Flats at head height. The Forty-First's sentries could not see their own wire. Out of the smoke, before the barrage had lifted, came the first wave.

LEAF 58-31, THE FORWARD TRENCH. FIRST WATCH

Engines first. Through the smoke and the shellfire the Forty-First heard them coming, a rising noise like a heat-well opening, and then tank shells began to arrive in the forward trench from close range, flat and fast. The Thureos tanks of the Arsenal's vaults had come out of the second belt behind the barrage and were firing into the Forty-First's trenches from four hundred metres, into fire bays full of soldiers standing shoulder to shoulder. Each shell that landed in a bay killed everyone in it.
The relay filled up. Tanks on the front of the Third Cohort. Tanks and Hoplon carriers on the Sixth. Tanks on every cohort front of the regiment at once, all the way along nine kilometres of trench. The Arsenal's thralls came out of the smoke behind them with bayonets and picks and casting-hooks, running, and in front of the thralls came the Laistrygons.
The Lictors had them on stimulants. They came across the ash at the speed of a cantering horse, three metres tall and grey with cinder, with forge-hammers and slag-rakes and, some of them, shell casings carried as clubs. The Forty-First fired into them with rifles and with the heavy guns of the forward bays. Rifle rounds did not stop a Laistrygon. A heavy round did, if it hit a leg. Most of them reached the trench.
A Laistrygon in a fire bay was the end of the bay. They went along the forward trench in packs, killing everything, and the thralls came in behind them and finished what they missed. The tanks drove up to the parapet and fired along the trench.
One after another the Forty-First's forward positions were overrun, and the tanks drove on.

THE FORTY-FIRST'S COMMAND DUGOUT. FIRST TO THIRD WATCH

Colonel Halde watched it through a periscope from his command dugout in the support line, six hundred metres back. Through the drift of smoke and dust he could see shapes moving along his forward trench, and tank hulls on his parapet, and the muzzle flashes of the tanks firing into his bays from close range. He could hear his cohorts on the relay. One by one they stopped answering.
He ordered his reserve cohorts into the forward line to stop the tanks. The reserve cohorts went forward up the communication trenches into the barrage, and the barrage killed half of them on the way, and the half that got there were fed into the fight a maniple at a time.
He asked the Fifth Line Phalanx for reinforcements. The Fifth told him that it had none to send. Its reserve regiments were committed on Leaves 61 and 62 against attacks of their own, which had come out of the second belt at the same bell. He was to hold where he stood with what he had.
The fighting went on through the first watch and the second. By the third, the Archflamen's guns had started again on the support line, and the Fifth's guns had started again on the second belt, and the Forty-First's support line and the Arsenal's forward cohorts were both under fire from both sides at once.
By the end of the cycle the Arsenal had overrun nineteen of the Forty-First's heavy mortar and quad-launcher pits in the second line. In most of them it turned the guns around and used them on the Forty-First until the ready ammunition was gone. When the dark watch came there was no front line on Leaf 58-31. There were pockets of the Forty-First in pieces of trench, and Arsenal cohorts in other pieces, and tanks moving between them, and both sides probing each other in the dark.
At the first bell of the second cycle, the Arsenal attacked again. The Forty-First's own guns answered. The thralls came forward from trench to trench, covered in ash and blood, and in the second hour they reached the support line.
Halde fought them off from his own dugout. He stood behind the sandbags at its entrance with a rifle from the rack and fired with his staff until the first attack went back. The second attack was held. The trenches to the left and right of the command dugout were in the Arsenal's hands by then, and Halde and his staff were cut off. His second, Tribune Senna Oake, was killed when a Thureos put a shell through the dugout's observation slit. He woke at Watchpoint Hades seven days later.
By mid-cycle there were eighty soldiers left around the command dugout, and the relay to the Fifth was cut. Halde had the wounded carried out as best his people could, down a collapsed communication trench toward the rear. He split the rest into two groups. The first was to break out, and take word of the breakthrough back to the Fifth. Halde would hold the dugout with the second, for as long as he could, until either the Fifth came up or the Arsenal took it.
He did not expect the Fifth to come. He told the breakout group's optio that the colonel's compliments should go to the Phalanx-General with the regiment's position, and that he was sorry about the guns.
The breakout group got out. Halde held the dugout through the rest of the cycle and into the dark watch. The Arsenal came at it four times. On the fifth time it brought up a crawler.
The Arsenal's armourers had built it in the first rotation of the siege, out of a Hoplon chassis and a heat-well slag projector, a pump that took molten slag from a reservoir in the hull and threw it at two hundred metres in a jet a thousand degrees hot. The thralls called them pourers. The Third's soldiers called them other things. The pourer drove up the communication trench to within forty metres of the command dugout and filled it.
Nothing was found of Colonel Halde. The slag sealed the dugout and set in it like stone.
His implant must have fired. Implants fire at the moment of death, and an implant does not need to survive the death to finish its upload. The relay logs on the Fifth's section showed an upload from the Forty-First's command dugout at the seventy-first minute of the dark watch, under Halde's code, and showed it handed up to the noosphere. It never reached Watchpoint Hades. The Répertoire entered Colonel Brannoc Halde as null on the two hundred and twelfth cycle of Rotation Three. He was the first officer of the Third above the rank of centurion to be lost that way in the siege.

THE CRISIS OF THE FIFTH. ROTATION THREE, CYCLES 212 TO 260

With the command dugout gone, the Forty-First's support line was broken too, and there was nothing left of the regiment to hold the line. Pieces of it fought on. A maniple held a sump at the end of the Third Cohort's trench for two cycles. A section of breachers held a mortar pit on the left until the pourers came. But the Forty-First as a regiment was spent. In the retreat, most of its stores and its heavy equipment were left where they stood.
After two days the Arsenal had its breakthrough, and the Fifth Line Phalanx had to give ground. The Forty-First had been the Fifth's left flank, and with the Forty-First gone the whole of the Fifth's line along the second belt could be rolled up from the left.
The attack had to be stopped first, or slowed, to give the Fifth time to swing its regiments back and face the new threat. The Fifth's Phalanx-General gathered what he had. He had the Twentieth Armoured Regiment, which the Fourth Assault Phalanx had lent him for the northern advance, and the Forty-First's own outrider squadrons, which had been in the rear on their armoured hounds when the attack came. He threw both into the Arsenal's path. He knew it would not stop the attack. He wanted time, and every hour the tanks and the outriders bought was an hour for his other regiments to turn.
He ordered the Fifth's regiments to fall back. The Forty-Fourth Trench Regiment of the Second Line Phalanx, which held the line on the Fifth's right, was ordered to swing its left back across the ash and dig in facing north, to hold the hinge.
Centurion Ilsa Marrow's Sixth Cohort dug the hinge. It dug a kilometre of trench in a cycle and a half, under fire, facing the wrong way, and held it.
On the third day, the Arsenal's tanks kept coming, and the Fifth had at last turned enough of itself to meet them. The Twentieth Armoured had forty-eight tanks on the line by then, Thureos tanks and a squadron of the old Ajax heavies, against what the outriders reported as more than a hundred and twenty Arsenal tanks and growing. Over the next three days the Twentieth's tank commanders fought a series of delaying actions back across the Flats, a few hundred metres at a time, from one cinder rise to the next. Each cycle the Arsenal took a little less ground. On the sixth day of the counter-attack its armour was spent, and the Laistrygons had run through their stimulants, and the attack stopped.
It had stopped short of the Fifth's rear. It had not stopped short of much else. It had driven a wedge eleven kilometres deep through the Fifth's line, and the Fifth had had to give up most of the ground it had taken since the outer belt fell. The Forty-First Trench Regiment had been destroyed in two days. Seventy-six thousand of its soldiers had been transferred or captured, including its colonel. Four thousand two hundred of the transfers were null.
The Arsenal's losses had been heavy too, worst of all in tanks. The Twentieth Armoured counted ninety-one Arsenal hulls on the Flats when the attack was over. The Arsenal's dead lay on the ash with the Forty-First's dead, and they would stay there. The Répertoire put the Archflamen's losses in the counter-attack at forty-one thousand and called it a favourable exchange.
Along the arc, the Third's centurions did not call it anything. The attacks that should have worn the Archflamen down seemed only to have made him bolder. He had organised his defence. He had shown that he could come out of his trenches and hurt the Third, and that his gunners could read the Third's movements and be waiting. The second belt would be harder to break than anyone had planned for.

THE PENS UNDER THE TABULARIUM. ROTATION THREE, CYCLE 214

The strays had been moved out of the pens a year before. The Archflamen fed them at his own table and they slept in the Tabularium's old guest rooms, under the Arsenal's best blankets, and some of them had grown fat. Dace still called the rooms the pens. He did not explain why.
He sat with them most nights. He brought them sweets from the Archflamen's kitchens and sat on the floor among them with his back against a wall and asked them what they had heard that day. They were Hecatean children and young people without registry implants, and they heard things. Most of them heard the river. Some of them heard the Imperator, or said so. A few heard the noosphere.
There was a girl of eleven named Mirren who heard the noosphere more clearly than the rest. She said it sounded like a flock of birds going over, all the time, high up. On the night after the Forty-First died, she told Dace that many birds had gone over in the morning, many more than usual, and that one of them had been loud. It had been shouting a number. She told him the number.
Dace wrote it down. He checked it against a list he kept, of the codes of the Third's officers, taken from the Tabularium's files the night the Tabularium fell. It was Colonel Halde's code.
Dace asked her whether she could call that bird down if it went over again. Mirren was a careful child, and told him she did not know but could try. Dace told her to try. It is the first recorded instance of the experiment that would become the Echo Mast.

CODE 78E. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 3

The Third did not take long to recover the ground, though it took longer than it had taken to lose it.
In the north, the Fifth Line Phalanx was filled up again from the vats and from the depot at Kappa. A regiment the Command had held back on the ceiling for a reformation peak further up the Phlegethon came down the Stair to take the Forty-First's place, which the Forty-First's own soldiers did as they came back from Watchpoint Hades, in their thousands, and were posted to other regiments, since the Forty-First no longer existed. The Fifth went forward again against the second belt in the way it had gone at the outer belt. The Mattocks came up, and the cohorts dug, and the trenches crept across the ash under the guns, metre by metre.
They were three cycles into the new rotation when the sun came through the ceiling.
The Fifth's spotters on the lip of the Mora Cut saw it first. A light came down out of the dark above the Flats, two hundred kilometres to the north, where the Phlegethon came round its upper bend at Depth 38. It looked like a piece of the sun falling. It came down on a long slant, burning, from the ceiling toward the ash, and the spotters watched it fall for twenty-seven seconds by the counter on their periscope. Then it hit.
The Flats shook. The trenches of the Fifth shook. Up and down the arc, from the Mora Cut to the Demus Gorge, cinder ran down the revetments and the duckboards jumped, and soldiers in the dugouts thought the Archflamen had blown a mine under the line. On the northern skyline a column of fire stood up from the impact, kilometres high, lit from underneath by the river. It burned for seven minutes, with a sound the Fifth's soldiers could hear two hundred kilometres away, a long roar like engines. Then it died down, and there was a glow on the northern skyline that did not go out for a rotation.
The Répertoire had the registry within the hour. The Fifth's officers read it on their slates in the command dugouts. Most of them had never heard of the ship. A few of the older ones had.

Ten minutes after the registry, the klaxons went on every quarterdeck and in every hold of the theatre, and Athena's own voice came down every relay in Tartarus at once, into every trench and dugout and command post on the Cinder Flats. The Third's soldiers had never heard it before. They had heard the Répertoire's verdicts read by officers. They had not heard the Répertoire speak.

QUARTERDECK NINETEEN. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 3

Lord-General Castorre read the verdict twice at his terminal, and then he asked his staff whether it applied to the Third.
His chief of staff, a hard-faced Athenian named Leda Corvis, told him that it applied to every mortal combat asset in the theatre. That was what it said.
Castorre's objection, as recorded in the Quarterdeck log, was that the Third was engaged in a siege and that Grid Delta lay two hundred kilometres up the river. Corvis already had the answer. The Répertoire had sent it down with the verdict, in a second leaf addressed to the Third's command alone. The Third was to send two Trench Regiments and the reserve of the Fourth Assault Phalanx up the ceiling relays to the Wedge at once, to reinforce from the rear as the clarification required. The rest of the Third was to initiate total assault where it stood. It would make a general attack on the second belt of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal along its whole front, under continuous saturation fire, to hold every enemy asset on the Cinder Flats in place while the Wedge was driven in at Grid Delta. The Répertoire did not want the Archflamen's army, or anything that answered to it in the river, free to move north.
Castorre had planned for the second belt to take a rotation of sapping before anyone attacked it. He noted that the Third had not yet reached the belt. Corvis corrected him. The Third had reached it on twelve leaves and was inside two kilometres of it on the rest. He noted that the attack would have only six regiments, and Corvis replied that the Répertoire's own figure was six.
He signed it. The Répertoire did not ask to be argued with, and in six hundred years of the Third's records no Lord-General who had argued with a CODE 78 had kept his command for a rotation afterwards. The log shows that he then sat at the terminal for some time before asking who had crashed the ship. Corvis could tell him only what the registry said, that it was an Alpha-Class entity identified as Minerva. She advised that the Wedge command at Grid Delta was Lord Commander Varric von Romhardt's, and that if anyone had seen who came down with the ship, it would be him.
Castorre asked for a line to Varric's base. The relay told him that the base had been emptied. Every officer and runner in it had gone out with the assault.

THE SECOND BELT. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLES 3 TO 9

The Twenty-Second and the Thirty-Third Trench Regiments went up the Stair that night with the Fourth Assault Phalanx's reserve, a hundred and eighty thousand soldiers and two hundred tanks, and were on the ceiling relays to Grid Delta by the end of the next watch. The Third never got the Thirty-Third back. It went into the Wedge at Sector Nineteen-F with Varric's assault on the first cycle and was still in the Wedge four rotations later, holding a piece of the line that the Répertoire had promised would be permanent, and that was.
The rest of the Third attacked the second belt.
Six regiments went in on the fifth cycle of Rotation Four along the line from the Mora Cut to the Lethe Cut, after nine hours of fire that the Third's gunners afterwards called the Saturation. Every gun on the Flats fired, without a pause and without a plan, as the verdict said. The Phaethons fired so fast that two of them split their barrels. The Archflamen's trenches on the second belt vanished under the smoke. When the guns lifted, the Third went over the top.
In most places the attack failed at the wire. The second belt's wire had been laid for two rotations, and it was deeper than the outer belt's, and the nests behind it were older and harder, and the Saturation had not been aimed at them. The Third's dead lay in heaps in front of it.
In other places the attack got in. The Third's soldiers took the Archflamen's forward trenches and then the support trenches behind them, and then the attack stopped. Every one of the Archflamen's guns had been ranged on his own forward trenches for a rotation, against this day. The counter-barrage came down on the Third's soldiers in the trenches they had taken, and cut them off from their own lines behind them. Then the Archflamen's garrison cohorts came up out of their shelters and counter-attacked before the Third could reinforce, with the barrage still falling, and drove the Third back out.
It went that way for six cycles. The verdict had said continuous. The Third's waves went in and were thrown back, and went in again. Each cycle the attack was widened to a neighbouring leaf to take out the fire from its flank, and each cycle it failed there too. On the ninth cycle, the Répertoire lifted the CODE 78E for the Cinder Flats. The Wedge at Grid Delta had held. Its message to the Third said that the Archflamen's army had been fixed in place as required. It said the Third's general attack had achieved its object.
The Third's general attack on the second belt had gained three hundred metres on two leaves and cost three hundred and twelve thousand transfers. Eleven thousand of them were null.

THE LONG STALEMATE. ROTATION FOUR, CYCLE 9 TO ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 400

After the Saturation, the Third went back to sapping. The Mattocks came up and the cohorts dug, and the trench lines crept across the ash toward the second belt, a metre at a time. Soon the Flats inside the old outer belt were a web of saps and parallels and communication trenches, and the web thickened every cycle, and by the end of Rotation Four it was a maze that the Third's own runners got lost in.
The Third attacked the second belt hundreds of times over the next year of front time. A regiment took a nest, and the counter-barrage cut it off, and the garrison took the nest back. A cohort got into a trench, and was killed in it. The Archflamen's raiders came the other way and took the Third's forward saps and filled them in with the Third's dead. No breakthrough came. Another rotation passed.
In the north the Fifth Line Phalanx did better. It had been filled up again, and it had a grudge, and it was fighting in ground that the Archflamen had not had time to fortify, between the old northern spur and the main line of the second belt. Without the cover of his deep shelters, the Arsenal's cohorts were pounded by the Third's guns whenever they came out to fight. Slowly the Fifth took back what it had lost in the counter-attack. It took six months to retake the ground lost in a week. In the last month of it, the Fifth reached the thrall-town at Ore Head, a settlement of forty thousand prefabricated huts at the northern end of the second belt where the Arsenal had housed the ore-lift gangs. The Third's guns shelled it for a month, and then the Fifth took it with almost no fighting. Nothing was left of the huts.
By the end of Rotation Five, the Third's line ran around the whole of the second belt from the Mora Cut to the Lethe Cut, and the Arsenal was ringed by a front of a hundred and nine kilometres.
On the way in, the Third had overrun most of the Arsenal's outer stores, the ammunition dumps and ration depots the Arsenal kept on the Flats for its patrols. Every one of them had been stripped. The Archflamen had moved everything closer to the Acropolis, down to the last round.
The war went on as it had, and it ate soldiers every cycle. The artillery duel never stopped. The Flats between the lines were churned into a field of craters so dense that a soldier could step from one into the next without touching level ground. Wherever the Third gained ground, the Archflamen's counter-attacks took it back. Both sides harassed the other without a break, with mortars and raids and marksmen, so that no one on either side of the line could rest. For months at a time the front did not move.
The Third's Lord-General wanted a breakthrough. For now, he had the attrition.

THE VATS AT WATCHPOINT HADES. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 117

Private Jory Venn went up for the eighth time on the hundred and twelfth cycle of Rotation Five, in a sap on Leaf 31-14, when a solar round came down in the bay where he was standing. He woke on the hundred and seventeenth with the tube in his mouth and the light over him, and the same attendant was saying his number.
The vat check on recovery was standard. Venn gave his name, his mother's name and the motto of the Third. The attendant examined his eyes for longer than usual and noted on his record that his responses were slow, which was within the expected range for an eighth recovery. She then directed him to the list on the wall by the showers and told him to report any names he knew to the clerk on the way out.
The list was four metres long. It was in small print. At the top of it someone had written, by hand, NULL, ROTATION FIVE, CINDER FLATS, and under it were the names. He found three from the Fourteenth. One of them was the optio who had replaced Desh. He told the clerk, and the clerk wrote it down, and asked him whether the optio had been near a relay pylon when he went up. Venn said he did not know. The clerk wrote that down too.
He was posted back down on the hundred and twenty-sixth cycle. On the cable down he sat beside an Old Ladder from the Seventh Line Phalanx, a woman of forty with her hair gone white, who had been up thirty-one times. She asked him how many he had. He said eight.
Venn later gave her remarks to a Hunt interviewer as the first time he heard the problem put into words. The Third was losing more soldiers on the way up than it used to. There had always been a null list, she said, but it used to be a single sheet. Something on the Flats was catching them on the way up.

CEILING-LANCE SEVEN. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 402

On the four hundred and second cycle of Rotation Five, the Twenty-Ninth Trench Regiment made a raid in strength on Leaf 50-27, against Ceiling-Lance Seven.
The lances were the reason the Third fought the Arsenal on foot. Sixty of them stood in hardened silos across the inner belt and the spur, and ten more in the second belt, and any ship that came down through the ceiling to fire on the Arsenal came inside their reach. Lance Seven was one of the ten. It stood in the second belt in front of the Twenty-Ninth, in a silo cut forty metres down into the basalt, and its emitter rose out of the ash behind razor-wire and a minefield and four gun emplacements. Most of the silo was underground. The Arsenal had built it eight thousand years before to stop anything with wings coming up out of the Phlegethon, and it had stopped them.
The Répertoire wanted it gone. With Lance Seven out, a corridor would open through the lance envelope above the northern Flats, and the Tartarus Fleet could bring a heavy cruiser down into it to fire on the second belt from the ceiling. One heavy cruiser's main guns would do in a cycle what the Third's Phaethons did in a month.
The raid would go without a barrage, to keep surprise. The Twenty-Ninth's breachers would infiltrate across no-man's land in the dark, through the wire and the minefield, and take the silo's surface works at the first bell. Then they would go down into the silo and destroy the lance. When the breachers signalled that the silo was taken, the Twenty-Ninth's cohorts would go over the top in daylight and occupy the ground around it, and dig in.

LEAF 50-27. THE DARK WATCH

The breachers went out at the start of the dark watch, eleven sections of them, two hundred and sixty soldiers. They had left their plate behind. Plate could not crawl. They wore grey smocks over light armour, with their faces and hands blacked with cinder, and carried carbines and charges and wire-cutters and knives.
They went out over the parapet into the craters, slowly, one section at a time, and crawled from crater to crater through the paste. The seep-storm had come down two cycles before, and the ground was grey mud to the elbow. Every few minutes the Arsenal's gunners fired a flare over Leaf 50-27. When a flare burst, the breachers lay still on their faces in the mud, or on their backs with their arms out like the dead around them, and there were plenty of dead around them to copy. When the flare came down and went out, they crawled on.
One section crawled into an Arsenal patrol in a crater halfway across. There was a fight, with knives at first and then carbines. On the second belt, the sentries heard it and took it for the usual business of the dark watch, a patrol meeting a patrol, and fired a flare and some rounds into the dark and went back to their periscopes.
Seven sections reached the wire around Lance Seven before the third hour. They cut through the first belt and cleared a lane for the sections behind them. They were in the second belt of wire when a flare burst directly overhead.
An Arsenal sentry in the nearest gun emplacement saw shapes in the wire that had not been there an hour before. He shouted. A heavy gun in the emplacement opened up on the wire, and the Arsenal's garrison in the silo's surface works was roused by its centurions.
The breachers had no choice. They went through the wire as fast as they could, and threw their charges at the emplacements, and ran for the silo's surface works, to keep from being cut to pieces in the wire. Leaf 50-27 went up in fire. For the rest of the dark watch the Twenty-Ninth's sentries watched it from the forward trench, the flashes of charges and grenades and the long streams of tracer from the emplacements, and could not tell who was winning.
When the first bell came, Lance Seven was hidden in smoke and dust. The breachers had not signalled. The Twenty-Ninth's relay operators had heard nothing on the breachers' bands but the Arsenal's jamming.
The Twenty-Ninth's colonel asked the Ninth Line Phalanx what to do. The Ninth told him to attack as planned. The orders said that the cohorts went at the first bell, and the Répertoire's estimate was that the breachers had probably taken the surface works, and the jamming was probably why there was no signal. The colonel gave the order. The Twenty-Ninth's cohorts went over the top in the half-light, into no-man's land, toward the smoke.
The breachers had not taken the surface works. Most of them were dead in the wire, and the rest were fighting in the emplacements, or taken.
The Arsenal's gunners on the second belt saw the Twenty-Ninth's cohorts coming and fired everything they had into Leaf 50-27. The ground between the lines became a slaughterhouse. The cohorts that did not go to ground were cut down in the open, in rows, and the cohorts that went to ground were shelled in their craters, until the Twenty-Ninth's colonel called them back. The survivors came back into their own trenches across ground covered in their own dead.
The next cycle, the Third's spotters looked at Lance Seven through their periscopes. The surface works were scorched. Two of the four emplacements were wrecked. The emitter was untouched. At the change of the second watch it fired, as it had every cycle for eight thousand years, at a Stymphalian swarm passing high over the northern Flats, and took the swarm out of the dark in a single beam.
Six hundred and forty soldiers of the Twenty-Ninth were transferred in the raid. Of the two hundred and sixty breachers, nineteen came back to their own lines alive. Most of the rest were transferred in the wire. The Répertoire counted thirty-one as taken.

THE WIRE OF LEAF 50-27. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLES 404 TO 411

The thirty-one taken breachers came back on the fourth cycle after the raid. The Twenty-Ninth's sentries saw them at the first bell, on the Arsenal's wire in front of Lance Seven, three hundred metres from the Twenty-Ninth's forward trench.
They had been tied to the wire stakes upright, with their arms spread along the wire, in a line across the front of the silo. Some of them were moving. The Arsenal's sentries left them there and did not fire on them. They fired on anything of the Third's that went toward them.
The Twenty-Ninth's colonel asked for permission to send a party out to bring them in. The Ninth Line Phalanx refused it. The wire in front of Lance Seven had been pre-sighted by every gun on that part of the second belt, and the Répertoire assessed any rescue as a second massacre for the sake of thirty-one soldiers who were going to be transferred anyway.
The Twenty-Ninth's medicus, who had been watching through a periscope since the first bell, disputed the assessment. Her report to the colonel stated that the thirty-one were not going to be transferred, and asked him to look at them. The breachers on the wire were alive, and the Arsenal was keeping them that way. There were thralls going out to them at night, the sentries said, with water. The breachers' implants were in the base of the skull, and every one of the thirty-one had a dressing at the base of the skull.
They died on the wire one at a time over the next week, from the cold, and from thirst, and from what had been done to them before they were tied up. The Twenty-Ninth's relay operators watched their bands. Each time one of the thirty-one died, his implant fired, and the upload went up into the noosphere, and the Twenty-Ninth's operators logged it going.
None of the thirty-one reached Watchpoint Hades.
When the last of them had stopped moving, the Arsenal left them on the wire. They were still there a rotation later. The Twenty-Ninth's soldiers looked at them every morning through their periscopes, and stopped looking, and looked again.
The Répertoire's technical officers sent their report on Leaf 50-27 up to the Command at Watchpoint Hades. It said that the Archflamen's people had found a way to interfere with the transfer of soldiers in their hands, at or before death, by work on the implant. It said this was beyond the ordinary competence of a forge-hold's armourers. It asked whether the Arsenal had help.
The Command noted it.

THE KATABASION. ROTATION FIVE, CYCLE 411

On the night the last breacher died on the wire, the Archflamen went down into the Katabasion alone, as he had in the years of his seclusion, and prayed before the bones of the First Descent until the dark watch.
Haruspex Dace waited for him at the top of the stair. He had the girl Mirren with him, wrapped in a blanket, asleep on her feet.
The conversation that followed was recovered from the Tabularium's own records after the siege. Dace reported that Mirren had called down the last breacher's upload off the wire and held it for nearly a minute before it went. Orrhae asked what it had been like. Dace answered that she said it had been frightened and had asked for its mother. He then set out the principle on which the Mast would be built. An upload was in the open for seven seconds between the implant and the first relay. If the girl could hold one for a minute, she could hold one until it was empty.
The Archflamen asked whether that was what the Imperator wanted. Dace's answer is the earliest statement of the Kindlers' doctrine in the Arsenal's records. The Imperator had gone down into the dark and come back up through it. The Empire's soldiers had forgotten what the dark was, and went up and came back down as though it were a lift. The Imperator wanted them to remember. Dace added that there were others in the river who wanted the same, who had been waiting for someone to ask them, and that he had asked.
The record notes no reply from the Archflamen. He went back down the stair, and Dace carried the child to bed.
Four cycles later, a picket of the Fifth Line Phalanx on the lip of the Mora Cut reported a column of soldiers coming up out of the Cut on the old ore road below Ore Head, from the direction of the Styx. The picket said the soldiers were wearing the Empire's armour. Their transponders answered with the Empire's codes.
