THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART SEVEN. ON THE CUSP OF DEFEAT

"The Third will pay what it costs. Tell me the price, and then stop telling me."
Marshal Theron Kastellan, on taking command

A NEW COMMANDER. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 1

The word of the Third's position on the Flats reached the Lord Marshal of the Tartarean Theatre at Watchpoint Hades at the turn of Rotation Nine.
The Lord Marshal saw the whole of the theatre from the command deck at Hades, every hold and every front from Depth 3 to the Cocytus. She had more armies than she could count, and a direct line to the Senate on Olympus Primaris, and it was her staff, with the Répertoire, that had drawn up the plan for the reduction of the Arsenal and given it to Aurelian Castorre. Now it fell to the same staff to save it. She had already given Logothete Irene Vasska of the Dionysian Logistics Directorate the power to inquire into the Third's command. Vasska's first recommendation was to remove it. It had failed in its duty and failed the Imperator, and wasted shells and nectar and vat time that the Directorate had put at its disposal in quantities never before given to one siege.
The news of his removal made little difference to Lord-General Castorre. He took it at his terminal in the command ring of Quarterdeck Nineteen, calmly, knowing that a man of his house could not fall far. A post was already waiting for him on the staff of the Second Ceiling Army on Callistus, which was mustering against the reformation peaks at Depth 12. It was the officers below him who would carry the weight of the failure. Those who had not already found themselves posts elsewhere were sent to the Forlorn Cohorts, or to field commands on the Cocytus front, where the ice took soldiers and did not give them back. For most of them it amounted to a death sentence, and on the Cocytus a death sentence was meant literally. Leda Corvis was posted to the Cocytus. She went down the cables on the twentieth cycle of Rotation Nine and the Third did not hear of her again.
With the Third's command being replaced, there was room at the top, and there were powerful voices waiting to fill it. The question of who would run the war was open.
On the ceiling world of Olympia Sacra, the Pontifical College said that the war had failed for lack of faith. The reduction of the Arsenal should be a crusade to free the Katabasion and the bones of the First Descent from the apostate. Orrhae had been one of the College's own, and if anybody was to bring him to justice, it should be the College's own forces. The College could send its temple legions to steady the line on the Flats and stave off defeat, it said, but only if it could name the Third's commander. Enforcement backed the College. Its Lictor-Superiors were ready to take command, and it proposed to put the whole of the Third under Enforcement's authority and a Lictor at the head of every regiment. It was a strong alliance, and the lobbying for the Third was fierce. In the halls of Watchpoint Hades, the Third had become a prize that a great many people wanted.
The Directorate would not hear of it. The Third must stay under the Directorate's control. It was the Directorate's supply lines and the Directorate's stores that the Third lived on. If the Directorate could not name the commander, Vasska warned, the priority of the war would have to be reviewed. Whoever took the Third without the Directorate behind them would find that shells were scarcer than they had been, and that the vats at Hades had other fronts to fill.
As commander of the theatre, the Lord Marshal held that the appointment was hers, whoever petitioned her. She had a hard political task in reconciling them. In the end she kept the right to name the commander in the field, and let the College and Enforcement put their own people on his staff and in his administration. Few of them were happy with it. The new command would be under pressure from the start to turn the war around. The College in particular kept up its pressure and kept back its temple legions, until it had enough influence to think them worth sending.
The officer the Lord Marshal chose to take this poisoned cup was Marshal Theron Kastellan of Pyrrhus Command. He was a hard-fighting general with a long record of victories, and old now, and the Lord Marshal thought him a safe pair of hands. Like Castorre, he was a patrician, of a house with a long history in the upper ranks of the theatre. He brought a large staff with him, and among them his own flamen, a sign of how seriously he took his prayers. He was a man of strict routine and fixed ideas of how a war should be fought, and he had the experience to fight this one. He had commanded the Sixty-Third Trench Regiment of the Second Siege Army through the twelve-rotation siege of Hold Charybdis, on the Cocytus, and he had won it.
He had also served for forty years under Stephanos Pyrrhus. When the Lord Marshal gave him the Third, she gave him a second letter, under the Pyrrhus seal, which he did not show his staff. It asked him to find out, if he could, what had happened to Stephanos Pyrrhus's daughter at Segment Twelve.
Kastellan's first task, once he had his staff, was to put together a force to relieve the Third. He had to break the drowned fleet's hold on the low geometry, get supplies and soldiers down onto the Flats, and open again the supply lines that the Third needed to win.
His appointment marked a change in the Empire's handling of the Arsenal. To Kastellan, the siege had gone on far too long. It was time to commit enough to finish it. The Third had done a good job of wearing the Archflamen down. Now fresh forces and a fresh approach would break him.
He put together a relief force. He went first to the Forge of Hephaestus. The Forge had a long connection with the Third, and had built most of its tanks and all of its Phaethons. The drowned Behemoths would have to be met and destroyed, and only Behemoths could do it. The Forge's lords agreed to send the Forge Cohort Talos, the oldest of the Forge's walking cohorts. Deep in the Forge, the cohort's engines were made ready. Rites were held. The cradle-crews were briefed and blessed. The feet of every Behemoth were anointed with ichor from the Forge's own altars, a sign that the engines would lay the river's dead at the Imperator's feet. When the rites were done, the Behemoths went into their forge-lighters for Tartarus.
Next, the Third needed soldiers, and the ceiling worlds' drafts were called on again. More soldiers were needed than ever before, and above all sappers. The Forge sent nine regiments of Hephaestean sappers with the Behemoths, engineers who had been trained to dig under fortresses since before the Empire had a name for them. More soldiers and guns and tanks were embarked for Depth 40.
The Tartarus Fleet would provide the ships and the escort for the relief convoy. It also put two Wrath-pattern strike wings at the Third's disposal, a wing of interceptors and a wing of bombers, with Lantern escorts. These would fight for the ceiling geometry over the Flats, which the drowned had taken, and give the Third a long arm it had never had. The slow war of attrition was becoming total war, with almost everything the theatre could put into it.

THE LAST STAND OF THE FIFTY-FOURTH. ROTATION NINE, CYCLES 1 TO 30

None of it could help the Fifty-Fourth Trench Regiment.
The Fifty-Fourth had been left on the edge of the Lethe Cut, surrounded, with its back to a drop of seven kilometres into the grey mist at the bottom of the Cut. Its sacrifice had saved the rest of the Eighth and Ninth Line Phalanxes. That did not comfort the Lethe Hold drafts in its trenches, who knew what was coming.
The instrument of it came up out of the Cut.
The Unremembered had come up the Lethe when the Echo Mast called. They had come up the river under the grey mist, where the Lethe met the Phlegethon at Depth 43, and they had been waiting since the drowned fleet rose for the Archflamen's war to give them the ground they wanted. They were the Lethe's own: soldiers of the Empire, and of the old wars before it, who had drunk from the Lethe, or been made to, and forgotten who they were, and then been given something else by the river to be instead. Their Legate called herself Ysander Pale. She had not had another name for nine hundred years. The southern Flats, with the dead lying unburied in the craters and the ground sour with eight rotations of shellfire, were the kind of ground they liked. With the Fifty-Fourth caught on the edge of their river, the Unremembered gave it their full attention.
The Third's commanders had suspected since the first rotation that the Arsenal held proscribed weapons. The Arsenal's vaults had held, for six thousand years, a store of distillates the Empire had forbidden and had never been able to destroy. They had fallen to the Archflamen when the Tabularium fell. Chief among them was a distillate of the Lethe itself, the seventh of the nine grades the old Hephaestean chemists had refined from the river before the Senate stopped them, which the Arsenal's inventory called Grey Seventh.
Grey Seventh was a heavy grey vapour that stayed close to the ground. A soldier who breathed it forgot. He forgot the last hour first, and then the last day, and then his name, and his mother, and how to hold a rifle, in about the time it took to say the motto of the Third. It did not need to be breathed. It went through the skin, and through the eyes, and through the seals of a respirator the way the Lethe went through a hull. Soldiers who caught a light dose came out of it in a few hours with a hole in their memory and a headache. Soldiers who caught a heavy dose did not come out of it. They stood up out of their trenches and wandered, empty, and if they were killed while they were empty, their implants uploaded an empty room. The upload arrived at Watchpoint Hades as a body's worth of nothing.
Until now the Archflamen had kept his Grey Seventh in the vaults. He had held it back as his last weapon, for the day when everything seemed lost.
The coming of the drowned had made the Archflamen's army stronger, but it had made the Archflamen weaker. Among his new allies were many who had no intention of obeying him. The Cinderborn and the Unremembered had come for their own reasons, and where those reasons did not match his, they ignored him. He had no hold on the warbands that now went where they liked across the Flats. Even Hesper had little influence over them, and did not try to use much. To her the Archflamen's revolt was a chance to do the work of something larger, and nothing more. The allies had all come to destroy the Third, and they had different ideas of how. Orrhae was shut away in the Katabasion, under the Phaethon fire every night, hearing reports from his councillors, of whom the first was Dace, who now commanded the Sworn. He had little idea of what was happening outside the Wall.
The Unremembered had not gone straight into battle when they came up the Cut, as the Cinderborn had. They had gathered their strength, and chosen their first victims with care. They chose the Fifty-Fourth. What they meant to do to it was an experiment, not a battle fought for ground. It was a rite, a preparing of the ground. They took the Grey Seventh from the vaults by force, from under the Sworn, and the Archflamen did not stop them.

THE LETHE CUT. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 11

The first attack came at the change of the first watch on the eleventh cycle of Rotation Nine. There was no barrage.
The Fifty-Fourth's sentries on the edge of the Cut saw a pale light down in the Cut, staining the mist. It grew stronger, and the mist began to climb, thick and heavy, up the face of the Cut toward them. The sentries gave the alarm. The mist came up over the lip of the Cut, and lay down on the ash, and ran into every shell-hole and crater, and filled them. Then it came into the trenches.
The Fifty-Fourth had been trained, as every regiment of the Third was trained, to fight through a gas attack. It had respirators and seals. They did not help. The Grey Seventh came through the seals and through the skin, and the Fifty-Fourth's soldiers began to forget.
In some places the mist was thin, and the soldiers it reached sat down in the fire bays holding their heads and came out of it an hour later knowing nothing about the last cycle. Where it was thick, it took everything. Whole maniples stopped. A section of the Fifty-Fourth's Third Cohort, standing to in the forward bay, put down their rifles one after another, and looked at each other, and at the trench, and at the rifles, as if they had never seen any of it before. One of them took off his helmet. Another climbed up out of the trench and walked out over the wire into no-man's land, in no hurry, looking around her at the craters. Then the first attack came out of the mist, and the soldiers who were standing in it did not know what it was.
Out of the grey came engines, drowned Hoplons and drowned Thureos and old assault carriers of a kind the Forge had not built in a thousand years, their hulls rotted through and black with Lethe-water. They came across no-man's land against almost nothing, and the ramps came down, and the Unremembered came out.
It was hard to believe they had been soldiers of the Empire once. Their armour had rotted on them over the centuries and the river had grown into the gaps, grey and soft, like fungus on wet wood. Their faces were smooth. They had forgotten that they had faces, and the river had not bothered to remind them. But they had not forgotten how to fight. They went into the Fifty-Fourth's trenches with the discipline of the old legions, firing as they came, and they swept the fire bays clear and threw grenades of Grey Seventh down the dugout stairs. In the bays, the Fifty-Fourth's bayonets turned on their armour. Their own blades, long and grey, went through the Fifty-Fourth's armour as through cloth.
The Fifty-Fourth fought well. It stood and fought to the end. No other regiment of the Third could have stood the Grey Seventh and the Unremembered together any better, and it could not stand them for long. In desperation, its centurions called down its own guns on its own trenches, and the shells came down on the Fifty-Fourth and the Unremembered together.
The mist was thinning. As it lifted, the Fifty-Fourth's sentries saw more coming across no-man's land behind the Unremembered: a horde of thralls and Sworn, coming on through the counter-barrage in the Unremembered's wake. They too got into the trenches, and drove the Fifty-Fourth's survivors back along the communication trenches, and took the support line.
No help could be sent. It was too late for the Fifty-Fourth, and it had no choice but to make its last stand. There was nowhere to go. Its trenches fell one by one. Its field guns were abandoned when their crews ran out of shells. The regiment's last tanks fought from their pits, hull down, shooting at the drowned carriers as they came. The drowned carriers drove over the trenches, and the trench walls fell in on whoever was in them. One by one the Fifty-Fourth's tanks were knocked out.

THE TOXIC WAR. ROTATION NINE, CYCLES 11 TO 15

Night fell on the southern Flats, and the fighting did not stop. Colonel Corin Vadas reported to the Eighth Line Phalanx that he could hold for one more cycle. After that his ammunition would be gone, his last guns overrun and his regiment scattered.
His soldiers held for three more cycles, to the last of them and the last round, as the line shrank back toward the lip of the Cut. The Unremembered let the Grey Seventh down on them again and again. They did not take the Fifty-Fourth's dead away. They gathered them into heaps in the craters and left them in the mist. The empty ones, the soldiers of the Fifty-Fourth who had breathed it and lived, they did not kill. They let them wander. The empty ones walked about the craters of the southern Flats, among the heaps of the dead, looking at things.
All contact with the Fifty-Fourth ended at the fourth bell of the fifteenth cycle. The last pocket had been broken.
Behind them, the Unremembered left a charnel ground, with the grey still lying in the bottoms of the trenches and the deepest craters. Leaves 61-24 and 62-24 had been made into a place that belonged to the Lethe. Things could be seen moving in the trenches and the dugouts, things out of the river, that crawled and that walked on too many legs, and the empty soldiers of the Fifty-Fourth walked among them and were not harmed.
The Fifty-Fourth Trench Regiment had gone into the pocket on the edge of the Lethe Cut with a hundred and forty-one thousand soldiers. The Répertoire logged a hundred and nine thousand of them as transferred in the four cycles of the stand. Every one of the hundred and nine thousand arrived at Watchpoint Hades null. The rest, the thirty-two thousand empty, were never transferred at all, and the Répertoire could not list them as dead while their implants still reported a heartbeat. It entered them as absent.
It was the largest loss of the siege in a single action. It was also the first time in the war that a regiment had been lost entire, without one soldier coming back.

LETHE HOLD, DEPTH 31. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 16

Commander Thaleia of Lethe Hold read the list at her desk in the Hold's command bunker on the sixteenth cycle, three hours after the Répertoire sent it.
The Fifty-Fourth had been raised in the Hold, from the Hold's own ration rolls, when the shells stopped coming in Rotation Two. She had signed its draft orders herself. She had not known many of them. She had known some of them. She had known the colonel, Vadas, who had run the Hold's ration board, and had volunteered for the regiment when it was raised. The Hold's ration board had sent the regiment's soldiers' families onto plasma rations, and he had not felt that he could stay behind.
She read down the list. It was a hundred and nine thousand names, and the Répertoire had put NULL beside each one, and at the bottom it had put the thirty-two thousand ABSENT.
Her adjutant asked whether the families were to be told. Thaleia ordered that all of them be told, including the families of the absent. They were to be told the word absent and nothing more.
When he had gone, she logged the Fifty-Fourth's loss on the Hold's register. The Hold's register had a code for a regiment lost entire with no return. It had not been used in her time. She looked it up. It was CODE 44.

THE GREY

The Unremembered had only begun. The Fifty-Fourth had been a test of the Grey Seventh, and the test had worked, and now they could turn it on the rest of the Third. They used it from then on wherever they went, and the Third's soldiers soon learned that a grey light in the craters meant an attack was coming within the hour. Wherever the Unremembered went, they left the ground behind them grey, and spread their river into the Flats.
It was deliberate. The Unremembered had no interest in the Archflamen's war of attrition. It did not matter to them whether the Arsenal stood or fell. What mattered to them was what the Lethe wanted, which was for the Flats to forget that they had ever been held by anyone. They were preparing the ground. They were making the southern Flats into the Lethe's ground, a place fit for the things in the river that had not yet come up, and were waiting to. It was not war as the Third's commanders understood war. It was a rite, carried out with care, that made way for something worse.
The Third's soldiers called the southern Flats the Grey after that. The trench maps from the Arsenal campaign carry the Grey in pale wash, from the Lethe Cut almost to the Demus Rim, and the wash spreads from map to map, rotation by rotation, like a stain.

THE SECOND BATTLE OF THE LOW GEOMETRY. ROTATION NINE, CYCLES 30 TO 300

While the command was being changed, the Third's regiments still had to face the enemy in front of them, and keep their soldiers and their stores alive while they did it. All attacks were stopped. The aim of the siege was put aside for the aim of surviving. The Archflamen and his allies had the initiative now, and they made the attacks. The Third dug in deep, and held where it could, and rationed what it had until relief came.
While Kastellan's staff gathered the relief force, the Archflamen's army used its advantage. With the drowned behind it, it attacked in greater strength than it ever had. The Third's guns were rationed to a few rounds per gun per watch, so the Third could not break up the attacks before they came across, and many of them took the Third's trenches and held them. Salients were driven deep into the Third's line. The Third was losing ground that had cost it millions of transfers, and being driven back a metre at a time. For almost a rotation, the Third had nothing but defeats and withdrawals. As its soldiers and its stores ran down, it seemed it was at the edge of losing the war.
Kastellan had been working without a pause. On the three hundredth cycle of Rotation Nine, his convoy was ready to come down to Depth 40: transports and supply ships, and the great forge-lighters of the Forge Cohort Talos, with a strong escort. The Fleet had not forgotten the battle of the low geometry. The drowned battleship was still out there, and could not be ignored.
The Fleet's first move was to send scouts down into the geometry over the Flats. Its fastest ships, the three Lantern destroyers of Picket Ithaca, went down through the ceiling cracks to find the drowned fleet's strength and report. They found the wrecks of the battle drifting in the low geometry, the Themis Unbowed and the Long Labour and the rest, but no enemy came out at them. They went back up and reported all quiet.
Nobody knew where the drowned fleet had gone, or where the Eye of Light was. She might still be in the geometry, among the wrecks over the spur, or down in the river cloud. It would be wise to expect an attack. The Fleet gave the convoy two cruisers, the twin heavy cruisers Lysander's Oath and Kallisto, and a full squadron of eight Lantern frigates. The convoy went down through the ceiling ready for a second battle.
It came down slowly and carefully, the transports in the middle and the escorts around them. The Lysander's Oath led, and the Kallisto came last. For the first cycles nothing came at them. They left the ceiling cracks behind and came out over the Flats and still nothing moved. Perhaps the drowned had gone back down into the river, or gone off to some other bend of the Phlegethon to look for richer prey.
Most of the drowned fleet had in fact gone. Its battle won, the raiders had gone back to the river. The Eye of Light had not. She had been lying among the wrecks of the first battle in the low geometry for most of a rotation, with her reactors banked and her drowned crew at their stations, as still as the hulks around her.
She was nearly impossible to see. Her hull was black with river-fire and blue with Cocytus ice, and the wrecks drifted around her, and she gave off nothing the Fleet's augurs could read. The Forsworn had served on the Empire's ships for six hundred years. They knew what a convoy escort looked for, and they had made sure the Eye of Light showed none of it. As the convoy came over the wrecks, she sprang.
Her reactors came up all at once, river-fire roaring down cables and conduits that had last been fed by Apollo's engineers. Her power signature lit up the augur of every ship in the convoy, and the alarms went on every deck. She was already in range. As the escort captains called for full speed, her first broadside hit. Two of the Lantern frigates disappeared, hit again and again by her main batteries. The Lysander's Oath's shields screamed under a second volley, which went down her hull and took out her starboard thrusters and her steering. A supply transport loaded with Phaethon shells was crippled and left drifting.
The cruisers answered as fast as their crews could. The Lysander's Oath's guns came in ragged at first, and then steadily. The Kallisto swung around, fired a wide spread of torpedoes, and went straight at the Eye of Light. While the cruisers fought, the transports made for the cables at Kappa, with the frigates putting themselves between the cargo and the Eye of Light's guns. It was brave and it cost them two more ships crippled, as the Eye of Light fired everything she had.
The two cruisers came into close range. The Eye of Light was a battleship, and her armour was thick, but she could not ignore two heavy cruisers at that range. She turned her guns on them and took the Kallisto's torpedoes as she did it. Fires broke out inside her, showing red through the split in her hull where the Cocytus ice had been packed. She was hurt.
She did not care. Her batteries hammered the two cruisers. The Lysander's Oath was caught in it and tossed like a boat in a storm, burning, with her atmosphere venting flame into the geometry. The Kallisto was close now, and her guns were tearing into the Eye of Light's hull, into the old wound below the burned face of the god. It was a matter of which of them would break first.
The Eye of Light broke off. She was hurt, and her prey was getting away to the cables. Her first strike had destroyed four of the escorts and left the Lysander's Oath dying. Hundreds of the Lysander's Oath's crew lay dead at their guns. But the Kallisto had held her course and never flinched, and she had torn the Eye of Light's flank open and set her burning inside. Rather than risk destruction for nothing, the Eye of Light pulled away and was soon lost again among the wrecks over the spur, with two drowned escorts close beside her. The Kallisto could have gone after her, but alone. Instead she went to the Lysander's Oath.
The Lysander's Oath was beyond saving. Her captain ordered her abandoned, and left the fires on her lower decks to burn her out. They burned for weeks. Her crew crossed to the Kallisto, and the Kallisto went on down to Kappa. The low geometry over the Flats had one more wreck.

PLANETFALL. ROTATION NINE, CYCLES 300 TO 330

The convoy had got through, narrowly. The two cruisers had saved it, and perhaps the siege. Most of the supplies reached the Flats, and all the Behemoths. The Eye of Light was still out there, but she was badly hurt, and she would not try another fight like that one.
The convoy came over Landing Shelf Kappa and began to unload. Soon soldiers were coming down the cables by the thousand, and tanks by the hundred, and shells by the thousand tonnes, with fuel and food and everything else, and all of it was sent forward to the front at once. In the middle of the cargo landers came down one of the forge-lighters, slab-sided and huge, and settled on its own cables onto the Flats at the foot of the Cinder Stair. Its doors rolled back. Out of it walked the Behemoths of the Forge Cohort Talos, under the oldest cradle-master in the Forge, the First Cradle-Lord Doros Varne.
The ground shook under them as they took their first steps on the Flats. There were twenty-two of them, in honour banners, with the eagle of the Empire and the bronze hammer of Talos on their plating. Ten Brontes-pattern Behemoths, eighty metres tall, walked at the back. In front of them walked twelve Steropes-pattern, lighter and faster, for hunting. The Forge Cohort Talos had come to meet the drowned Behemoths on the ash and take up a war that the Forge had been fighting against the river for as long as there had been Behemoths, since the first engine had gone down into the Phlegethon and come back up walking for something else.
The Flats would see it again.
The Third's soldiers came up out of their trenches to watch them come, which was against orders, and nobody stopped them. Private Jory Venn watched them from the support line on Leaf 31-14, standing on the firestep, as the Brontes went past three kilometres off with the light from the river on their plating. He had been on the Flats for nine rotations. It was the first time he could remember thinking that the Third might win.

BACK FROM THE BRINK. ROTATION NINE, CYCLE 330 TO ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 240

Even while the Fifty-Fourth was dying on the Lethe Cut, Kastellan was planning his offensive, the Third's next attempt at the inner belt. After a long time of bitter defence, the Third was being filled up and supplied every cycle, ready to attack again. New attacks were planned along the whole front for every regiment. The Third would not just hold its trenches any more. It would go out over no-man's land and take the war to the Archflamen.
It took a long time to prepare. Every regiment on the line had to take in new soldiers, arm them from the stores and post them to the cohorts. The shell and fuel stocks had to be built up, and the stores of everything else. Cohorts and batteries moved into place for the coming fights, and the colonels worked through every watch on their plans: attack plans, fire plans, reserves, and the thousand other things an offensive of that size needed.
The Kastellan Offensive was set to begin on the two hundred and fortieth cycle of Rotation Ten. Its aim was to retake all the ground lost since Gallows Rise and to break the inner belt, so that the Third could reach the Cinder Wall. It was an ambitious plan, and it took most of a rotation to make the battered regiments ready for it. It would be Kastellan's first big step toward winning the war.
His biggest decision was where to use the Behemoths. The lines of command did not allow him to give Cradle-Lord Varne orders. His reports to the Forge's lords would decide where the Forge Cohort went. In the end, after a careful review, all twenty-two would support a main effort on the front of the Seventh Line Phalanx. The Brontes and the Steropes would go in beside the Seventh's four regiments: the Twenty-Second, which had been the first regiment of the Third to come down the Cinder Stair; the Thirty-Seventh, which had made the first assault on the outer belt; the Forty-Ninth; and the Twenty-Ninth.
The Twenty-Ninth had been disbanded after the Big Push, and its colours burned. Kastellan raised it again. He took its survivors out of the Forlorn Cohorts on the Cocytus, all eleven thousand of the ones still alive, and gave them new colours and a new colonel and filled the regiment up from the drafts. He told its officers that the Twenty-Ninth had a debt on Leaf 50-27, and that he expected them to pay it.
The Fourth Assault Phalanx would back the Seventh, with its Colossi.
The first part of the plan was a long barrage. The new shells went first to the Ordnance Phalanxes and the mortar batteries. With the low geometry under the Wrath wings now, and convoys coming down to Kappa every week, millions of shells were carried forward and stacked at the gun lines for a barrage that would last for weeks before anyone went over.
The barrage built up slowly over the weeks before the attack. The shells went over without a pause, harassing the Archflamen's lines and making it dangerous to bring anything up to them. In the last cycles before the attack, the barrage grew, and grew again, until the guns were firing without a stop, barrels glowing red, breech blocks and recoil pistons wearing out under the strain.
On the two hundred and fortieth cycle of Rotation Ten, everything was ready. In the forward trenches along the arc, the assault cohorts stood with bayonets fixed, waiting for the order. Across no-man's land the shells came down on the Archflamen's front in a storm, rising to a single long roll of thunder, burying the inner belt in ash and steel. Then the guns stopped.

LEAF 54-45. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 240

On Leaf 54-45, Colonel Imre Saldane of the Twenty-Second Trench Regiment had come up to the forward trench to go over with his soldiers, with a signifer at his shoulder carrying the regiment's standard, which had come down the Cinder Stair in Rotation Zero. At the time set, he fired a single red flare. It went up over the Flats and hung there like a star. The relay operator sent the codeword to every cohort in the regiment. The order to attack had been given.
The Twenty-Second went up the ladders, as it had thousands of times before, and out into no-man's land. Behind it the Thureos and the Colossi ground into gear and went forward. The mortar batteries put down smoke and high explosive to keep the Archflamen's gunners' heads down. Further back still, on the command deck of the Brontes-pattern Behemoth Anvil of Talos, Cradle-Lord Varne received the signal. He was wired into the Behemoth's cradle through the old Hephaestean links, and he did not speak the order. He thought it, and the Behemoth's great gears and pistons heaved it forward in a long stride. The other Behemoths followed, and the Forge Cohort Talos walked into the battle.
Along the whole front of the Seventh Line Phalanx it was the same.
Infantry and tanks went forward into the defensive fire, wading through the paste to the wire and the trenches and the pillboxes of the inner belt. The tanks laid a curtain of shells across the front and swept it with their secondary guns. The field guns and mortars fired again, lobbing their shells just ahead of the infantry. Behind them, the heavy guns lifted to targets further back, or to the strongpoints. The battle was joined.
For the first hours it looked like the same battle as before, with the same result, heavy losses for small gains. But on the Seventh Line Phalanx's front the attack was going well. The Behemoths of Talos were walking forward, eighty metres of bronze and iron, and nothing in front of them stopped them. They fired on the inner belt with everything they had and smashed a road through it, with their Aegis-wards flaring blue under the Archflamen's fire. On Leaf 54-45 the Archflamen's cohorts counter-attacked with tanks and infantry, and a fight with bayonets and knives broke out in the middle of no-man's land. Tanks burned. They did not stop Varne's advance. Soon the Wrath interceptors came over low, adding their bombs and their cannon to the fire coming down on the inner belt. The forward trenches were overrun, and the Third's cohorts and breachers went on into the maze of trenches and dugouts behind them, clearing as they went.
By the end of the first cycle the Twenty-Ninth had retaken Leaf 50-27. It took down the thirty-one breachers from the Arsenal's wire in front of Ceiling-Lance Seven, what was left of them, and carried them back across no-man's land, and did not let anyone help. Every regiment that had had the Behemoths beside it reported good progress. At the end of the cycle the Behemoths went back to their laagers behind the line, and fresh cohorts and tanks were pushed forward for the next cycle.
Across the Flats, the other regiments reported a mix of success and failure. Some had been stopped by the fire, or thrown back across no-man's land by heavy counter-attacks. Wherever the Cinderborn or the Forsworn or the Unremembered came into the fight, the Third did badly. On the edge of the Grey, the Fiftieth Trench Regiment had been hit by the Grey Seventh and lost heavily, and lost them all null. The Fiftieth asked for permission to answer in kind. The Third had taken stocks of the Arsenal's own Grey Seventh shells in the outer stores, in Rotation Three, and they had been sitting in a sealed magazine behind Ore Head ever since. Kastellan granted the permission, against the advice of much of his staff. The Fiftieth's guns were soon soaking the Archflamen's lines on the edge of the Grey in the same grey vapour.
The Fiftieth's soldiers saw the Archflamen's thralls on the edge of the Grey stand up empty out of their trenches and wander. They did not suspect that they were doing the Lethe's work for it. Every shell they fired put more of the river into the ground.

THE SECOND CYCLE. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 241

The second cycle began with more strikes from the air, but this time the drowned answered. The Wrath interceptors and the Stymphalian swarms twisted and dived together high over the Flats, under the ceiling, while the armies below braced for another cycle. On Leaf 57-48, two waves of Wrath bombers came in low and fast over the inner belt and unloaded straight into the trenches. With that much support from the air, Colonel Lowe's Fifty-Second gained more ground in a cycle than it had in a rotation, and Kastellan noted that he would ask for more bombers.
As a seep-storm rolled over the Flats, Varne's Behemoths went forward again. The Anvil of Talos destroyed eleven Arsenal tanks it had seen moving up as reinforcements, in a single minute, with its missile racks and its rotary cannon and its lance, and burned the column to a ruin. As its gunnery crew reported the column gone, its augurs picked up the drowned Behemoths coming.
After the losses of the first cycle, the Archflamen's allies had answered the Forge Cohort with engines of their own. The drowned Behemoths had come across from the Mora Cut and up from the Demus Rim to meet their old cohort, twelve of them. Varne gave new orders. The drowned Behemoths were the first targets. All firing solutions to be changed. While the battle on the ground went on around their feet, the Behemoths of Talos and the drowned Behemoths of the river fought their own war.
Lances and Phlegethon-cannon fired. Aegis-wards flared and burned under them. The missile racks emptied. The rotary cannon ran low. The reactors strained under the demand, and the Hephaestean forge-acolytes in the engine decks worked without stopping to send more power to the wards and the guns, as the Behemoths groaned under the strain. The two sides were evenly matched, and they tore at each other, rocking under the hits, plating scorched and split.
The first to fall was the Brontes-pattern Steadfast. It closed on the drowned engines and was raked by the fire of a drowned hammer-gun, a weapon of the old Forge pattern that the cradle-crews called the Smith's Laughter for the sound of it, and its last ward failed. Before its forge-acolytes could raise the ward again, a Phlegethon-cannon put a jet of river-fire through the command deck. It melted through the plating and the blast screens and killed the whole cradle-crew in seconds. Their screams went into the Behemoth's own mind through the cradle links as they died. With its higher mind burned out, the Steadfast stopped where it stood, with its guns still raised and silent. More fire hit it as it stood. Its last forge-acolyte could not leave it. He knelt on the engine deck and prayed, as it shuddered and groaned in its own death. Hit and hit again, the Steadfast stayed on its feet, a burned and silent sentinel over the Flats. It stayed there for the rest of the war, the tallest thing on the Cinder Flats outside the Acropolis. At the Forge of Hephaestus, a bell was rung for it, and would be rung again for each of its brothers that fell at the Arsenal.
The fight of the giants went on all cycle, over the heads of the soldiers fighting back and forth along the trenches below them. The advance slowed. Without the Behemoths' fire, there was hard fighting everywhere. At the end of the second cycle, when the Behemoths went back to their laagers, little more ground had been taken. The third cycle would see the fight resume, and the fourth.

THE BREACH. ROTATION TEN, CYCLES 244 TO 246

Both sides had lost heavily by the fifth cycle. The Third's soldiers, worn out, gathered up rifles and ammunition and got ready for another cycle in the grinder. Of all the regiments in the offensive, the Twenty-Second had gone furthest. It had driven a salient into the inner belt on Leaf 54-45, and that was where Varne would put his weight. A narrow crack might be forced open there. If it could not, the Behemoths would draw the drowned engines onto them and let another regiment break through somewhere else.
The Behemoths had been rearmed and patched in the night by the Hephaestean crews that went with the Forge Cohort. Varne led what was left of it back into the fire. It walked among the tanks, each Thureos with a knot of grey infantry behind it, and walked through the Archflamen's first barrages unhurt. After five cycles of fighting, the Archflamen's line was thin. It had lost many soldiers and tanks on the first cycle, and it was feeling the losses now. The drowned Behemoths had lost engines too, and only their presence had held the line. Now, outnumbered, and scarred from three cycles of fighting, the drowned Behemoths fell back to regroup.
The Archflamen's cohorts could not hold alone against Varne's fire. They brought down one of the Steropes with a battery of divine mortars, shell after shell until the Behemoth fell full length into the paste, and that was their only success. Some of the thralls fought like zealots. Others broke under what the Third's soldiers called Behemoth-shock, the sight and sound of the engines walking toward them, and ran. The Twenty-Second went forward, and the tanks and outriders of the Fourth Assault Phalanx joined the pursuit. One more cycle of fighting saw the inner belt breached and fresh tank columns going through the gap. Kastellan's offensive had broken the inner belt and was driving on toward the Cinder Wall. The Behemoths had won him the battle.
This time, though, the Archflamen's army did not leave its works when the belt broke, as it had at the outer and second belts. The ground between the inner belt and the Cinder Wall was not bare any more. Over the rotations of the siege it had been covered in trenches and pillboxes and gun pits, without the deep shelters and careful siting of the inner belt itself, but with plenty of places from which to stand and fight for every metre. The Archflamen's commanders knew they could not afford to fall back to the Wall and dig in again. Close to the spur, every metre had to be fought for. Fall back much further and there would be nothing to fall back to. So there was no clean breakthrough this time and no quick advance. There was a slow crawl forward against attacks and counter-attacks every cycle. On Leaf 54-45 the Archflamen's strength was spent, and the Twenty-Second made more large gains before it too had to stop, before it outran its supplies and its guns.
The Behemoths were pulled back into reserve to be repaired and wait for their next use. They had made the difference. They had won their battle. They had lost four Brontes and seven Steropes, which would have to be replaced from the Forge. The Third's staff counted six drowned Behemoths destroyed, a good total, and Varne's crews could go home to the Forge with honour.
The battle to reach the Cinder Wall was not over. There were many more battles still to fight, and Kastellan was already preparing the next phase of his offensive. But a long step had been taken toward victory. The outcome of the siege was no longer in doubt. The Archflamen had lost heavily, and the Third's replacements would keep coming down the cables. His could not. Kastellan knew that a siege of attrition always favoured the besieger, once the besieger was fed.
For the first time in four rotations, the Third could say that it was winning the reduction of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal.
On the night the inner belt broke, Kastellan sat at his terminal on Quarterdeck Nineteen and read the Répertoire's figures for the offensive. They were good. The transfers were lower than any offensive of the siege, and the nulls were lower too, all but on the Fiftieth's front on the edge of the Grey. There the nulls ran at forty percent. And on the Répertoire's map of the Flats, the pale wash of the Grey had spread another eleven kilometres north since the Fiftieth started firing.
He asked his staff to bring him everything the Third had on Grey Seventh. They brought him the Arsenal's old inventory, which said the distillate was proscribed, and nothing else. Nobody in the Third had a chemist who knew what it was.
