THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART FIVE. THE BIG PUSH, AND WHAT CAME UP THE RIVER

"Give it everything. It keeps what you give it."
Colonel Damaris Lowe, Fifty-Second Trench Regiment, Eighth Line Phalanx

THE PLAN. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 1

The Third had been on the Flats for eight rotations of front time. By the Répertoire's plan it should have been dug in before the inner belt four rotations ago, with the Acropolis under its heaviest guns. It was still in front of the second belt. It had had to ask for more soldiers, and for help with the Echo Mast, and the Command had given it a Line Phalanx and sent a pure-blood cohort it had not asked for, and the cohort had gone home without speaking to it.
The Cohort of Nemesis had done one thing for the Third, all the same. The second belt was thinner than it had ever been, and Castorre saw his chance.
He had four rotations of the Command's patience left, by Corvis's reckoning, and perhaps fewer. The Eighth Line Phalanx was fresh. The second belt was stripped. He ordered the Eighth's three regiments and every regiment already in the line to plan a general offensive on the second belt along its whole front, with constant pressure kept up until the Archflamen's reserves had been drawn in and destroyed, by the guns or by the infantry. When the belt broke somewhere, the Fourth Assault Phalanx would be ready to go through the gap and drive for the inner belt. If it went fast enough, it might get a foothold in the inner belt before the Archflamen could man it.
The Third's soldiers called it the Big Push before the orders had reached the regiments. The Répertoire called it Operation Antilochus, after a son of Nestor who had died saving his father, and nobody on the Flats ever used the name.
The Archflamen's army saw it coming. It could not have failed to. Its patrols and its sentries on the second belt watched the Third's trenches fill, and the stockpiles grow in the support lines, and the new gun pits being dug behind the old ones. The Arsenal's gunners shelled the Third's lines every watch to break up the preparations. The Third's gunners shelled back, at the Arsenal's guns. Every gun knocked out now was one that would not be firing when the infantry went over.
On the eighty-ninth cycle of Rotation Eight, the preparatory barrage began.
Every gun in the Third fired. The Flats lit up white and gold from the Mora Cut to the Lethe Cut, and the second belt disappeared under smoke, and the barrage went on through every watch for eleven cycles. In the shelters under the second belt, the Archflamen's thralls waited with the ground shaking around them. In the dark watch of the hundredth cycle, the Third's communication trenches began to fill with soldiers, the first wave going forward into the fire bays, and the second wave packed in behind them in the support line, shoulder to shoulder, loaded with grenades and ammunition and three cycles of rations. At the first bell of the hundred and first cycle, the barrage stopped.

DAY ONE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 101

The order went out on every relay set in the forward trenches. The Third went up the ladders.
It went over the parapet on a hundred and nine kilometres of front, in a single wave, four hundred thousand soldiers across the ash. On the second belt, the Archflamen's thralls were already coming up out of their shelters to their guns. They opened fire before the Third's soldiers were in sight, into the smoke, low, along the ground. The Third's soldiers began to fall. The rest went on. The Arsenal's batteries that the barrage had missed opened up behind the machine guns, and shells began to come down among the waves.
On Leaf 50-27, in front of Ceiling-Lance Seven, the Twenty-Ninth Trench Regiment's attack fell apart.
The Twenty-Ninth had gone over on that leaf before. Its soldiers had looked at the thirty-one breachers on the Arsenal's wire through their periscopes every morning for two rotations. They went over on the first bell and walked into a barrage that the Arsenal's gunners had been ranging on Leaf 50-27 since the raid, and it broke them in the first hour. The first maniples went to ground. The next maniples went to ground among them. Then the soldiers in the craters began to come back.
The Third's provost-optios were in the waves to stop that. There was one with every cohort, a Command officer with a pistol and the authority of the Répertoire, and on Leaf 50-27 the provost-optios tried to drive the Twenty-Ninth back toward the wire. Three of them shot soldiers who were coming back. In two cohorts of the Twenty-Ninth, the soldiers who were coming back shot their provost-optios.
At the parapet, the first wave coming back met the second wave going forward. The second wave's centurions tried to force their maniples through, and there was fighting in the fire bays between the two, with fists and rifle butts and entrenching tools, while the Arsenal's barrage came down on both. Within two hours the Twenty-Ninth's attack had turned into a rout.
The Command's verdict on the Twenty-Ninth came three cycles later. Its colonel and his staff were arrested by the provost-optios and sent up the Stair to Watchpoint Hades under guard, where they were tried by the Command and sentenced under CODE 31. The Répertoire carried out the sentence in the vats. Seven officers were transferred by order and arrived null by order. The Twenty-Ninth Trench Regiment was disbanded and its colours were sent up to the depot at Kappa to be burned. Its survivors were sent to the Forlorn Cohorts on the Cocytus front, where the Empire sent its condemned. None of the soldiers who had shot the provost-optios were ever identified.
Elsewhere on the front there was more to show for the day. In some places the Third's soldiers reached the second belt and went over it with their bayonets. Some regimental commanders reported good progress. Others reported failure, and losses that their staffs had not finished counting. In spite of everything, the second wave was ordered forward along the whole front, and went. By the end of the cycle it was clear that most of the Archflamen's cohorts had held. Where they had been driven out, they had gone back inside the second belt to positions behind the first ones, and manned those.
In the south, the Ninth Line Phalanx had done best. On the Thirty-Ninth Trench Regiment's front, on Leaves 51 and 52, the Thirty-Ninth's gunners had won the counter-battery duel outright. They had knocked out nearly every Arsenal gun that could reach their front, and when the Thirty-Ninth's infantry went over, almost nothing fired on them from behind the wire. The Thirty-Ninth went through the forward trench of the second belt and kept going. It was still advancing in the dark watch.
The Third's staff on Quarterdeck Nineteen seized on it. The Thirty-Ninth's front would be the point of greatest effort for the rest of the push. The Fourth Assault Phalanx had been held in reserve all day, and now it was ordered to the Thirty-Ninth's front, to attack through the Thirty-Ninth with its Hoplons and its tanks. It would take a cycle to bring the Fourth into position. The Thirty-Ninth would have to hold what it had taken through the second cycle, and could expect the Fourth on the third.
The first cycle had cost more than anyone had planned. Many cohorts were already short of soldiers, and the vats at Watchpoint Hades were full, and the first transfers of the Big Push would not be back on the line for a week. The cohorts that had got into the second belt had to hold there until the reinforcements came. The second cycle would be for holding, and for the counter-attacks that would come, and the third for the next great effort. The gains so far were small. The Archflamen's army had fought hard to hold its ground, in places like zealots. The attack had to go on.

DAY TWO. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 102

The second cycle was a cycle of holding and of scattered attacks. Each cohort that had got into the second belt tried to make its own position safe, or tried again for the objective it had failed to take on the first cycle. The artillery duel raged overhead. Underneath it, in the craters and in pieces of captured trench, the infantry fought a hundred separate battles along the front.
By the end of the cycle, several of the Third's colonels were asking for the push to be called off, or cut down. At the rate they were losing soldiers, their regiments would not exist by the end of the week, and the vats could not refill them fast enough to matter.
The Third's command would not consider it. Lord-General Castorre could not. The push was too important to be given up for the cost, however high the cost. Speed mattered more than anything. The longer the Third waited to follow up, the longer the Archflamen had to dig in again. On the third cycle there would be more attacks. The Third was bleeding badly. It assumed the Archflamen was too.

DAY THREE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 103

On the third cycle the fighting went on along the whole front, as ordered. The weight of the Third's effort went into the south, on Leaves 51-41 and 52-41, where the Fourth Assault Phalanx went in through the Thirty-Ninth.
The Fourth's tanks went forward slowly across the craters of no-man's land and spread out into line, and began to move. Behind them came the Hoplons, fifty soldiers to a carrier, ready to drop their ramps and go for the Arsenal's trenches.
The Archflamen's positions in front of them were a loose line of smashed bunkers and walls and shell-holes, and they could not stand the weight of the Fourth for long. The Hoplons came down on them with their ramps and put their breachers into them, and by the middle of the cycle the Fourth's tanks and infantry were moving forward again behind them. The breakthrough at the second belt looked close.
The Archflamen's commanders were not blind to it. They had seen the danger in the Thirty-Ninth's gains on the first cycle, and they had used the second cycle to reinforce the sector. They counter-attacked on the third. Out of the second belt came Thureos tanks and Hoplons of the Arsenal's own, new divine mortars on crawlers from the vaults, Kyklops siege carriages, and a squadron of twelve Lance-Hunters, the long-barrelled tank destroyers the Arsenal built for the deep holds. As the Fourth's leading tanks rolled forward, the resistance in front of them grew. The counter-attack did what it was meant to. By the middle of the afternoon watch the Fourth had stopped, and the Arsenal's armour was pushing it back.
The Fourth's tank losses rose through the watch. Its commanders called for a defensive barrage to cover them while they pulled back. The breakthrough had been stopped.
There was better news elsewhere. At the far eastern end of the attack, the Twenty-Sixth Trench Regiment of the Fifth Line Phalanx had taken its objectives for the first cycle on Leaf 62-38, two cycles late, and was getting ready to push again on the fourth. The Fifth brought up the tank companies of the Fourteenth Armoured to help it.

DAY FOUR, AND AFTER. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 104 TO 124

The Third could not give up the gains of the first three cycles, however small they were. It looked as though the Third meant to beat itself to death against the second belt. Its whole strength was committed. Its only reserves were the cohorts that had been put together from the survivors and stragglers of the first cycle, and the soldiers coming back down from the vats a few thousand at a time. On the new front, inside and outside the second belt, the battle went on without a pause.
As the cohort commanders fought for their local objectives, the momentum of the push ran down. The shell stockpiles were falling, worst of all the Phaethon shells. Cohorts were the size of maniples. Maniples were the size of sections.
The push had gone on for seven cycles without a breakthrough when the Fifty-Second Trench Regiment of the Eighth Line Phalanx made its attack on Leaf 57-40.
The Fifty-Second was a new regiment in its first battle, and its colonel was a new colonel. Damaris Lowe was thirty-four, a legacy of Athena on her mother's side and of nobody in particular on her father's, from a ceiling world called Ostia Minor that had sent the Third soldiers for four hundred years. She had come down the Stair three months before with three thousand of her regiment's soldiers who had never been to Depth 40, and she had spent those months in the forward bays on Leaf 57-40 looking at the second belt.
In the confusion of the push, she got her cohort commanders together and gave them a plan. She would make a night attack on the trench line in front of her: one attack, at one time, by the whole regiment together, in place of the separate pushes that the Third had been making for a week, which had won almost no ground for a great many transfers.
The Eighth Line Phalanx refused it at first. It thought the risk too great. Moving a regiment across no-man's land at night was hard enough to coordinate. If the Arsenal found it in the open, it would be stranded in front of the second belt with nowhere to go. Lowe answered that if the guns were strong enough, the Arsenal's guns could be kept quiet even if the attack failed, and that a regiment brought back across no-man's land under its own guns' cover would not cost much. The Phalanx gave in. Lowe was given permission to try.

LEAF 57-40. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 106. THE DARK WATCH

They went out in the dark on their bellies, eleven cohorts, with Lowe at the front of the first.
The Fifty-Second crawled. It had been taught to walk, in parade order, as the Third had taught every regiment for six hundred years, and its colonel had made it crawl instead, through two months of nights in the craters in front of its own wire. It crawled through the paste and the dead in silence. It did not stop for the flares. Lowe had told it not to. A regiment that froze for every flare took twice as long to cross, and the Arsenal's sentries were watching for things that froze.
As the Fifty-Second crawled, every gun the regiment had, and every battery Lowe had been able to borrow from the Phalanx, opened up together on the second belt behind the trench line in front of her. At the planned minute, the fire dropped onto the trench line. Then it lifted and rolled forward.
The Fifty-Second stood up out of the craters and ran.
The Arsenal's thralls in the trench line had been in their shelters through the barrage, as they always were. When it rolled forward they came up their shafts to their guns, as they always did, and found the Fifty-Second already on the parapet. Most of them never reached their guns. The Fifty-Second went over the trench line and down into it and along it, and the thrall cohorts in the shelters underneath it came up their shafts one at a time into the Fifty-Second's bayonets.
Lowe went across the first trench at a jump, waving her soldiers on behind her, toward the support trench. She landed on the far side and turned to see them come, and a Laistrygon came up out of the support trench in front of her.
It was three and a half metres tall. It had been on stimulants since the barrage began, and it had a forge-hammer in its hands that weighed more than she did. It hit her once.
Her implant fired. The Fifty-Second's relay logged the upload going up from Leaf 57-40 under her code, and the Répertoire logged it arriving at Watchpoint Hades ninety seconds later, whole. She woke in the vats eight cycles after that.
Her regiment did not know any of this until later. It went on without her. At the first bell, the second wave went through the first wave and into the support trench and beyond it. The second belt had been broken.

THE FOLDING OF THE SECOND BELT. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 107 TO 160

The news of the Fifty-Second reached the Third's staff on Quarterdeck Nineteen within the watch, and the reinforcements to widen the breach were moving within two.
The first to arrive was a battery of quad-launchers, towed forward across no-man's land by tractors, which came into action on the far side of the second belt's forward trench and fired salvo after salvo into the Arsenal's last attempts to counter-attack. More followed. Tanks came, and Hoplons, and heavier guns. The Third's outrider squadrons came through at the gallop on their armoured hounds and wheeled east and west along the inside of the second belt to take its cohorts from behind.
As with the outer belt six rotations before, the second belt folded in the cycles that followed. The dam had broken, and the Third went through it. The Archflamen's commanders left rearguards to die in the nests, and pulled the rest of their army back. One by one the Third's regiments went forward into the second belt, in some places against nothing. Any of the Archflamen's cohorts that did not get away before the ring closed were surrounded and destroyed.
The Third was too badly hurt to rush on into the inner belt. It needed time to rebuild, and to plan what came next. The cost of the breakthrough had been too high. The Big Push had cost two million, nine hundred thousand transfers in sixty cycles, and a hundred and eighty thousand of them were null. It had taken eight rotations of front time to reach the inner belt, rotations of slaughter as bad as anything the theatre had seen since the Second Grand Offensive. By the Répertoire's count, the Third had transferred more than twenty-three million soldiers on the Cinder Flats. Six hundred and eighty thousand of them had not come back.
But for the first time, it seemed that the end was in reach. The Archflamen was bottled up behind the inner belt and the Cinder Wall, and the front had shortened again, to sixty-one kilometres. The Third could put more guns and more soldiers on every kilometre of it than it ever had. On the hundred and sixty-second cycle of Rotation Eight, a Phaethon heavy mortar of the Tenth Ordnance Phalanx fired a shell from a new pit on the old second belt, and forty seconds later the shell landed on the cinder cone, on the lower terraces of the Acropolis of the Arsenal. For the first time in the war, the Third's guns could reach the Archflamen.
Colonel Damaris Lowe came back down the Stair on the hundred and twentieth cycle, and was given back her regiment. She asked the Eighth Line Phalanx what had happened to the Laistrygon. Nobody knew. She went out to Leaf 57-40 and looked for it among the dead in the support trench. She did not find it.

THE ACROPOLIS. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 170

Standing on the edge of defeat, the Archflamen did not seem to care.
He told his commanders that the end he had preached was here, and that the slaughter must go on. He did not talk about victory any more, or defeat. He talked about the dark, and about going down into it, and about the Imperator coming back up through it, and he took the Katabasion's procession out every cycle onto the terraces of the Acropolis under the Phaethon fire and walked it around the cone with the bones of the First Descent carried in front of him, and some cycles a Phaethon shell landed in the procession and some cycles it did not.
By now Hesper had become the Archflamen's closest adviser, and Dace's. She came to the Archflamen in the Katabasion after the second belt fell and told him that it was not over. The Empire's army was closing the ring, she said. But there were powers in the river greater than any army with guns. The river would stand with him if he held firm. There were allies in the deep, ready to come up and fight for him, if he knew how to call them. She knew how.
When the Archflamen asked what it would cost, Hesper told him that the Kindlers would name the price, since it was theirs.
She brought the Kindlers up out of the docks' ruins on the eightieth cycle, by the cliff stair the Arsenal's smugglers had used. There were nine of them. They were Cinderborn priests of the Red Furnace, the Phlegethon's own congregation, which had been down at the bend for as long as the Arsenal had been on the spur, and their skin had burned and healed and burned again until it had the texture of slag. They could call a reformation.
Every soldier of the Third knew what a reformation was. The monsters that came up out of Tartarus did not stay dead. When the Third killed one, it went back into the deep, and the deep made it again, in a rotation or a century, and sent it back up. A reformation peak was when the deep did that faster than usual, and sent more of them at once, and the Third had been built to meet them. Nobody in the Third had known that the peaks could be called.
The Kindlers went into the heat-wells under the Acropolis with Dace. The Archflamen did not go with them. What they did there, the Répertoire learned later from the Arsenal's own records, and from the Vestals of the House of the Hearth, six of whom were found alive at the end. It took nine cycles. It took four thousand thralls, and the forty Laistrygons of the Arsenal's last breeding pen, and all of the Hecatean strays except the girl Mirren, whom Dace kept. They went into the Number Four heat-well one at a time, as the Kindlers sang.
The Third's sentries on the old second belt saw the first sign on the ninetieth cycle. The Phlegethon was rising. The river below the shelf, which had burned red at the edge of the Flats for six hundred years, began to burn orange, and then yellow, and the heat came up over the edge of the shelf and across the Flats in waves. Red cloud gathered under the ceiling over the spur. The Flats had no weather. They had seep, and the seep-storms. They had never had cloud. The cloud grew every cycle, black and red, and lit from underneath by the river, and it turned slowly over the Acropolis like water going down a drain.
The Répertoire read the heat curves from the Phlegethon, and the soundings from the river's bed, and issued a reformation warning for the bend at Depth 40, and the warning went to every hold in the theatre. It assessed the peak as the largest in the Third's six hundred years on the Flats. It assessed that the Third could not meet it with what was left of it after the Big Push.
The Command sent the Third a reserve.

SEGMENT TWELVE. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLE 201

The reserve went to Segment Twelve.
Segment Twelve was the last segment on the Third's arc, out on the extreme south-western end of the line, where the Third's trenches ran along the rim of the Demus Gorge and stopped. It was a fringe position. It had been a fringe position for two hundred years, held by a single battalion of the Ninth Line Phalanx under a Major, and the Major's soldiers called it Segment Twelve because the ration post said Segment Twelve, and because the augur overlay stamped the same identifier across their visors every time the alarms began.
It was where the reformation came up first.
The Répertoire had predicted the bend. The Kindlers had not called the peak at the bend. They had called it under the Demus Gorge, nine kilometres down, and on the two hundredth cycle of Rotation Eight it came up the Gorge's walls in the dark watch and went over the rim into Segment Twelve.
Segment Twelve held. It had been dug millennia before by hands that knew their work, and its walls held, and its soldiers held behind them. They held against the first wave and the second, with their rifles and their keyed mortars and a legacy of Poseidon who flooded the kill corridor with brine until his eyes bled. They held when the breach opened in front of them, a hole in the place that should not have been a hole, with forms behind it that marched almost like soldiers. They held when something tall and armoured came out of the breach and raised its hand, and the ground in front of their parapet folded upward and turned their range stakes to dust. Their centurion triggered the emergency beacon.
The reserve answered it.
Two ships of the Fleet were holding station over Segment Twelve in the low geometry, inside the reach of the ceiling-lances and in no position to be there. They had come down through the ceiling with the reserve, against every rule the Fleet had for Depth 40. The reformation warning had said the reserve was needed now. They were the Axis Dawn and the Warbringer Fury. They carried four million soldiers of the reserve in their holds, an army raised for the peak, with its armour and its guns and its Behemoths, and supplies for a theatre. The Axis Dawn's augur put her at twenty-three percent operational after the passage through the ceiling. The Warbringer Fury was at forty-one.
They fired. The heavy batteries of both ships came down on the breach in front of Segment Twelve, and the breach and what was in it were taken out of the world, and Segment Twelve's trench shook, and held.
With the ships came their commander. The Command had sent officers and command authority with the reserve, to take the peak in hand, and had sent them under the most senior field commander it could spare: a Senior Commander of the Waves of Poseidon, ranked nine thousand and fifty-first on the Unified Leaderboard, callsign Dulaberre-Six. Marien Pyrrhus, the daughter of Stephanos Pyrrhus. She came down to Segment Twelve with the first pallets to see the ground for herself.
She was on the landing shelf behind the segment with the Major when the shelf went.
It did not collapse. It went out of existence, from the edge inward, crates and soldiers and the shelf itself, into an absence under it that ate the light and gave nothing back. Marien Pyrrhus threw a soldier clear and turned for another, and the ground under her foot was not there, and she fell into it. Her bodyguard fired an anchor line after her. It did not catch on anything. He went in after it.
The Major called it in. Sector Command answered that the fleet elements would commit. If Dulaberre-Six went in, the ships could not leave her. The Major put his soldiers into the ships' holds, carrying their wounded, while the absence ate the rest of the shelf. The Axis Dawn and the Warbringer Fury went in after Marien Pyrrhus.
The Répertoire's log for Segment Twelve, Rotation Eight, cycle two hundred and one, has four lines at the end.

DULABERRE-SIX. NO SIGNAL.
UOE AXIS DAWN. NO SIGNAL.
UOE WARBRINGER FURY. NO SIGNAL.
RESERVE: NOT AVAILABLE.

The Third did not learn for a long time where they had gone. The Répertoire classed the event as a subreality shear and the loss as total. It took the reserve off the Third's books, and with it the officers the Third had been promised, and the four million, and the guns. The reformation peak came up the Demus Gorge that night onto a front that had nothing behind it.
And while the Third was looking at the Gorge, the river at the bend began to give up its dead.

WHAT CAME UP THE RIVER. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 201 TO 230

The Echo Mast had been calling down the rivers for two rotations. Most of what heard it had come up already, in ones and twos. The Kindlers' peak brought the rest.
It brought the drowned fleet.
The Phlegethon had been eating ships for as long as the Empire had flown them in Tartarus. A ship that went down over the bend went into the river, and the river did not let it burn out. It kept it, hot and whole and dark, on its bed, with its dead crews still at their stations. There were hundreds of them under the bend at Depth 40. The Frost Masons of the Cocytus had been working on some of them for two rotations, since the Echo Mast first called, coming up the river under the ice they made around themselves. They had sealed the breaches with Cocytus ice that did not melt in the Phlegethon. The Kindlers' peak lit their reactors with river fire. On the two hundred and fourth cycle of Rotation Eight, they began to rise.
The first of them came up out of the river at the bend in the dark watch, steaming, and lifted on its old engines into the low geometry over the Flats, inside the envelope of the ceiling-lances. The lances did not fire on it. The Arsenal's gunners had been told what was coming.
The Third's spotters on the old second belt watched it go up past them. It was twelve kilometres long, a slab of bronze-green divine steel with a prow carved into the figure of a god a kilometre high. The god was Apollo. Half of his face had been burned away, and the rest was black with river-fire, and the ice of the Cocytus was packed into the hull where it had split open along a third of its length, glowing blue through the black.
The Répertoire did not need long to put a name to it. It had logged the same hull four rotations before.
It was AC-09-1642, the Eye of Light: an AnnChase-class battleship of the 9th Olympian Reach, Apollo's ship, which the Order of the Hollow Crescent had captured and the Empire had taken back at Luna. In Rotation Four, the Alpha-Class entity called Minerva had taken it from the Forge of Hephaestus and crashed it into the Tartarus front at Grid Delta, Sector Nineteen-F, two hundred kilometres up the river from the Flats. It had been a waypoint. The Répertoire had issued CODE 78E on it, Lord Commander Varric von Romhardt had taken every mortal soldier in his sector out into the assault behind it, and the Wedge had been driven in. The Third had lost the Thirty-Third Trench Regiment to that Wedge. Its soldiers had watched the Eye of Light come down, burning, for twenty-seven seconds.
After the Wedge was driven in, the Eye of Light's grave had sat at Grid Delta glowing on the Command's maps, half-buried in the ash at the edge of the river. At the end of Rotation Five the river had risen at Depth 38, as it did every few rotations, and when it went down again the Eye of Light was gone. The Wedge's engineers logged it as lost in the Phlegethon. The Phlegethon had carried it down the river for two rotations, along the bed, to the bend at Depth 40, and the Frost Masons had found it there.
The Forsworn were aboard. Hesper Calloway had gone down to it on the first cycle the Frost Masons had it sealed, and had taken it for her own, and her Forsworn crewed it now from its old stations, alongside its drowned crew.
Behind it came the rest.

THE BATTLE OF THE LOW GEOMETRY. ROTATION EIGHT, CYCLES 204 TO 211

The Tartarus Fleet's squadron over the Flats had been there for nine rotations.
It had come down with the Third's first convoy under Navarch Iole Saturnin in the heavy cruiser Pallas Ascendant, and it had stayed, guarding the convoys from the ceiling cracks down to Landing Shelf Kappa, and holding station north of the lance envelope to keep the ceiling clear of things that came up the river with wings. The Pallas Ascendant was the squadron's teeth. She was one of four hundred of her class in the Fleet, and she could outfight anything the deep had sent up through the ceiling at Depth 40 in six hundred years. The Third's soldiers called her the Old Owl, and looked up at her running lights every night from the trenches.
Nobody had expected anything to come up out of the river that the Pallas Ascendant could not handle, and over nine rotations the Fleet had taken most of the squadron away for other work. By Rotation Eight, Saturnin had the Pallas Ascendant, three Lantern-pattern frigates that were overdue for refit, three ceiling monitors that were fit to fight and short of crew, two armed haulers and a troop lighter. She had three fire barges, old ore-haulers packed with keyed charges, for use against things too big to shoot. She had written to the Fleet every rotation that the Flats' ceiling was secure.
She sent the frigates out north and east as pickets when the Eye of Light came up, with orders to find what else was coming and not to fight it, and to come back to the line at full speed when they had.
The frigate Iris Four came back first. She had found three drowned raiders coming up out of the river below the bend, small fast hulls of a pattern the Fleet had not built for two thousand years, which had come up close enough to read the squadron and then gone back down into the river cloud without firing. Saturnin knew what it was. It was a probe. The deep knew her strength now, or her lack of it, and it would come in enough strength to overwhelm her.
It came on the two hundred and tenth cycle. The pickets counted fourteen hulls coming up out of the river at the bend. They came up the ceiling geometry in two lines, toward the squadron.
Saturnin met them with everything she had. The Fleet sent a cruiser group behind them from the ceiling cracks at Depth 36, under Navarch Cassia Thorne in the cruiser Themis Unbowed, with the cruiser Hyperion and the old grand cruiser Patience of Hera, and a squadron of escorts. It arrived a few hours behind the drowned fleet. It could not stop several of the drowned ships from getting through into the geometry over the Flats.
The drowned fleet split. Five hulls, led by the Eye of Light, swung around the end of Saturnin's line on the north. Nine more, with a drowned heavy cruiser in the lead that the Répertoire could not name and the Third later called the Cinder Dawn, passed down the south side. The two lines went past each other at close range in a gun battle that wrecked both.
The Eye of Light raked the length of Saturnin's line as she went by. Her batteries were Apollo's batteries, built to fight the Princes, and the river had not hurt them. They stripped the squadron's shields and buckled its armour. The frigate Iris One was crippled and left drifting. Two of the monitors were destroyed. The troop lighter Long Labour lost three reactors to attack craft from the Cinder Dawn and was running on minimal power with her thrusters gone, a sitting target. Saturnin had to leave her. The Long Labour's captain told his crew to man the guns and keep firing, and they did.
The Pallas Ascendant had been hit and hit again. She was listing in the geometry, but she was still fighting. Her guns had hurt a drowned transport hulk and destroyed one of the raiders. The drowned fleet was hurt too. And, for reasons nobody in the squadron understood, two of the drowned cruisers in the southern line had not fired at all. As Saturnin watched from the bridge, they turned and began to pull away.
Their guns could have finished the Pallas Ascendant and every ship left with her. Saturnin did not know why they had not. She did not know, either, that Thorne's cruisers were coming. Most of her relays had been shot away.
Even with the cruisers coming, the Eye of Light and the drowned transport went on down toward the Flats, to put their cargo on the ash. The Cinder Dawn turned to launch her attack craft again. This time she launched boarding boats, which went for the crippled Long Labour to take her.
Saturnin sent in her fire barges.
The crew of one barge was ordered to steer it onto its target and abandon it. They did not abandon it. They steered it into the stern of the drowned transport, and its keyed charges went off. The transport was already deep in the low geometry, and the blast sent it down. Some of its drowned crew got the descent under control, somehow. It came down on the Shale Plateau, beyond the head of the Demus Gorge and a hundred and forty kilometres behind the Third's line in the south-west, and tore a furrow three kilometres long across the plateau. It lay there and did not move. Its holds stayed shut.
While the transport fell, the Eye of Light went down into the lowest geometry over the Flats, inside the lance envelope, where no ship of the Empire could follow her, and opened her holds.
The Forsworn had gathered their allies in the river for two rotations, and they came down onto the Cinder Flats from the Eye of Light in their thousands, in drowned landing craft and in boarding boats and in the Eye of Light's own Lanterns. Behind them, from the ship's lower holds, she lowered the drowned Behemoths onto the Flats on her cargo cables. They were old walking engines of the Forge of Hephaestus, lost in the river over the millennia. Four of them stood up on the ash in front of the Mora Cut and began to walk.
Navarch Thorne knew she was too late to stop it. She came on anyway, in the hope of catching the Eye of Light on her way back up and destroying her. In the fight that followed, her escorts were cut to pieces and the Pallas Ascendant's engines were wrecked, and the battle turned against the Fleet for good. The drowned fleet had four capital ships still fighting, the Eye of Light and the Cinder Dawn among them. The Fleet had lost the low geometry over the Flats.
Saturnin knew what it meant for her. She told the captain of the Patience of Hera, in the last relay she could raise, to report her own view to the Fleet's board of inquiry, which was that she had failed in her duty. She did not expect to keep her command.
Thorne did not mean to go home beaten. She had one chance left, which was the Eye of Light. She ordered the Themis Unbowed to ramming speed.
The ram of the Themis Unbowed is still taught at the Fleet's school at Callistus. It was brave and it did not work. The Themis Unbowed struck the Eye of Light a third of the way along her length, below the burned face of the god, and broke herself on the Eye of Light's hull. The Eye of Light's Cocytus ice took the blow. Thorne and her crew of eleven thousand were transferred, or not, in the next four seconds. Ninety percent of them came back. Thorne did not.
The fight had turned the low geometry over the Flats into a field of wrecks. Eleven of the Fleet's ships were lost or abandoned: the Themis Unbowed, four frigates and escorts, two monitors, the Long Labour, and the three fire barges. Every other ship in the battle was damaged. The Pallas Ascendant survived, just, and would need two rotations in the yards. The Hyperion was badly hurt. Only the Patience of Hera came out with light damage and fit to fight.
Of the drowned fleet, the Fleet could confirm the transport on the Shale Plateau and four raiders. The rest had gone back down into the river cloud, or into the low geometry over the spur where nothing could reach them.
The drowned held the low geometry over the Arsenal now. They could land what they liked.
The Cinder Dawn landed hers the next cycle. She brought the Cinderborn of the Red Furnace, the Phlegethon's fighting congregation, ten thousand of them, under their war-priest Draugos the Kindled, who had burned and healed and burned again for four hundred years and whose followers said that he could not die in fire. From the river cloud, the drowned ship the Third came to call the Rimebound lowered the Frost Masons of the Cocytus onto the spur, a warband of engineers in armour white with ice, whose craft was the siege, and who would build the Archflamen defences the Third had never seen.
In all, by the Répertoire's estimate, more than sixty thousand new enemies came down onto the Flats in the eleven cycles after the battle of the low geometry, from the Eye of Light and the Cinder Dawn and the Rimebound and the river. None of them carried implants. All of them had come to kill the Empire's soldiers in a way that would last.
The Third had lost its reserve at Segment Twelve and its fleet over the Flats in ten cycles. It stood before the inner belt of the Pyriphlegethon Arsenal with sixty-one kilometres of front, a reformation peak coming up the Demus Gorge on its flank, four drowned Behemoths walking toward it from the Mora Cut, and nothing coming down the Stair behind it.
