THE SIEGE OF THE PYRIPHLEGETHON ARSENAL

PART EIGHT. THE WAR BENEATH, AND THE WAR ABOVE

"Under the ground there are no relays. Dig as if you meant to stay."
Saying of the Hephaestean sapper regiments

THE PLAN UNDER THE GROUND. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 250

The inner belt had been broken four rotations after the Répertoire's first plan said it would be, at a cost the Répertoire was still counting. Kastellan used the breach to open a new phase of the siege. He had meant to from the day he took command. The Forge had sent him nine regiments of Hephaestean sappers, and he meant to open a second front under the Flats.
His plan was to put the sappers under the Archflamen's works. Their tunnels would let the Third's soldiers come up behind strongpoints, or under them, where their guns did not point. By opening a front that the Archflamen could not see, Kastellan hoped to take the strongest of his works by surprise and win quick victories against them. He also hoped the Archflamen would not have the strength to match him underground, and would be stretched until he broke. If need be, Kastellan would dig right under the Cinder Wall and the cone, under the ceiling-lances and the Acropolis's wards, and come up inside. The Archflamen would try to stop him, of course. Kastellan was counting on having more soldiers and more stores than the Archflamen did, enough to dig wherever he liked.
He did not mean to stop the war on the surface. It would go on as before, wearing down the Archflamen's strength, which could not be replaced. But it was under the ground, Kastellan believed, that the Third would have its greatest advantage, and where the reduction of the Arsenal would be won.

THE GROUND

On the Flats, the sapper commanders were told to make ready. They began with a survey of the ground in every regiment's sector. Small teams of sappers went forward to the front trenches and drilled deep bore-holes, to find out how deep each layer of rock ran and whether it could be mined. The bore-holes found water too, bad water, which the Third's quartermasters distilled, and heat, which was worse. The tunnels would serve one of two purposes. Shallow saps would let the Third's soldiers move under cover for surprise attacks. Deep galleries would go under the Archflamen's works and end in mines, chambers packed with explosive, to be fired from underneath before a surface attack went in.
On the two hundred and fiftieth cycle of Rotation Ten, the Hephaestean sappers went into the forward trenches with the Line Phalanxes and began to map the ground.
The Cinder Flats were poor ground for mining. The top thirty metres were cinder and ash over pumice, soft and full of holes. It was easy to dig a trench in, and hopeless to tunnel through. The cinder ran. A tunnel would fill itself as fast as it was dug, and its roof would come down. The seep made it worse. The seep-storms drained straight down through the cinder, and a tunnel in it became a stream in a cycle and never had time to dry before the next storm came. The wet cinder swelled and pressed on the bracing from every side at once, without warning. It would have been like tunnelling through wet sand.
Below the cinder, the rock hardened into old lava, and in the lava the seep had made rivers. They ran in channels and caves through the rock, down toward the Phlegethon. Where the river's heat had got into the basalt, the heat veins ran too, the same veins that boiled the trenches on the surface, a thousand degrees and more. This layer could be mined, slowly. But every gallery risked breaking into a seep-river and flooding, or into a heat vein, and a heat vein came up a gallery the way it had come up the Twenty-Ninth's dugout shaft in the first rotation of the siege.
Below that, from about eighty metres down, the rock was the basalt of the spur itself, hard and dry and hot. Galleries cut there could be hewn with picks and would hold with little bracing. The going would be slow, and if the sappers brought in boring engines to speed it, the Archflamen would hear them. The air was bad that deep, and hot, and in places the sappers would meet the old dykes of the shelf, black glass harder than the basalt around it, and have to blast through, and the blast would be heard on the surface.
And at eighty metres down there was nothing that could carry an upload.

THE DEEP LISTS

Every soldier of the Third carried his implant at the base of the skull. When he died, it uploaded what he was to the nearest relay, and the relay passed it up through the noosphere to Watchpoint Hades. It had seven seconds to reach a relay. On the surface, in a trench, a relay pylon was never more than a few hundred metres off, and seven seconds was plenty.
The implant's signal did not go through rock well. Through twenty metres of basalt it went badly. Through eighty it did not go at all.
The Hephaestean sappers knew this. They had always known it. Sappers who died deep did not come back, and the Forge's sapper regiments were raised from volunteers who understood that, and paid three times a trench soldier's rate, and buried their own under the ground where they fell, since there was nothing to send up. The Third's own sappers had known it for six hundred years. The Third's soldiers had never had to go under the ground to fight, so they had never thought about it.
The Third's signallers tried to run relays down the galleries behind the sappers. A relay set in a gallery needed a line to the next relay, and the next, all the way to the surface, and it needed power, and the line had to be shielded from the heat. The relays the Third had were built for trenches. They failed in the heat, or the line was cut by a collapse, or a counter-mine, and the soldiers beyond the break were deep without a relay again. The Third's signallers did not have the craft to build a better one. Nobody on the Flats did.
So the war beneath was fought by soldiers who knew that if they died in it, they died. The Répertoire kept the losses below the ground on a separate list. The sappers called it the Deep List. By the end of the siege it ran to two hundred and twenty thousand names.

THE ENEMY UNDERGROUND

The Third had a strong sapper force now. The Archflamen had no shortage of miners either. Every vault of the Arsenal had been dug by thralls, and the thrall gangs had been digging the heat-wells for eight thousand years. Thousands of the Archflamen's soldiers knew how to dig a gallery and brace it and listen for the enemy, and they had the Arsenal's own cutters and borers to do it with.
And they had the Frost Masons. The Frost Masons of the Cocytus had been building and breaking fortresses under the ice for longer than the Empire had held the Cocytus front. Their craft was the siege, above the ground and under it, and they had no implants to lose. They took command of the Archflamen's counter-mining as soon as they understood what the Third was doing, and they were better at it than anyone the Third had ever fought. Once the Archflamen knew there were galleries coming, the Third could expect every one to be met.
When a sapper officer had chosen where a tunnel should go, his sappers began by sinking a shaft to the depth he wanted. Some shafts went straight down, with ladders. Others went down on a slope, as inclines, so that heavier gear could be brought down them. The mouth of a shaft had to be hidden. Many were behind false trench walls that could be lifted out and put back in seconds, with trench soldiers standing sentry outside. Larger inclines had to be sunk further back from the front, behind a cinder rise, out of sight of the Archflamen's spotters.
The first metres through the cinder went quickly, even dug by hand, with prefabricated bracing put in as the shaft went down. The Hephaestean sappers worked in squads of five. Two cut at the face with picks and mattocks. One carried the spoil back, usually in sandbags for the trenches above. One braced. The fifth listened. The listener sat in a niche in the gallery wall with a geophone augur pressed to the rock and listened, through every watch, for the sound of someone else digging. If a shaft hit wet rock, it had to be sealed with heavier bracing against the swelling and the floods, and that slowed it down. As a tunnel went on, power lines were run along it, and pumps fitted to clear the seep, and winches at the shaft heads to lift the spoil and lower the gear. When the officer judged the shaft deep enough, the sappers began a gallery off the bottom of it. A gallery had to be carefully planned, since it was hard to keep a straight course underground. The squad's optio surveyed it constantly, measuring distance and depth and keeping the heading. At first the galleries were narrow, two sappers wide. As the war beneath went on, the narrow galleries became guides for wider ones, cut by the Forge's boring engines.

THE SILENT DARK

A sapper had many enemies before he ever met the Archflamen's.
The first was collapse. Every tunnel roof will fall in time under its own weight, and every one had to be propped. The second was flooding. The surveys usually found the seep-rivers, and the galleries went around them, and hand pumps cleared what seeped in, but the sappers often worked in water to the ankle, and the water was hot. The third was the air. Deep in the basalt there was little oxygen, and there were gases that had no smell. The sappers carried small gas detectors and respirators with their own air, and when the detectors went red they put on their masks and kept working. The fourth was the heat. In the lowest galleries, near the old heat veins, the rock was hot to the touch, and the sappers worked in shifts of an hour and came back to the shaft foot grey in the face, and drank, and went back.
The last enemy was noise. Under the ground, the only way to find an enemy was by sound. Sound carried well through the rock. A pick on stone made noise. So did a spade, and boots, and voices. In the dark of a deep gallery, any noise could give a tunnel away and bring a counter-mine onto it, and a collapse-charge set in secret. The war beneath was a war of silence. The sappers wrapped their picks in cloth and went barefoot in the deep galleries, and spoke with their hands.
When an enemy tunnel was found, it could be destroyed by counter-mining. A counter-mine team would dig to meet the enemy gallery and set a collapse-charge against it. The charges were long and thin, the shape of a spear, and the Third's sappers called them darts. They were made to throw their blast one way, into the rock, and bring the enemy's gallery down. The Hephaestean sappers carried them made up. The Archflamen's miners made their own from the Arsenal's blasting stores, and the Frost Masons made theirs from ice. A dart was set by boring it into the side of the gallery, walling the gallery behind it with sandbags to hold the blast in, and running a cable back to a safe distance, usually to the surface. When it fired, the rock came down on anyone in the enemy gallery, or walled them in behind a fall of stone that could not be moved. By the time a rescue party dug through, the miners behind the fall were dead of the air. If the counter-miners had found an enemy tunnel without being found themselves, the dart came without any warning. Under the ground, as on the surface, death came out of nowhere. Under the ground, it did not come back.
A counter-mine team might also try to break into an enemy's galleries and take them. When one side broke through into the other's tunnel, there was always a fight, close and short and savage, in the dark lit only by hand lamps swinging, with shotguns and pistols and grenades and knives.

LEAF 54-47. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 402

In the mining under a bunker on Leaf 54-47, a squad of the Sixty-Sixth Sapper Company found an enemy counter-mine and dug in underneath it, and waited for a chance to break in and see where it went. The company set a collapse-dart behind them in their own gallery, as insurance, so that it could be sealed in a moment if the enemy broke through. All work stopped. The listeners listened through every watch, until no digging could be heard in the enemy gallery overhead.
Sapper-Lieutenant Ansel Rook judged that nobody was there. He took a hand-picked section to the end of his gallery and had them break through. Working fast with mattocks and picks, they opened a hole in the floor of the enemy gallery. It was dark and silent beyond. Rook went through first and stood up in the low tunnel with his lamp. He sent half his section to the left, and took the other half to the right himself.
He was close to the Archflamen's forward trench, by his reckoning, when he came to a fork. He put out the lamps and listened. From the right fork came the sound of boots, coming toward him. He took his people up the left fork, and they waited in the dark for the boots to go past, hoping they were going to the tunnel face. Through the gap in his lamp's shutter, he counted six of the Archflamen's miners go by with tools in their hands and pistols on their belts, going the way he had come.
He had a choice to make. If he went on, he might find a way up into an Arsenal trench or bunker. But the six miners were heading straight for the other half of his section. He made up his mind. He put two sappers at the fork to guard it, and to set a demolition charge to drop the junction. With the other three, he would follow the miners and hit them from behind before they met the rest of his section.
He went after them in the dark with his pistol out, three sappers behind him. Before he caught them up, the gallery ahead lit up with lamps coming the other way. The other half of his section was coming back from the face, and did not know the miners were in front of them. Rook fired, and hit the last miner in the back, and the miner went down screaming. Then everything was firing at once in every direction. The miners were caught between the two halves of Rook's section, and they fought hard with their pistols. The gallery was full of ricochets and muzzle flashes and the roar of shotguns, and smoke, and one by one the miners went down. A grenade went off in the gallery, and the blast went down the tunnel and knocked Rook and his three off their feet. In the confusion, Rook had been shot through the arm, and he could feel his sleeve going wet. He got his lamp on and shouted to the sappers ahead that they were friends. The shooting stopped. The six miners were dead. Three of Rook's sappers were hurt. He sent them back through the hole to their own gallery. The rest he took back to the fork, where the charge was nearly ready.
The firefight had been heard, and more of the Archflamen's people were coming to see what it was, and these came ready to fight. They found the two sappers at the fork. As Rook came back toward it, he heard the shotguns going. He ran, bent double in the low gallery, and found his two sappers firing down both forks. Another grenade went off in the gallery, and the blast came through his section a second time and filled the tunnel with grey smoke. Both sappers at the fork were killed by it.
Their implants fired. There was nothing for them to fire to. They were ninety metres down.
Through the smoke, Rook could see more lamps coming. There was no time to fire the charge. He ordered his sappers back to their own gallery, to get ready to seal it, and said that he would hold the fork. He took a dead sapper's shotgun and the spare shells and fired down the forks while they went. The shotgun kept the Archflamen's people back while he worked his way back to the hole, and dropped through it. They were close behind him in the dark, firing blind. Rounds went past him. One hit his breastplate and broke his lamp.
He got back to the detonator at his own gallery's end, where the five survivors of his section were waiting. He was still bleeding and starting to black out. He waited for the Archflamen's people to come down through the hole after him, and then he had the dart fired. The roof of the gallery came down, onto them, and sealed the tunnel.
The Sixty-Sixth Company sent two names up to the Deep List that night. Four of Rook's sappers were wounded, including Rook. His raid had killed at least six of the Archflamen's miners, and probably more. With the enemy gallery found, the Sixty-Sixth fired more darts and destroyed the rest of it.
The Archflamen's miners answered raids like Rook's with traps, and more guards in their galleries. The Frost Masons began to fill the galleries they abandoned with ice, which closed behind them and did not melt, and then to leave galleries open, and empty, for the Third's sappers to find, with darts already set in the walls.

VAULT 9. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLES 60 TO 64

The ground under Leaves 55 and 56 was the most heavily mined on the whole front. The Seventh and Eighth Line Phalanxes both used their sappers there without a break. Their main objective was Vault 9.
Vault 9 was one of the Arsenal's outer vaults, cut into the basalt under the old inner belt, two kilometres outside the Cinder Wall. It was still being used. The Archflamen kept stores and armour in it, and it was a safe shelter for both. The Phaethons had shelled it for a rotation with little to show, since the stores were deep in the rock. The sappers' plan was to dig to it, break in by surprise, and overrun it from below.
The Frost Masons heard them coming, and counter-mined the ground around it heavily. Soon a second war was going on under the Flats, in the dark, around Vault 9. It became clear that the Archflamen knew what the sappers were after, and was doing everything he could to stop them. A maze of shafts and galleries and tunnels grew up under Leaves 55 and 56. With surprise gone, Kastellan sent in the Forge's boring engines to speed the work.
The fighting under the ground there was as heavy as anything on the surface. The sapper companies lost more soldiers than the trench regiments did, and all of them went onto the Deep List. Galleries met and fought every cycle. Fights went on for days as both sides tried to take each other's tunnels and push on from them. Slowly, by numbers, the Hephaestean sappers got the upper hand and pushed the Frost Masons back. On the sixtieth cycle of Rotation Eleven, they reported that they were in a position to break into Vault 9.
Five sapper companies would make the break-in and hold the breach. More companies stood behind them to exploit it, stiffened with breachers in plate, carrying meltas and demolition charges.
The Archflamen knew the attack had to come soon, and was ready for it. He sent reinforcements into the vault, among them a warband of the Cinderborn called the Ninefold Brand, who wanted nothing more than close fighting, room to room and tunnel to tunnel. What the Third's commanders did not know was that most of the vault's stores and armour had already been taken out of it, deeper into the spur. The vault was nearly empty. The stage was set for a battle under the Flats where no tank or shell or mortar could help either side.
To help the battle beneath, the Forty-Ninth and Fiftieth Trench Regiments were ordered to attack on the surface on either side. Their attacks were diversions, but they were to push them hard, and draw in the Archflamen's cohorts, and stretch him. The Fourth Ordnance Regiment was put in place to fire a box barrage over Vault 9, to seal it off from reinforcement from above. Its guns were in position and well stocked when the time came.
Ninety metres under Leaf 55-46, the leading companies went down the shafts and along the galleries, carrying heavy loads of gear and food for a long fight. Two galleries had been cut under the vault, and in each a breaching charge had been laid: hundreds of tonnes of explosive, tamped in with walls of sandbags to drive the blast upward. When they fired, they would blow two wide holes up into the vault and kill whatever was in the rooms above. As the dust settled, the sapper squads would go up through the holes and start clearing the vault's halls and storerooms and barracks.
The vault was a great warren of cut basalt, with storerooms the size of a cathedral nave and barracks for its guard. Most of its defences were on the surface. Inside, the Archflamen's people had built barricades of everything they could find: drums of slag, crates of shell casings, sandbags, blocks of basalt. They blocked the main passages and had heavy guns covering the approaches. A single thrall with a heavy gun behind a barricade could turn a passage into a killing ground. Every passage would have to be stormed, and paid for.

UNDER LEAF 55-46. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 64

On the sixty-fourth cycle, the order to fire the breaching charges was given. Sapper-Captain Odile Marchand of the Twentieth Sapper Company pushed down the plunger.
The blast was heard in the trenches on the surface, ninety metres up, like thunder coming. The ground shook like a quake. Inside Vault 9, the two charges brought down two storerooms. The blast threw blocks of basalt as big as a Thureos across the rooms, and they shattered against the walls. The roofs came down in a slide of rock. The whole vault shook. The heat-lamps failed and it went dark, and the smoke and the dust rolled out down the passages like fog. The thralls nearest the blast were crushed or buried. The battle had begun.
The first squads up through the two holes came into a dark world full of choking fog. There was rubble everywhere, piled metres high. The squads spread out with their lamps swinging and climbed over the rubble. Whatever had been in these rooms to defend them was gone, and for the moment the sappers went forward against nothing. In minutes they were pouring up through the holes.
In the dark beyond the rubble, the Archflamen's thralls were holding their nerve, with their fingers on their triggers, waiting for the shock of the blast to pass. Then a heavy gun opened up and broke the silence. A long burst came down a passage and through the sappers in it, and more guns opened, muzzle flashes in the dark, grenades going off loud enough to deafen. Sappers were hit, cut down with nowhere to go, and the dead piled up in the passages. The sappers fired back with their shotguns, and the noise went up and down the stone. The Archflamen's gunners kept firing down every passage and across every storeroom the sappers came into. The sappers threw grenades back, to knock out each gun in turn, but the gunners were behind their barricades, and any gunner who was hit was replaced. Every barricade cost the sappers dead and wounded. Flame-sections came up, and filled the passages with fire that ate the air and left the passages full of fumes. Far overhead the box barrage rumbled like a slow drum, shaking dust down from the ceilings onto the sappers' helmets as they crouched in the dark.
The first attack stopped. Every attempt to go forward was cut down by fire. The sappers had a toehold in the vault and could get no further. The first fight died down to the odd blast of a shotgun down a passage, to keep the thralls' heads down.
Captain Marchand came forward to see for herself, and to find a way past the barricades. She ordered her sappers to set melta-charges against the inner walls and blow their own passages through them, so that they could come into rooms from where the thralls were not looking. She also authorised gas grenades to drive the thralls out from behind the barricades.
Marchand's orders got the sappers moving again. They went forward carefully. Marchand led. A melta-charge opened a dark narrow passage beyond a wall, and she went through it with her sappers behind her, and came to a flight of steps going down. At the bottom was a heavy blast door, welded shut. She had a melta-charge set on it and pulled back. It went off in an orange flash. The door ran and steamed, and its middle fell in as a pool of molten iron.
Behind it, they were waiting. They had crouched behind a barricade in the dark, out of the melta's blast, ready to spring, with blades of molten iron in their hands. They were the Ninefold Brand. As Marchand came through the cooling breach, they stood up and charged, howling a hymn of the Red Furnace. Marchand was the first to die. They cut her apart in seconds. Her implant fired into ninety metres of basalt. The sappers behind her fell back, and the Cinderborn cut them down one after another, and the passage ran with blood. It became a slaughterhouse. The sappers could not match them. The Cinderborn were tall and fast and strong, and the river's fire ran in them, and they wanted nothing but to kill. In the dark of Vault 9 they killed sappers by the hundred and left the bodies where they fell.
The fury of the Cinderborn's counter-attack stopped the sappers again. They sealed their breaches against the Cinderborn, who now went where they liked through the vault's passages, and fought to keep what they had taken. Their gas grenades did nothing to the Cinderborn, who did not seem to breathe.
For the rest of the first cycle the deadlock held. On the second cycle the breachers were sent in from the reserve, in the hope that their plate would do what the sappers could not. They had to make the same rush down the same fire-swept passages, and were cut down the same way. But in a few places they drove the thralls off a barricade with a well-thrown grenade, and turned the barricade around. Then the Cinderborn counter-attacked again, at the barricades, through the breachers' fire. At one barricade held by breachers, the Cinderborn charged with rounds coming off their armour and leaving scorch marks and doing nothing else. Grenades went off among them and they barely stopped. A breacher with a heavy flame-gun sent a stream of keyed fire down the passage. The first of the Cinderborn walked into it and through it and over the barricade, burning, singing, and killed the breacher who had fired. Those breachers who did not run were killed. The Third's soldiers had no answer to an enemy that the fire did not hurt.
On the third cycle it got worse. The Frost Masons had expected the attack and prepared well, and that included digging counter-mines in secret. Their aim was to cut the Third's approach galleries. While the battle went on inside the vault, their miners crept closer to the Third's tunnels. A gallery came down without warning. A sapper squad on its way to the vault was in it, and the squad was buried. Now the whole flow of soldiers and supplies to the battle ran through a single gallery. If that one was cut, everyone in the vault would be cut off and wiped out. The sappers could not risk it. Squads were pulled out of the fight to dig counter-mines and man extra listening posts. With the box barrage still pounding the surface, the listeners could hardly hear anything.
For three cycles it had been a desperate fight. All five sapper companies of the first wave had lost terribly. Each had gone in six hundred strong, and most were now below half. The Twentieth Sapper Company was down to a hundred and eighty-seven. Many had been killed hand to hand by the Cinderborn. All of them were on the Deep List.
After four cycles of no gains, the attack was called off. The Frost Masons were digging again, toward the last supply gallery. An attempt to dig through the first fall had ended badly: the Frost Masons had been waiting for the diggers, and fired a second dart that brought the gallery down on them too. There would be no breakthrough at Vault 9 this time, and one by one the sapper squads came back.
They came up out of the shafts into the forward trenches filthy and black with smoke, many of them walking wounded, after four cycles under the ground in a battle with no quarter. They had failed to take Vault 9. They would try again.
Three battles were fought for Vault 9 in all, before the Twenty-Second Trench Regiment overran it from the surface at the end of Rotation Eleven. The Archflamen's people trapped inside by the advance were given no mercy. The sappers sealed every exit and guarded it. Then they opened the heat-well conduits that ran under the vault from the Arsenal's casting lines, which the Archflamen's own engineers had shut off at the start of the siege, and let the Phlegethon in. The river's fire filled Vault 9 to the roof, and set, and the Archflamen's people were in it, and so were the Ninefold Brand, if the river's fire could kill them. Nothing could have lived in it. The sappers had their revenge.

THE GREAT MINE. ROTATION ELEVEN, CYCLE 80 TO ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 230

The failure at Vault 9 did not stop the war beneath elsewhere. Every regiment had sapper companies with it now, working under the ground. A second front ran under the trenches of the Flats, and the silent fighting went on every cycle, taking its toll of both sides. The Hephaestean sappers crept slowly toward the thing they had been sent for, which was the Cinder Wall.
The Cinder Wall was not a single wall any more. It was thirty metres high and twelve thick, built of the Arsenal's own cast basalt, with bunkers and barracks inside it and guns along its top. Over the rotations of the siege, the Frost Masons had laid trenches and pillboxes in front of it, and with them it made one more line of works, thinner than the three belts, but still a line, and it would have to be taken by assault.
Kastellan's plan had always been to mine the Wall, lay great charges under it, and blow breaches through it. Phaethon fire and the Wrath bombers might do it in time, but they were needed elsewhere. Until the Third's trenches were close enough to the Wall to fire on it over open sights, a great mine was the best chance of a breach.
On the hundred and ninetieth cycle of Rotation Twelve, more than a rotation after the mining had begun, the sappers reached it. Under Leaf 57-44, the Sixty-First Sapper Company reported that it had found the Wall's deep footings and was ready to lay a mine for a breach. In complete secrecy and silence, the sappers carried the explosive down to the chamber they had cut under the footings. Each charge was stacked by hand, and a second detonator wired to the first. It took many cycles of silent work to build the mine. On the surface, the Thirty-Fifth Trench Regiment got ready to attack. It would be the Thirty-Fifth's task to go first through the breach, and force a way through the last works between the Third and the Acropolis.
Only the Sixty-First knew what was being planned. Across the Flats, the other mining was stepped up to draw the Archflamen's attention away from Leaf 57-44 while the mine was laid. Under Leaf 56-46, the sappers drove heavy borers at the Wall as a deliberate diversion. The Frost Masons heard them at once, and sent several counter-mines against them, and there were more bloody fights in the dark.
The mine under Leaf 57-44 would be the largest of the siege. A wide breach would need a large charge, and it nearly emptied the Seventh Line Phalanx's stores. The Third would have one chance at it.
Zero hour was set for the two hundred and thirtieth cycle of Rotation Twelve. The Seventh Line Phalanx's command gathered in a bunker three kilometres back to watch the mine and the attack. The cables were checked and checked again, and every sapper was brought up out of the galleries, as the count ran down. The warning went out on the relay to every trench on Leaf 57-44.
The sapper-major at the detonator was a son of Hephaestus with one hand, who had lost the other in the first rotation of the war beneath. He counted down from ten on the open relay and fired the mine at zero.
A wind came up out of the shafts and galleries behind the Third's trenches and blew cinder and stone back out of their mouths. The ground began to shake hard. A column of yellow fire went up off the Flats in front of the Wall, hundreds of metres high, and black smoke behind it, and behind the smoke the rock. The column of rock and basalt and cast Wall went up, and up, like a mountain growing out of the Flats, and spread into a great cloud under the ceiling, and hung there, and began to come down. With a long crash, the shock rolled out across no-man's land and through the trenches and dugouts of the Thirty-Fifth. In front of them everything was hidden in smoke and dust.
When the smoke cleared, the watchers in the bunker could see that the Wall had gone. A long stretch of it had been blown to pieces. In its place was a crater, a hundred and ninety metres across. The mine had done what it was meant to do. The Third had its breach. Now it had to take it.
The Thirty-Fifth's guns fired together, onto the ground beyond the Wall, shell after shell, to prepare it for the first wave in its Hoplons.

THE WAR ABOVE. ROTATION TEN, CYCLE 250 TO ROTATION TWELVE, CYCLE 230

With the Third refitted, and Behemoths of its own on the Flats, the balance had shifted back toward the Empire. The Third could match the drowned Behemoths now. With the Wrath wings on the Flats, it could fight for the ceiling geometry too.
With the drowned had come the things that flew. The Stymphalian swarms of the deep had always come up the rivers, and for six hundred years the ceiling-lances had burned them out of the dark as fast as they came. Now the lances were the Archflamen's and the swarms came up unopposed. With the swarms came the drowned ships' own craft, old Lanterns and attack boats flown by drowned crews, fast and hard to see. They struck where they liked, bombing and strafing. The Third had almost nothing to fire back with. It was short of anti-air guns. The plan for the siege had never thought it would need many, since the ceiling-lances had kept the air clear for six centuries, and the few it had brought had long since been turned on the ground or lost to the shellfire. The Third had no air support of its own to call on. The air over the Flats belonged to the drowned.
They could strike anywhere. Nowhere on the Flats was safe. Swarms and drowned Lanterns came down on supply dumps and gun lines across the Flats, and even on the foot of the Cinder Stair, where they cut the cables twice and stopped the supply. The Third's losses to the air grew every cycle.
Kastellan could meet them now. The Wrath interceptors would defend the Flats and escort the bombers, and the bombers gave the Third a reach far beyond its guns. The Acropolis would soon be under regular bombing. Strongpoints could be hit by precision bombing at low level. Whole stretches of the front could be carpeted before an attack. The war was going to grow again.
The interceptor wing had ten squadrons of about twenty Wraths each. It would mostly defend, flying patrols over the Flats every watch to meet the swarms when they came, and flying with the bombers as escort. The bomber wing had seven squadrons of a dozen heavy Wraths each, with four Vigil-pattern craft for scouting and command. Eighty-odd bombers, heavily loaded, could pound the Archflamen every watch.
It took time to make the craft ready after their long journey, and to stock enough fuel and munitions, while labourers built landing fields for both wings on the old outer belt. On the three hundred and thirtieth cycle of Rotation Ten, the work was done and the first missions were ready. The Flats, which had never had a sky, had engines in the dark above them for the first time in six hundred years.
To the soldiers in the trenches, looking up at the running lights of the bombers and their escorts going over under the ceiling, the Wrath crews seemed to have the easiest war on the Flats. They did not face the shelling every watch, or the walk across no-man's land into the guns. They lived in hardened barracks far back on the old outer belt, and knew nothing about the line. The crews knew better.
The Acropolis was ringed with guns. The Arsenal had built its own air defence over eight thousand years against the swarms, and on top of the guns there were the ceiling-lances, which the Archflamen's gunners had learned to fire flat across the Flats and could fire upward just as well. The crews who flew against the Acropolis called it the Wasp Nest. A raid on it was met with a wall of flak and lance-beams, and the swarms came up out of the river cloud to meet the bombers and went for them without any regard for their own losses. The dogfights under the ceiling were savage, and the Wrath wings' losses grew. Over the Acropolis, a Wrath crewman had less chance of coming back than a trench soldier did, and when he did not come back, he was more likely to come back null. The noosphere over the Acropolis was the noosphere nearest the Echo Mast, and the relay crews at the Wrath fields learned that uploads from crews shot down over the cone arrived at Watchpoint Hades empty half the time. The war in the air was as bitter as the war on the ground.
The Acropolis was the main target. The first Vigil missions found that in the rotations of the siege, the Archflamen had not lived only on the stores in the vaults, vast as they were. The cone was now ringed at its foot with forges of its own. A shanty-town of foundries and machine shops and casting pits had grown up around the cone inside the Cinder Wall, where tanks were repaired and shells and rifles were made, working every watch to supply the front. They were beyond the reach of the Third's guns, and they became the bombers' first targets. The Acropolis itself was too well defended, under its wards and its guns. The forges around it were well defended too, and the air war over them was the fiercest on the Flats, the bombers going in through the flak to hit them. There were other missions, against strongpoints at the front and against the Archflamen's roads, but the cone came first. As the air war grew, the Third's soldiers stopped looking up when the Wraths went over.
Kastellan was assured that the Fleet's commitment to the Flats was for the length of the siege, as the Third's was. A steady stream of new craft and crews would keep the wings up to strength. The Archflamen's strength in the air was unknown, and hard to count, since the swarms came and went with the river. Estimates ran from two hundred to five hundred. The drowned and the swarms probably had more craft at the start. As the losses mounted on both sides, and the Fleet's replacements came down, by the middle of Rotation Eleven most crews thought the two sides were about level. From then on, the Wrath wings would be gaining, if only by numbers.
The craft that fought over the Flats had little in common. Most of the interceptor wing flew the standard Wrath, the workhorse of the Fleet's strike wings, well armed and well armoured, fast and handy enough. A few squadrons flew the lighter Wrath Seconds, quicker and lighter and less heavily armed, which could stay with the swarms in a turning fight. The bomber wing's heavy Wraths had long range and a big load of bombs, and were slow and heavy and could not fly without escort. The Vigils carried no bombs at all. They carried augurs and imagers instead, for scouting a target before a raid or looking at it after, and they were few and precious.
Against them came the swarms and the drowned craft. The Stymphalians were small and fast and turned inside anything the Fleet flew, and came in numbers, in clouds, around the Wrath formations, throwing their bronze. The drowned Lanterns were bigger and heavier, fighter-bombers that carried bombs as well as guns and could still hold their own against a Wrath in a turning fight. So far nothing bigger had come up out of the river to fly. The crews on the Wrath fields talked about what would happen if something did.
