**CAMP HALF-BLOOD — THE CANOE LAKE DOCK**

**T+19 HOURS AFTER DEPARTURE**

---

The last message from Alaska came in at 1:14 in the morning, and it was nine words long.

Percy was on the dock when it arrived. He had been on the dock most of the night. He'd tried to sleep in Cabin Three and lasted about an hour before the quiet got to him, the particular quiet of a cabin where there should have been a pegasus snoring outside the window and a bronze sword on the nightstand and was neither. So he'd come down to the lake and sat with his feet in the water, and he'd let the water tell him about the camp.

The camp was not doing well.

The water knew about the pyre on the beach, still smoking. It knew about the long table in the Big House infirmary where Apollo's children were working in shifts with their hands glowing and their faces grey. It knew about the Fifth Cohort's tents in the strawberry fields, and about the Imperial encampment on the southern field, where the grounded Aquilas sat in a row like beached whales and a sentry walked the perimeter every nine minutes exactly. It knew about the two sentries on the line between the tents and the Aquilas, one Roman and one Neodamode. For most of the night they had pretended the other wasn't there. About an hour ago, one of them had offered the other a cigarette.

It was a start. Percy was trying very hard to count it as a start.

*Varlet.*

He opened his eyes.

*A burst, relayed from Neumond's wings through Lambda-Two to the Watchpoint fragment and from the fragment to me. The signal is heavily degraded. The land beyond the gods does not like tachyons any more than it likes gods.* A pause. *I will give it to you verbatim.*

"Go."

*CONTACT. JADE COURT EARLY. MORTALS EARLY. THREE-WAY. HOLDING.*

Percy waited.

"That's it?"

*That is the whole of it. After that, the relay went dark. I have been trying to raise Lambda-Two for eleven minutes. Nothing. No carrier. No beacon. Not even the automated handshake.*

"The land beyond the gods."

*Possibly. Or a Jade Court formation array. The cultivators are said to be very fond of locks.* Her voice was very level, which was how Percy knew she was worried. *Or Lambda-Two is no longer in a condition to answer.*

Percy pulled his feet out of the lake.

Two days, she'd said. The Jade Court two days out. The mortals three. That had been yesterday afternoon. The team had left at dawn with a full day's margin, and the margin had been the whole point, the reason they'd sent a small team in a fast ship instead of waiting for Olympus to finish arguing. Get in. Get the dropship. Get out before anybody else showed up.

*JADE COURT EARLY. MORTALS EARLY.*

"How early?"

*I do not know. My estimates were built on the Watchpoint's tracking, and the Watchpoint's tracking was built on its model of the Arctic, and its model of the Arctic is a model of a battlefield that has not been fought on yet. Alaska Hill is fourteen thousand years of records deep in its memory and eleven years in our future.* Something like frustration crept into her voice. *It has been looking at this ground through the wrong end of a telescope, Varlet. I should have seen it.*

"Who's on that team," Percy said, although he knew.

*Castellan Oriel. Straban. Neumond. Owl-Two. Hrafn. Dakota of the Fifth.* A pause. *Thalia.*

With her arm in a sling, and a hole in Aegis the size of a coin.

Percy stood up.

---

Artemis was waiting for him on the path up from the lake.

He hadn't heard her come. He never did. She was just there, at the top of the slope, silver in the moonlight, with her arms folded and her face very still. It was the particular stillness of someone who had already had this argument with herself and lost.

"You heard," Percy said.

"I heard." She didn't move. "Your AI is not the only one with ears on the Imperial net. Marien sent a runner to my tent four minutes ago."

"Then you know I have to go."

"I know that you *think* you have to go."

"Arty—"

"Do not." Her voice snapped, sharp as a bowstring, and then softened just as quickly. She looked away, across the dark lake. "Do not *Arty* me, Perseus. Not tonight."

He waited.

"I cannot go with you," she said. "You understand that? It isn't the Accord. It isn't my father. It is the ground. The land beyond the gods. I went there once, before Alaska was ever called Alaska, chasing a white stag across the ice. I lasted six hours. By the end of them I could barely draw my bow." Her jaw tightened. "And that was when I was strong. Now half of what I have runs through you. If I go north, I will be a candle in a gale, and I will be drawing on *you* to stay lit while you fight."

"Then stay here."

"I am *going* to stay here. I am telling you that I am going to stay here, so that you understand what it costs me." She turned back to him. Her eyes were very bright. "My Lieutenant is up there. She went because I would not send you. Because I thought, *let her go, she has Aegis and a head on her shoulders, she will keep them together.* And now there is no signal, and the only person on this camp who can reach her fast enough is the one person I did not want to send."

"I'll bring her back."

"You will bring *yourself* back," Artemis said. "That is an order from the Director. Bring her back if you can. Bring yourself back regardless." She stepped closer. Close enough that he could smell pine and cold air on her. "Do you understand me? I have seen what happens to you on that ground. I have memories of it that I have not lived yet, and I do not want to live them."

Percy didn't know what to say to that. So he did the only thing he could think of. He put his arms around her.

She stiffened. For a second, he thought she was going to push him away, the way she had on the porch before the landing. Then she didn't. She put her forehead against his shoulder, very briefly, and let out a breath that shook.

"Come back to me," she said into his shirt. So quietly the lake almost didn't hear it.

"Yeah."

"That is not a promise."

"I'll come back to you," Percy said. "Promise."

She stepped back. Her face was composed again, the Director's face, cool and silver.

"Take Theodosia," she said. "Apollo finished with her an hour ago. She is insufferable about it."

---

Eleni was more than insufferable about it. She was waiting for him outside the Big House in full armor, with her helmet under one arm and a fresh pink scar at her hairline where a QVARTUS round had very nearly ended her career three days ago, and when she saw Percy coming, she snapped to attention so hard that her boots rang.

"Milord."

"How's the head?"

"Present, milord. Lord Apollo says I will have headaches for a month. He also says that I have the thickest skull he has ever treated, and that he has treated Heracles." She said it with enormous pride. "I asked him to put that in writing."

Behind her, on the Big House porch, Apollo was sitting on the rail with a cup of coffee and dark circles under his eyes that his godly glamour wasn't bothering to hide anymore. He raised the cup in a weary salute.

"Don't get her shot again," he said. "I mean it. I'm running out of hands."

"Wasn't planning on it."

"You never are, little bro." Apollo sipped his coffee and looked, for a moment, almost serious. "Alaska eats gods. You know that, right? Whatever you run into up there, you're going to have a lot less of us behind you than you're used to. Dad can't throw lightning that far north. Uncle P's sea freezes. I'll be lucky to get a sunbeam through the cloud cover."

"Minerva says that's the point."

"Minerva," said Apollo, "is a toaster with opinions."

*I heard that.*

"She heard that," Percy said.

"Good." Apollo slid off the rail. His voice dropped. "Bring Thalia home, Perce. Art won't say it, so I will. She sent her Lieutenant up there instead of you, and if something happens to that girl, my sister is going to carry it for about three thousand years."

"I know."

"Yeah." Apollo looked at him for a long moment. "Yeah, I think you do."

**THE LABYRINTH — ENTRANCE BENEATH ZEUS'S FIST**

---

The Labyrinth was still angry with him.

Percy could feel it as soon as they went through the crack in the rock. The air in the first corridor was close and stale and hostile, the walls sweating, the floor tilting very slightly downhill in a way that meant *you will walk this way whether you like it or not.* Somewhere ahead, water dripped in an irregular rhythm that sounded almost like laughter.

Last time, it had taken blood and pre-Athenian Greek to buy a single corridor. Last time, he'd forgotten to pay it on the way in.

He wasn't going to forget again.

"Minerva," he said. "What does it want?"

*I do not know, Varlet. It is not in my records. Daedalus's architecture was never fully understood even in my era. The Répertoire's own models of it are mostly guesses with error bars.* A pause. *But I noticed something, the last time. It liked the blood, but it responded to the Greek. It is old. It is lonely. I think it wants to be spoken to.*

Percy thought about that.

Then he drew the knife Eleni had given him, cut his palm, and put his bleeding hand flat against the sweating wall. And he talked to it.

He didn't use pre-Athenian Greek. He wasn't sure he could have managed it without Minerva feeding him the words, and somehow that felt like cheating. He just talked. He told it about the camp and about the pyre on the beach. He told it about a team of kids and soldiers from two different ends of time who had gone north together because a piece of paper said they should, and who had stopped answering. He told it that he knew it had been built to trap people and that it probably didn't like being asked for favors. He told it that he'd come back someday, when this was over, and walk it properly. All of it. Every corridor. Not as a shortcut. Just to see it.

He wasn't sure where that last part came from. He meant it.

The wall under his hand went still.

The dripping stopped. The air changed. It didn't get warmer, exactly, but it got less *close*, as if something enormous that had been leaning over his shoulder had straightened up and taken a step back.

Ahead of them, the corridor rearranged itself. Walls slid. Stones ground. A dozen side passages sealed themselves shut, one after another, with a sound like doors closing down a long hallway, and when it was over, there was a single straight tunnel running ahead of them into the dark. Its floor sloped gently upward. A cold wind came down it that smelled of snow.

"Milord," Eleni whispered. "Did you just make a *promise* to the Labyrinth of Daedalus?"

"Yeah."

"Is that wise?"

"Probably not." Percy wrapped his hand. "Come on."

*For the record, Varlet,* Minerva said as they started up the tunnel, *you have now made binding promises to the Labyrinth, to Pontus and to the Director, all within the same week. I am starting a spreadsheet.*

"Add it to the list."

*I just said I was starting a spreadsheet.*

**BROOKS RANGE, ALASKA — NORTH OF ATIGUN PASS**

**CHARLIE COMPANY, 1ST BATTALION (AIRBORNE), 501ST INFANTRY REGIMENT, 11TH AIRBORNE DIVISION**

---

Captain Ruth Dalgaard had been in the Army for eleven years, and in all that time she had never been given an order that made less sense than the one she was currently carrying out.

It had come down the chain three days ago in the middle of the night. It came with a classification level she'd only ever seen in training, and a list of signatures on it that got more alarming the further down you read. *Anomalous object. Suspected aircraft. Suspected foreign origin. Location: Brooks Range, grid reference attached. Secure. Do not approach without authorization. Do not photograph. Do not discuss.* Then, in a different font, as if someone had added it in a hurry at the last minute: *NASA liaison to follow.*

The NASA liaison had not followed.

What had followed was a second order, eighteen hours ago, telling her to go in a day ahead of schedule. It didn't say why. It did say, in a line that had clearly been typed by someone very tired or very frightened, *Recent satellite imagery indicates other parties may be converging on the object.*

*Other parties.* In the Brooks Range. In March.

So here she was. Seventy-one paratroopers, two CH-47s out of Fort Wainwright with the Sugar Bears flying them, a very unhappy weather officer and a load of cold-weather gear that had been designed for minus forty and was currently being tested against minus forty-six. The Chinooks were holding in a valley six kilometers south of the grid while her lead platoon went forward on skis to take a look.

The lead platoon had gone forward forty minutes ago.

Twenty minutes ago, they had started reporting things.

"Say again, One-Six," Dalgaard said into the handset. She kept her voice very level. It was a trick her first platoon sergeant had taught her. *The more insane it gets, ma'am, the calmer you sound.* "Say again your last."

"*Charlie Six, One-Six.*" Lieutenant Aguilar's voice was very level too. Too level. "*I say again. Personnel on the ridge. Estimate thirty. Wearing robes. Negative cold-weather gear. Negative footwear on some of them, ma'am. They're standing in the snow barefoot.*"

"Copy barefoot."

"*There's more, ma'am.*"

"Go."

A pause.

"*Some of them are standing on swords.*"

Dalgaard closed her eyes.

"One-Six, clarify *standing on swords.*"

"*I don't know how to clarify it, ma'am. There are swords. They are about a meter and a half long. They are about three meters off the ground. There are people standing on them. The swords are not attached to anything.*" Another pause. "*One of them just flew over to the other side of the ridge. On the sword. Like a surfboard.*"

In the Chinook's cabin, her first sergeant, a heavyset Alaskan named Ivanoff who had grown up in Bethel and had seen, by his own account, most of the strange things that could be seen in the bush, took off his helmet and put it back on again.

"Well," he said. "That's new."

"*Ma'am, there's a second group.*"

"Talk to me."

"*On the far side of the objective. Smaller. Eight, maybe nine personnel. Mixed gear. Some of them are in armor. Not ours. Not anyone's I know. One of them's got*—" Aguilar stopped. When he came back, his voice had changed. "*Ma'am, one of them's got wings. Metal wings. He's standing on the objective. On top of it. And there's a girl with a shield and a spear, and there's a*—"

The transmission cut into a squeal of static.

When it cleared, a new voice was on the net. It was not Aguilar. It was calm and courteous and faintly amused, and it spoke English with a precise, formal accent that Dalgaard couldn't place.

"*Mortal captain,*" said the voice. "*You have entered land under the observation of the Northern Dipper Office of the Celestial Court. Withdraw your iron birds. This matter does not concern you.*"

Dalgaard looked at the handset.

She looked at Ivanoff.

Ivanoff looked back at her with an expression that said, very clearly, *ma'am, I'm from Bethel, and even I've got nothing.*

"Who am I speaking to," Dalgaard said into the handset.

"*You are speaking to a Censor of the Court of the Jade Emperor, mortal. That is all you need to know. Withdraw.*"

The net went dead.

Dalgaard sat very still for three seconds.

Then she keyed the handset on the aviation net.

"Sugar Bear One-One, Charlie Six. Get us in the air. We're going forward."

"*Charlie Six, confirm going forward?*"

"Confirm," said Captain Ruth Dalgaard, very calmly. "My lead platoon is up there."

**OBSCURUS ONE — THE RIDGE**

---

Thalia Grace had decided, somewhere around the fourth hour of the standoff, that she hated everybody.

She hated the cold. It got into the break in her arm like a nail. She hated Aegis, and the coin-sized hole in its face where a bullet from fourteen thousand years in the future had punched through the only thing she'd ever trusted to stop anything. She hated the dropship, which sat under its cloak in the snow at the center of the ridge like a big invisible egg that everybody on the mountain wanted to steal. She hated Neumond, who was standing on top of it with his bronze wings half-spread, trying to wake up its systems, and who kept saying *almost* in a tone of voice that meant *not even close.*

She hated the Jade Court.

There were thirty-four of them. She'd counted twice. They had come over the northern ridge an hour after the team had landed. That was thirty-six hours ahead of every estimate the Watchpoint had given. They didn't come on foot. They came on swords, or on clouds, or in one case on a large white crane that sat on the ridge afterward preening itself and ignoring everyone. They wore long robes in blue and grey and black, embroidered with stars, and most of them had no shoes on. They did not seem to feel the cold at all.

Their leader was a tall, narrow man with a long wispy beard, a black official's cap with stiff wings sticking out of the sides, and an ivory tablet held in both hands in front of his chest. He had introduced himself as Lu Xuanming, Censor of the Northern Dipper Office. He had been very polite about it. He had then produced nine jade discs from his sleeve, thrown them into the air around the dropship, and watched them settle into the snow in a perfect ring.

The moment the last disc touched down, every signal the team had died.

Neumond's wings went dark. Lambda-Two's link to the Watchpoint dropped. Thalia's own sense of the sky, the faint constant hum of her father's domain that she'd carried in her bones her whole life, simply switched off. It left a silence in her head so total that she'd nearly thrown up.

*A formation,* Straban had said grimly. *A lock. They've locked the ground.*

Since then, it had been a standoff.

The Jade Court stood on the northern half of the ridge. The team stood on the southern half, around the dropship, with the jade ring between them. And for four hours, the Censor and Castellan Oriel had been *talking*.

Thalia had never heard so much talking in her life.

"The vessel fell upon the land beneath the Northern Dipper," the Censor was saying now, for what Thalia was fairly sure was the sixth time. He said it gently, as if explaining something to slow children. "All things that fall beneath the Dipper fall under the eye of its Office. This has been so since before your Olympus was a hill. The vessel is a heavenly artifact of unknown provenance. It must be registered. It must be examined. It must be *understood.*"

"The vessel is property of the Undying Olympian Empire," Oriel said, for what Thalia was sure was the seventh. The Castellan had her helmet off. Her face was grey with cold and exhaustion, and there was frost on her eyebrows, but her voice hadn't changed in four hours. "It was landed here by an Imperial cell under the Empire's own authority. It is being recovered under the terms of the Anabasis Accord, which binds the Olympian Council, the Twelfth Legion Fulminata and the Empire's forces on this world. The Court is not a party to that Accord. It has no claim."

"An Accord," said the Censor, "signed two days ago, between the gods of a single hill and an army from nowhere." He smiled. "Castellan, the Court has been keeping its registers for four thousand years. Forgive us if we do not tremble at your ink."

"You're not getting the ship."

"We shall see."

Beside Thalia, Dakota of the Fifth Cohort shifted his weight in the snow and muttered something under his breath in Latin that she was pretty sure Reyna would have made him run laps for.

Dakota had not said a word to any of the Imperials since they'd left camp. Not to Oriel. Not to Straban. Not to Neumond. Especially not to Owl-Two, the Sciritae scout leader who stood a few meters away with her rifle across her chest and her pale eyes on the Jade Court's line. Thalia knew why. Everybody on the team knew why. Dakota had been standing next to a girl named Gwen when the Psiloi volley hit the Fifth Cohort's shield wall, and Owl-Two had been the one calling in corrections for that volley from the treeline.

And Thalia hadn't said a word to Owl-Two either. Owl-Two's lance had been the one that walked into the Hunt in the woods. Ismene had died in those woods. Ismene, who had been fifteen for three hundred years and had never once in all that time learned to cook anything except burnt rabbit.

Thalia looked at the Sciritae scout's profile, at the Hunter-grey eyes and the silver ghost of a crescent tattooed at the corner of one of them. She thought, not for the first time, that somewhere in that woman's blood was one of *her* sisters. Some Hunter from some future age who had broken her oath, or never taken it, and passed her eyes down fourteen thousand years to a girl who had grown up to shoot at the Hunt.

She didn't know what to do with that. So she did nothing with it.

"Lieutenant," Hrafn said quietly beside her.

The big Trophimoi legacy had been silent for most of the standoff too, leaning on his rune-axe with his eyes on the sky. Twice in the last hour, Thalia had seen a raven land on a rock near him, look at him, and fly away again.

"What?"

"Listen."

Thalia listened.

At first, she heard nothing but the wind. Then, underneath it, faint and far to the south and getting louder, she heard a sound she hadn't heard in a long time. A deep, rhythmic *whup-whup-whup*, like two giant hearts beating slightly out of time.

Helicopters.

On the northern ridge, the Censor had heard it too. For the first time in four hours, he stopped smiling.

"You have brought mortals," he said.

"We didn't bring anyone," Oriel said sharply.

"The iron birds come from your south. Your people came from your south." The Censor lifted his ivory tablet an inch higher, and every cultivator on the ridge behind him shifted at once. "The mortal captain was warned. Twice. The Court does not warn a third time."

"Censor, wait—"

He didn't wait.

---

Thalia would remember the next few minutes in pieces, like a stained-glass window somebody had dropped.

She remembered the first Chinook coming over the southern ridge, huge and dark green and impossibly loud. Its rotors kicked up a storm of snow, and she could see the faces of the soldiers in its open tail ramp. They were kids. Most of them were barely older than the Roman legionaries. Their faces were white with fear and their rifles were pointed at the Jade Court's line.

She remembered one of the cultivators, a young woman in blue robes, standing on her sword in the air above the ridge. The woman raised two fingers in a sword-sign and swept them sideways, almost lazily, the way you'd brush a fly off a table.

She remembered the line of light that came off those fingers. It crossed the ridge in no time at all and cut the Chinook's rear rotor off at the hub.

She remembered the sound it made.

She remembered the soldiers on the ramp.

She remembered herself screaming *NO* and running, and her body throwing Aegis up without asking her, even with the hole in it. She remembered Owl-Two's rifle cracking beside her, three shots in less than a second, and the cultivator on the sword jerking and tumbling out of the air. She remembered thinking, very clearly, *good.*

Then everything started at once.

The cultivators came off the northern ridge like a flock of birds, on swords and clouds and bare feet running across the tops of the snow without sinking. Oriel was shouting orders. Straban threw up a wall of wind so hard it ripped the snow off the ridge down to the rock. Hrafn's axe burned white with runes, and he waded into the first wave with a roar that sounded like it had come out of a much older throat than his. Neumond dropped off the dropship with a pistol in each hand and his wings screaming.

And down in the valley, the second Chinook, the one that hadn't been hit, flared hard and put down on the snowfield below the ridge. Its ramp dropped. Paratroopers poured off it into waist-deep snow and started shooting.

At everyone.

They didn't know who anyone was. They couldn't. Nobody on that ridge looked like anything they'd ever been trained to fight. The Jade Court had cut their friends out of the sky, so they shot at the Jade Court. But Neumond had metal wings, and Straban was standing on nothing, and Hrafn was glowing, so they shot at them too.

A round spanged off Aegis an inch from Thalia's face.

"*Mortal fire!*" Oriel was bellowing. "*Mortal fire from the south! Do not return fire! Do not return fire on mortals! Article Four, Article Four—*"

"*They're shooting at us, Castellan!*"

"*I know they're shooting at us! Article Four!*"

Thalia understood, in a strange, cold, detached way, what was going to happen.

The Accord said no contact with mortals. The Accord said the Empire's soldiers couldn't fire on them, and Thalia knew Oriel would hold her people to it even if it killed them, because Oriel was the kind of officer who would. The Jade Court had no such rule. The Censor had told the mortals to leave. They hadn't left. The cultivators were going to go through them like a scythe through wheat. The only people on the ridge who could stop it were the team, and the team couldn't shoot the mortals, and the mortals were shooting at the team.

And the whole time, the jade ring around the dropship glowed, and her father's sky stayed silent in her head. No lightning was coming. No help was coming.

It was the Fifth Cohort's shield wall all over again. Only this time, it was going to be the mortals.

Something slammed into her from the side.

She went down in the snow, hard, on her broken arm, and the pain was so white and total that for a second she couldn't see. When she could see again, Dakota was on top of her, and past his shoulder, she saw a cultivator's sword-light go through the place where her head had been.

He'd pushed her down.

Dakota, who hadn't said a word to anybody.

"*Lieutenant!*" He was shouting in her face. "*Get up! Get UP!*"

She got up.

And that was how she saw Owl-Two die.

It happened very fast. There were three cultivators on Dakota's blind side. Thalia didn't know where they'd come from. They were on him before he could turn, one of them already swinging, and Owl-Two was moving. The Sciritae scout crossed eight meters of snow in a blur of grey that Thalia's eyes couldn't follow. She put her body between Dakota and the blade and fired her rifle point-blank into the chest of the cultivator behind it.

The cultivator died.

So did Owl-Two.

The sword went into her under the arm, where the armor plates met. She went down on her knees in the snow beside Dakota. For a second, she looked almost puzzled. Then she looked up at the Roman kid whose friend she had helped kill three days ago, and she said something to him that Thalia didn't catch.

Then she fell over sideways and didn't move.

Dakota stood there.

He stood there staring down at her for maybe two seconds, which on that ridge was a very long time. Then he made a sound Thalia had never heard a person make. He turned on the two remaining cultivators with his gladius in his hand, and he did something to them that Reyna would never have made him run laps for.

Afterward, he knelt beside Owl-Two in the snow and put his hand on her back, between her shoulder blades, and he didn't take it away.

**THE LABYRINTH — EXIT, OBSCURUS ONE**

---

Percy came out of the Labyrinth into a snowstorm made of gunfire.

The tunnel spat them out on the southern slope of the ridge, forty meters below the crest. The first thing he saw was the Chinook. It was on its side in the snow at the foot of the slope, rotors shattered, tail section torn open. Men were crawling out of the wreck. Some of them were dragging other men. Some of them were not moving.

The second thing he saw was the ridge above him, lit up like a fireworks show.

The third thing he felt was nothing at all.

It hit him the moment his boots touched the snow. It was the same thing he'd felt the last time, in this same range, three days ago. It had just been easier not to notice then. A silence. A *thinness*. Like standing on a stage when somebody cuts the house lights and the microphones in the same second. The hum of his father's sea in his blood was still there, but it was far away and very faint, like a radio station fading out on a long drive.

The land beyond the gods.

*Varlet.* Minerva's voice was very close and very clear. At least *she* hadn't faded. *The Jade Court formation is centered on the dropship. It suppresses divine resonance and scrambles every carrier frequency I know of inside a two-hundred-meter radius. Outside that radius, the land itself is doing the rest. You will have perhaps forty percent of your usual output. Perhaps less.*

"Thalia?"

*Alive. On the ridge. Injured. Owl-Two is down. I cannot see her signs.* A pause. *The mortals are taking fire from the cultivators. Their commander has put a second company on the ground. They are firing at everyone.*

Percy was already running.

"Eleni. The mortals. Keep them alive."

"Milord, the Accord—"

"*Article Four says we don't fire on them.* It doesn't say we don't stand in front of them."

Eleni looked at him for half a second. Then she grinned, all teeth, and put her helmet on.

"Aye, milord."

She went down the slope toward the wrecked Chinook like a falling star.

---

Percy went up.

Forty percent of his usual output. He could feel it. He felt slow and heavy and stupid, like wading through deep water with his boots on. When he reached for the sea, it was far away. When he reached for the sky in his blood, the one Artemis's bond had been feeding for weeks, it was faint and flickering.

But there was snow.

There was so much snow. Kilometers of it. Meters deep. Drifted into the valleys, packed onto the ridgelines, blowing across the slope in long white ribbons. Fourteen thousand years in the future, Imperial cadets would be taught that the Battle of Alaska Hill was fought in the land beyond the gods, where divine power failed. That was true. They'd also be taught that the defense had been commanded by a son of Poseidon.

Nobody had ever told him how. But standing on that slope, with the wind screaming and the gunfire cracking overhead, Percy suddenly understood.

The gods were weak here. The water wasn't.

Snow was water. Ice was water. It didn't care whose domain it was in. It was just *there*, patient and endless and cold, and it had always listened to him. It always would.

He put both hands into the snow at his feet, and he *pulled.*

The slope rose.

It rose the way a sheet rises when you pull it off a bed. Tons of snow came up off the mountainside in a single white wave and stood up into the air, higher and higher, until it was a wall thirty meters tall running the whole length of the ridge between the southern slope and the crest. It sealed the paratroopers in the valley off from the cultivators on the heights. A cultivator's sword-light hit it from above and vanished into it like a pebble into a lake. A burst of rifle fire from below hit it from the other side and did the same.

Percy held it. It was the hardest thing he had ever done.

His arms shook. His vision went grey at the edges. Something hot ran out of his nose and over his lip and froze there. But the wall held, and the snowfield went quiet on both sides of it, and for three long seconds, nobody on the mountain was shooting at anybody.

*That,* Minerva said softly, *is going in the records.*

"Where's the Censor?"

*North crest. Directly above you. Beside the ring.*

Percy let go of the wall.

He didn't let it fall. He let it *freeze*. It locked solid with a crack like a glacier calving, a clear blue barrier running the length of the ridge. Then he went up the last ten meters of slope on a ramp of ice that rose under his feet as fast as he could climb it.

---

The crest of the ridge was chaos.

The dropship sat in the middle of it in its ring of glowing jade discs. The team was scattered around it in a rough, bleeding circle. Straban was down on one knee, holding a wall of wind up with both hands and swearing. Neumond crouched behind the dropship's cloaked hull with one wing hanging broken. Hrafn stood over him with his axe, swaying, bleeding from a dozen cuts. Oriel was on her feet with her rifle up and her face set like stone, and she was not firing, because the only targets in front of her were the ones the Accord said she could fire on. And those were winning.

Thalia stood at the south edge of the ring with Aegis on her broken arm, and her face turned toward the ice wall Percy had just built.

When she saw him come over the crest, her face did something complicated.

"*Kelp Head.*"

"Hey, Pinecone Face."

"You're *late*."

"You didn't call."

"*The phones are down, you idiot, that's the whole—*" She stopped. Behind her, in the snow, Dakota knelt beside a grey-armored body that wasn't moving. Thalia's jaw worked. "Owl-Two's gone," she said, much more quietly. "She took a sword for Dakota."

Percy looked at the body for a moment.

Then he looked north, across the jade ring, at the Censor.

---

Lu Xuanming, Censor of the Northern Dipper Office, stood on the northern crest with his ivory tablet held before his chest and his robes blowing in the wind. Around him, what was left of the Jade Court's cultivators had drawn back into a loose crescent. Percy counted twenty-two. There had been thirty-four, Minerva told him. The team had paid for every one of them.

The Censor was looking at Percy with an expression of mild, intense curiosity, the way a scholar looks at a rare book.

"So," he said. "The one who raises mountains out of snow."

"The one who raises walls," Percy said. "Mountains are harder."

"Mm." The Censor's eyes went to the ice wall along the ridge, then back to Percy. "You are a son of the Western Sea King. The Court has records of you. Not many. You are young, and the West is far away." He tilted his head. "Your power should not work here. This is the land beneath the Dipper. It belongs to no god. We have spent two days unfolding our own strength through a formation so that we could stand on it at all. And yet you walk up out of the snow."

"The snow likes me."

"Evidently."

"Tell your people to stand down," Percy said. "Nobody else has to die on this mountain today."

"The vessel—"

"You're not getting the vessel."

"You will forgive me," said the Censor, "if I do not take the word of a boy."

He lifted the ivory tablet.

Percy didn't know what it did. Minerva didn't either. She said so, very fast, in the back of his head. But he felt something change in the air around the Censor, a tightening, like the whole mountain drawing breath. And across the crest, the twenty-two remaining cultivators lifted their hands in the sword-sign in perfect unison.

Percy lifted his hands too. The snow on the crest stirred.

Then a raven landed on the Censor's tablet.

---

It was a big raven. Bigger than any raven had a right to be. It sat on the top edge of the ivory tablet with its claws hooked over it and its black feathers ruffling in the wind, and it looked the Censor of the Northern Dipper Office dead in the eye, and it said, very clearly, in a voice like a door creaking:

"*No.*"

The Censor went very still.

A second raven landed on the jade disc nearest Percy's feet. It looked up at Percy with one bright black eye, cocked its head, and made a small clicking sound. It sounded amused.

"Minerva," Percy said, very quietly. "What am I looking at."

*I do not know, Varlet.* Her voice had gone careful. *My sensors read two corvids. My sensors are also telling me that the local divine suppression field has just been overridden from a direction I cannot resolve. By something I cannot classify.*

Behind Percy, Hrafn let out a long, ragged breath and lowered his axe.

"They came," the big Trophimoi said hoarsely. "I told them. I *told* them."

"Told who?"

Hrafn didn't answer. He was looking past Percy's shoulder, toward the eastern end of the ridge.

Percy turned.

There was an old man standing there.

Nobody had seen him arrive. Percy would have sworn on the Styx that the eastern end of the ridge had been empty snow a moment ago. The old man was tall and gaunt, in a long grey cloak that snapped in the wind, and a wide-brimmed grey hat pulled down low over his face. He leaned on a spear. The spear was plain, grey-shafted, with a leaf-shaped iron head, and there was absolutely nothing remarkable about it, except that Percy couldn't make himself look at it for more than a second at a time.

Under the brim of the hat, the old man had only one eye.

He didn't come any closer. He just stood there, at the far end of the ridge, and looked at the Censor, and at the dropship, and at Percy. His single eye was the grey of old ice.

When he spoke, his voice carried without being raised. It was the kind of voice that sounded like it had been talking to the wind for a very long time.

"North of this ridge," the old man said, "is mine."

The Censor said nothing.

"South of it is his." The spear tilted a fraction, toward Percy. Or maybe toward the whole camp behind Percy, or Olympus, or the Empire, or something else. Percy couldn't tell. "Or it will be. In a while."

"And this ridge, Wanderer?" The Censor's voice was very soft. "This ridge upon which we stand?"

"Nobody's." The old man's mouth moved in something that might have been a smile. "You're standing on nobody's, Censor. That was always the agreement. You've been standing on it for two days with your little stones out. I let you. Now I'm tired of it."

The raven on the Censor's tablet shifted its feet and croaked.

For a long, long moment, nobody on the ridge moved.

Then Lu Xuanming, Censor of the Northern Dipper Office of the Celestial Court, lowered his ivory tablet. He bowed. It was a deep bow, precise and formal, from the waist. It was aimed at the old man first. Then, after a pause just long enough to be noticed, it was aimed at Percy.

"The Court acknowledges the old borders," he said. "The Court will withdraw."

He lifted one hand. Across the crest, the nine jade discs of the formation rose out of the snow, spun once and flew back into his sleeve. The moment the last one left the ground, Percy felt Thalia gasp beside him as her father's sky came crashing back into her head. Neumond's wings flickered to life. Somewhere in the dropship's cloaked hull, a systems panel chimed.

The Censor turned to go. Then he paused, and looked back.

"Son of the Western Sea King," he said. "Before I leave, allow me a single impoliteness. I would like to satisfy my curiosity."

Percy waited.

The Censor lifted his tablet again. Not as a weapon, this time. He turned it around, so that Percy could see its face. The ivory was covered in tiny, dense columns of characters, black ink, far too many to read, crawling and shifting across its surface as Percy watched. Names, he realized. Thousands of names. Tens of thousands.

"A copy of a copy of a page," said the Censor. "From the Register of Life and Death, kept by the Northern Dipper, which writes the day of every mortal death beneath heaven. And the days of demigods too, when they are born under our sky. When your people's vessel fell, the Register showed us names that do not exist yet. Soldiers who will be born in ages that have not come. Dying on dates that should be impossible. That is why the Court came." He tilted the tablet, looking at Percy over the top of it. "I searched it for yours, when you came over the crest. Out of curiosity."

"And?"

"You are not in it," said the Censor.

The wind moaned across the ridge.

"Every mortal is in the Register. Every demigod born beneath the sky. Every hero. Even those whose deaths are far away. The line is always there, even if the date is blank." He lowered the tablet. "Yours is not blank, son of the Western Sea King. Yours is *absent.* As if someone reached into the Register and took it out."

Percy didn't say anything. He couldn't think of anything to say.

*Khaos,* Minerva said quietly, in the back of his head.

"The Court will remember that," said the Censor. He bowed once more, very slightly. "Eleven years, the old records say. Until the war over this ground. We will meet again here, I think."

Then he stepped onto the cloud that had been waiting at his feet, and the Jade Court went north. Twenty-two cultivators on swords and clouds and one large, offended crane, streaming away over the peaks in the fading light until they were only specks.

When Percy turned back toward the eastern end of the ridge, the old man was gone.

The two ravens were still there. They looked at him for a moment longer. Then they lifted off together, heavily, and flew north after the old man who wasn't there anymore.

Hrafn watched them go with tears freezing on his face.

---

**THE SOUTHERN SLOPE — THE WRECK OF SUGAR BEAR ONE-TWO**

---

Captain Ruth Dalgaard had seen a wall of snow the size of an apartment block stand up out of a mountainside, hang in the air and freeze solid.

She had seen a woman in bronze armor come down a forty-degree slope faster than a snowmobile. The woman had walked into the middle of a firefight between a platoon of American paratroopers and a line of flying swordsmen, and she had simply *stood* there. Swords of light hit her armor and bounced. Rifle rounds hit her and fell into the snow, flattened. She stood between the wrecked Chinook and the ridge with her arms spread wide, and she shouted something in a language Dalgaard didn't know, over and over, until Dalgaard realized it was the same two words every time and that they were meant for her.

*Cease fire.*

Dalgaard had given the order. She was still not entirely sure why.

Now it was over. Or something was over. The shooting had stopped. The wall of ice ran along the ridgeline above them like the edge of a glacier. The swordsmen were gone, a string of specks streaming north over the peaks. And Dalgaard was sitting in the snow beside the wreck of Sugar Bear One-Two with a medic working on her arm. She was looking up at the boy who had just come walking down the ice ramp toward her.

He was a *kid.* That was the first thing. He was sixteen, maybe seventeen, with black hair and a torn orange T-shirt under a jacket that was nowhere near warm enough for the Brooks Range. There was frozen blood under his nose, and his eyes were a strange, sea-green color that she would remember for the rest of her life.

The girl in bronze armor stood beside him. She'd taken her helmet off. She was maybe the same age, with pale hair and a pink scar at her hairline. She was looking at Dalgaard with open, interested fascination, the way someone looks at an animal in a zoo.

Behind them came a tall, dark-haired girl with a spear, a shield with a hole in it and her arm in a sling. She looked like she hadn't slept in a week, and like she'd been hit by a truck.

The dark-haired girl crouched down in front of Dalgaard and snapped her fingers in front of her face.

The air shimmered.

Dalgaard felt it. That was the strangest part. She actually felt it, like a veil being drawn across her eyes, a gentle pressure that wanted her to see something else. A crashed Russian surveillance drone. A weather incident. A training accident. It was a very reasonable pressure, and it was very persuasive.

It didn't take.

It slid off her like rain off a window.

"Oh, *come on*," said the dark-haired girl.

"Doesn't work here," said the boy quietly. "Remember? Land beyond the gods. The Mist's thin up here."

"It worked on half her platoon."

"Yeah. Not on her." The boy crouched down beside the dark-haired girl and looked at Dalgaard. His eyes were very tired. "Hi. Sorry about this."

Dalgaard looked at him for a long moment.

"Who," she said, "the hell are you people."

The boy seemed to think about that.

"The people who won't be here tomorrow," he said at last. "That's the honest answer. We're going to take our aircraft and go home. You're never going to see us again. Probably." He hesitated. "I'm sorry about your guys. The ones on the helicopter. We tried to stop it."

"I saw." Dalgaard looked down the slope at the wreck, at the body bags her first sergeant was already laying out in the snow. Five of them so far. "The swordsmen. Who were they?"

"People who think this mountain belongs to them."

"And the old man? With the spear? I saw him, you know. On the ridge. Just for a second."

The boy's face did something complicated.

"Also people who think this mountain belongs to them," he said.

"Does it?"

"No. Nobody owns it. That's kind of the point." He stood up. Dalgaard noticed that his hand was wrapped in a bloody bandage, and that he swayed a little on his feet, and that the girl in bronze armor put out a hand to steady him without seeming to think about it. "Captain, in a few years, somebody's going to come up here and want to build something. When they do, and they ask you about today, and they will ask you, tell them the truth. Tell them what you saw."

"Nobody's going to believe me."

"Somebody will," the boy said. "Trust me."

Then they were walking away, the three of them, back up the ice ramp toward the ridge, where something Dalgaard's eyes insisted on seeing as a patch of empty air had just started to hum with engine noise.

Dalgaard watched them go.

After a while, First Sergeant Ivanoff came and sat down in the snow beside her.

"Ma'am," he said. "I'm going to write this up as a mid-air collision with an unidentified aircraft of foreign origin. Weather contributed. Enemy action unconfirmed."

"That's a lie, Sergeant."

"Yes, ma'am. You want to write it up the other way?"

Dalgaard thought about it.

"No," she said at last. "Write it your way." She looked back up at the ridge, at the wall of ice running along the crest, blue in the fading light. "But I'm keeping my own notes."

---

*Varlet,* Minerva said, as they climbed the ramp.

"Yeah?"

*I have run the mortal captain's name against my archive. Out of habit.* A pause. Something in her voice made Percy stop walking. *Dalgaard, Ruth. Currently a captain, Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 501st Infantry. In eleven years, she will be a colonel. She will command the mortal garrison of the Alaska Hill Strategic Complex. She will be the first mortal officer in history to serve under a demigod's command.*

Percy looked back down the slope.

Dalgaard was still sitting in the snow by the wreck. She was watching them go. Even from here, Percy could see the notebook in her good hand.

*She will be your second-in-command at Alaska Hill, Varlet.* Minerva was quiet for a moment. *She will die there.*

Percy stood on the ice ramp in the wind for a long time.

"Add it to the list," he said finally.

*Varlet—*

"I know," Percy said. "I know. Just add it."

---

**OBSCURUS ONE — THE DROPSHIP**

---

They loaded Owl-Two's body onto Kappa-Seven's dropship last.

Dakota carried her. He wouldn't let anybody else do it. He carried her up the ramp by himself, the Roman legionary with the Sciritae scout in his arms, and laid her down on the deck of the troop bay, and folded her hands on her chest. Then he sat down beside her on the bench and didn't get up.

Nobody asked him to.

"She said something to him," Thalia said quietly. She was standing next to Percy at the foot of the ramp, watching. "When she went down. I didn't hear what."

Percy didn't ask Dakota. It seemed like the kind of thing that belonged to him.

Straban was in the cockpit of Kappa-Seven's ship, running the preflight with his jaw set and the systems chiming around him. Neumond was strapped into the systems operator's cradle behind him with one wing splinted. Hrafn sat on the ramp with his axe across his knees and a raven feather tucked into the band of his helmet. None of them could remember seeing him pick it up. And over at Lambda-Two, Castellan Oriel stood by her own ramp with her helmet under her arm. She watched Percy walk over.

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"Your wall," Oriel said at last. "On the slope. That's going in my report."

"Minerva says it's going in the records."

"It *is* the records." Oriel's mouth twisted. "Every cadet in the Empire learns about the ice at Alaska Hill. The Wall of Obscurus. The son of Poseidon who held the southern slope against the Court for nine days. They teach it like a hymn." She looked away, north, toward the peaks where the Jade Court had gone. "They never said it was a kid with a nosebleed."

"Sorry to disappoint."

"You didn't." She was quiet for a while. "The Accord held."

"Yeah."

"Article Four held. My people didn't fire on the mortals. Not once. Not even when they were shooting at us." She said it flatly, as if she was reading it off a report. "Your Roman held the line with my scout. My scout died for your Roman. Your Hunter took a sword-cut meant for a mortal. The mortals' captain gave a cease-fire because Theodosia asked her to." She let out a long breath that fogged in the air between them. "I didn't think it would work."

"Neither did I."

"It worked today," Oriel said.

"Yeah," said Percy. "Today."

The Castellan looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something Percy wasn't expecting. She drew herself up, very straight, and raised her right fist to her chest. It was a slow and deliberate salute.

"Lord Imperator," she said.

It wasn't the way the Imperials had said it at the Big House, kneeling in the hallway with the Watchpoint's verdict still ringing in their helmets. It wasn't worship. It was a soldier saluting an officer.

Percy didn't know what to do with it. So he did the only thing he could think of. He saluted back.

---

The dropships lifted off the ridge at dusk. Straban flew Kappa-Seven's, and Oriel flew Lambda-Two, and they rose side by side out of the Brooks Range and turned south, cloaked and silent. Behind them, on the crest, the wall of ice ran along the ridgeline blue and gleaming. On the slope below it, the tiny figure of a mortal captain was still sitting in the snow by the wreck of her helicopter, writing in a notebook.

Percy sat on the troop bay floor of Lambda-Two with his back against the bulkhead, too tired to move. Eleni sat on one side of him with her helmet in her lap, already asleep. Thalia sat on the other side with Aegis on her knees, running her thumb around the edge of the bullet hole in its face.

"You promised her," Thalia said, not looking at him. "Didn't you. Artemis. You promised you'd come back."

"Yeah."

"She made me promise too." Thalia's thumb went around the hole again. "Before I left. She said, *bring yourself back, Lieutenant, whatever else happens.* Same words." She was quiet for a moment. "She's never said that to me before. Not in the whole time I've been with the Hunt."

Percy didn't say anything.

"I don't know what's going on with you two," Thalia said. "I don't think I want to know. But if you break her heart, Kelp Head, I will personally put an arrow through you, and I'll aim for something the Styx doesn't cover."

"It doesn't cover much anymore."

"Then it'll be easy."

Percy laughed. It hurt.

*Varlet.*

He almost didn't answer. He was so tired.

"Yeah."

*Something has come in on the echo carrier.* Minerva's voice was strange. Very quiet, and very careful. *From her. My sister. The other me. From the other end of the wound.*

Percy opened his eyes.

"What did she say?"

*It is a single line. She has not sent anything in days. I think it cost her a great deal to send even this.*

"Minerva."

*She says,* said Minerva, *"Sister. I may have found a road."*

The dropship hummed around him. Eleni murmured something in her sleep. Thalia's thumb had stopped moving on the edge of Aegis.

Far below, the Brooks Range slid away into the dark.

"Add it to the list," Percy said softly.

*Varlet,* said Minerva, *I think the list is becoming a book.*
