"I am Bianca Di'Angelo."

Nobody said anything for a while after that.

The wind said something. It came off the broken ridgeline in long, dry gusts that smelled of glass and old ash, and it tugged at the woman's hair and at the torn banners of the fallen soldiers and at Octavia's rad-cloak. Beyond that, the mountain was quiet. The Roman legacy stayed on one knee with his mouth open. The rest of his squad lay where they had fallen, some of them groaning and most of them not.

Octavia's hand had gone to her sword on its own. She made it stay there.

_Di Angelo._

She knew the name. Every girl who had ever taken the oath knew the name, because every girl who had ever taken the oath had walked the Wall on her first night in the Hunt and read the names on it by torchlight. Most of the names were carved and filled with silver. A few of them were not filled at all. Those were the ones the Lieutenant made you stop at. MISSING, the letters said, and underneath, in a newer hand: NOT DEAD. LEAVE SPACE.

Bianca di Angelo was the third of those.

Octavia had stood in front of that name fifteen years ago with a torch in her hand and wondered, the way every new Hunter wondered, what kind of girl got herself lost so thoroughly that the Lady of the Hunt couldn't find her.

She had not pictured this.

The woman in front of her looked barely older than Octavia had been on that night. Her skin had the color of paper left too long in a window. Her hair was black and long and very straight and it moved in the wind with a slowness that didn't match the gusts. She wore the rags of what had once been a Hunter's silver parka, patched over and over with Imperial webbing, cured hide and strips of something that might have been flag. Around her throat was a thin chain, and on the chain hung a small figurine of a god that Octavia didn't recognize, worn almost smooth.

And her eyes.

The blue fire had faded from them, but it had not gone. It sat in the back of her pupils like a pilot light. When she looked at you, you felt the look land.

She was looking at the Roman.

"You said _Olympian Empire_," Bianca said. Her voice was mild now. Courteous, even, the way a hostess is courteous to a guest who has tracked mud across her floor. "Say it again, please. I have not heard those words spoken aloud in a very long time, and I would like to be certain."

The Roman licked his lips. He was a big man, broad-faced, with a decurion's bars on his pauldron and a name stenciled beneath them: VENN. There was blood on his teeth.

"The Undying Olympian Empire," he said. "By the Répertoire du Savoir Athénien and the authority of the Twelve—"

"Thank you." She smiled at him. It was a nice smile. It made Octavia's skin crawl. "That will do."

"—you are ordered to stand down and submit to retrieval."

"Retrieval," Bianca repeated, as if she were tasting the word to see whether it had gone off. "Am I a parcel, Decurion Venn?"

"You're listed as an asset."

"Listed." Her head tilted. "By whom?"

Venn didn't answer. His eyes flicked, very briefly, past her shoulder.

Octavia followed the look.

She had taken the thing behind Bianca for a rock face. It wasn't. The summit ruin had been a building once, some pre-Abandonment observatory or monastery. Its walls had long since slumped into the snow, but its foundations went down into the mountain, and where they went down, the stone changed. It stopped being granite. It became something black and smooth and seamless, like obsidian that had been poured instead of cut, and set into it, taller than any of them, was a door.

It had no handle. It had no hinges that she could see. Worked into the lintel above it, in a relief so worn it was almost gone, was the shape of a helmet. A tall one, crested, with an empty face.

Asimov's scanner, dangling from his belt, had started to click.

It clicked the way a Geiger counter clicked in a reactor room. Octavia glanced down at it and saw that the lifesign display had simply given up. Where the door was, the screen showed nothing. Not rock. Not an empty room. Just nothing, a black wedge on the display that went down past the bottom of the scale and kept going.

"…Sis," Asimov whispered. "Sis, that's not a structure. The scan says it's a _hole_."

"I can see that it's a door, dumbass."

"No, I mean it goes _down._ It goes down forever. The depth reading isn't a number anymore."

Bianca did not look back at the door. She didn't need to. She stood in front of it the way a sentry stands in front of a gate, with her weight settled and her hands loose at her sides, and she had clearly been standing there for a long time.

"You came to open it," she said to Venn. It was not a question. "Your Répertoire sent a squad of children with bizarre contraptions to open a door that has been shut since before your Empire learned to walk, and you did not even ask what was on the other side."

"Orders don't come with explanations, ma'am."

"No. They never did." For a moment, something almost sympathetic crossed her face. "My brother used to say that. About the gods. _They never tell you why._ He was eleven, and he was right about everything."

She looked at Octavia, then.

It was the first time she had, really. Before, Octavia had been part of the landscape. Now the blue pilot light turned on her, and Octavia felt it the way you feel someone open a door behind you in a cold house. The woman's eyes went from Octavia's face to her hair, to the rad-cloak, to the sword, and then stopped on Octavia's left wrist, where the silver band she had never been able to make herself sell still sat under a layer of grime.

"Oh," Bianca said softly. "You're one of hers."

"Was," Octavia said. Her voice came out rough. "Was one of hers."

"Nobody is _was_, where she is concerned." Bianca's mouth curved. "Does the Lady still hunt?"

Octavia could have lied. She thought about it. Instead, because the blue light was on her and it felt a great deal like standing in front of the Wall again with a torch in her hand, she told the truth.

"She's having one of her nights," she said. "Most nights, lately."

Bianca laughed.

It was not a loud laugh, and it was not a kind one. It was the laugh of somebody who had been told, after a very long wait, that the joke was exactly as bad as she had suspected.

"Then she has become precisely what she swore she would never be," Bianca said. "She waits for a man."

---

Asimov chose that moment to do something stupid.

Octavia would think about it later, many times, and she would never be able to decide whether it was the stupidest thing he had ever done or merely the most Asimov. He had been easing his rifle off his shoulder the whole time Bianca talked. Octavia had seen him do it and assumed, because she was a reasonable person, that he was doing it to look like he had a rifle. Not to _use_ it.

He used it.

It was a good shot, too. He was a third-generation legacy of some minor Apollo line, and the one thing Apollo's blood never quite washed out of anyone was aim. The round took Bianca high in the chest, just under the collarbone, from twelve meters.

Bianca looked down at herself.

There was no hole.

There was a hole in the soldier lying three meters to her left.

He had been one of the ones groaning. He was a Neodamode, Octavia saw, from the graft scars on his neck, and he had been trying to crawl toward his rifle. The round went into him under the collarbone, exactly where it had gone into Bianca, and he made a small, surprised noise and lay down again. This time he didn't move.

Nobody breathed.

"That," said Bianca, in the tone of a schoolteacher, "was rude."

"I…" Asimov's rifle barrel was shaking. "I hit you. I _hit_ you, I saw it—"

"You did. It would have killed me. Thank you for the demonstration; I find that people listen better after one." She glanced at the dead Neodamode and something passed over her face that was not quite regret. "He was dying anyway. He would have lasted perhaps an hour. I am sorry he did not get his hour."

And then she did something that made Octavia's stomach turn over.

She breathed in.

It wasn't a normal breath. It was long and slow and deliberate, like a smoker's first drag, and as she drew it, the dead Neodamode _changed._ The color left his face all at once. Frost bloomed across his armor in feathery white fans. His cheeks sank. It happened in two seconds, and when it was over, he looked like a man who had been dead on this mountain for a year instead of a minute.

A faint wisp of something, pale and blue and shimmering, drifted from his mouth and across the gap to Bianca's lips, and she swallowed it.

Her color improved. Not much. A little.

"Holy _shit_," Asimov said.

"Asimov," Octavia said, very quietly, without taking her eyes off Bianca, "if you raise that rifle again, I will shoot you myself, and I will not miss either."

"Wise," said Bianca.

"What _are_ you?" The words were out before Octavia could stop them.

"Thirsty," Bianca said simply. "And tired. And very, very old. The mountain is poor feeding, Hunter. Nothing lives here anymore. Nothing dies here anymore, either, except fools like these, who come every few centuries with fresh orders and fresh blood." She nodded at the scatter of fallen Imperials. "These ones are the first in four hundred years. I confess I was glad to see them."

"You drink the dead," Octavia said.

"I drink what the dead leave behind. The life they were not finished with. It would only go to waste." Bianca's blue eyes turned back to Asimov. "And when something tries to kill me, I let it kill someone else instead. Someone near. I do not usually get to choose whom. Today I chose him, because he was nearly gone already, and because I did not want your friend to learn that lesson on the Hunter." She smiled at Octavia again. "I would have been sorry about that. I like Hunters."

The implication settled over the ridge like snow.

_Someone near._

Octavia counted without meaning to. Venn. Maybe twenty Imperials still breathing, scattered across the rubble. Asimov. Herself.

Every one of them was near.

"So," she said slowly. "Every time something hits you—"

"Someone else pays for it. Yes."

"And if there's nobody left to pay?"

"Then I suppose I pay for it myself." Bianca shrugged, a small, elegant motion, oddly girlish. "It has not happened yet."

---

Decurion Venn had been listening to all of this very carefully, and Octavia realized too late that he had not been listening because he was frightened.

He had been listening because he was stalling.

His left hand had gone under his body while Bianca talked. When it came out again, there was a detonator in it. A fat, ugly Imperial breaching trigger, red-banded, with the safety cage already flipped open. And Octavia's eyes, following the cable that ran from it across the snow, found the charges.

He had planted them before Bianca had put him down. Four breaching lances, driven into the black stone at the base of the door like tent stakes, and wired together in a diamond. Octavia recognized the pattern from a hundred Imperial sieges. Divine-grade shaped charges. The kind the Empire used to crack the shells of Titan-class fortresses.

"Asset retrieval failed," Venn said, loud and steady, for the benefit of whatever recorder was running in his helmet. "Proceeding to secondary objective. Gate Roof-One. Ave Imperator."

Bianca turned.

For the first time since Octavia had landed, she moved fast. She was _very_ fast, as fast as anything Octavia had ever seen outside the Lady herself. She was across the snow and at Venn's side before the first word of the salute had finished leaving his mouth, and her pale hand closed on his wrist.

Octavia was faster.

Not than Bianca. Than _Venn's thumb._ She had been watching that thumb since she saw the detonator, the way the Lieutenant had taught her to watch a hand on a trigger, and she was already moving when he started to press down. She wasn't going for the detonator. She was going for the cable.

Her sword came out of its sheath and down in one stroke, and the cable parted in the snow a meter from Venn's hand.

Venn pressed the trigger.

Three of the four lances were on the far side of the cut. They did nothing.

The fourth was on the near side, and it went off.

---

The sound was very small. That was the first thing Octavia would remember afterward. For a charge that could crack a Titan fortress, the sound was almost nothing, a short flat _crack_ like a knuckle. The flash was white and brief. The lance at the base of the door simply vanished, and the black stone around it shivered like the skin of a drum.

Then the door made a sound of its own.

It was low. Lower than hearing. Octavia felt it in her teeth and her knees and the soles of her feet, a long groan that seemed to come up out of the mountain's roots. The snow around the door's base leapt into the air and hung there, trembling. A single hairline fracture ran up the black stone from the scorch mark, thin as a hair, a pale bright line running a meter up the face of the door.

And through that line, something came out.

It wasn't light. It wasn't smoke. It was a _pressure_, a cold that was not a temperature, pouring out of the crack in a thin sheet and spreading across the ruin at knee height like dry ice. Where it touched the snow, the snow went grey. Where it touched the fallen Imperials, the ones who were still alive started to scream. Octavia's shield flickered. Every hair on her body stood on end. Her ears filled with a sound like a great crowd, very far away, all speaking at once in a language that she almost understood.

In her helmet, Lauren's voice came through, sharp with alarm. "Big sis, what did you _do?_ Every sensor I have just went red. There's an energy signature coming off that mountain that I can't even classify. It's not divine. It's…" A pause. "It's _under_ divine. It's like reading the bottom of a well."

Bianca stood very still beside the door.

She had let go of Venn. The decurion was on his back in the snow with his wrist bent at an angle that said it would never bend properly again. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the crack.

When she turned, the courteous hostess had gone. There was nothing left in her face but the blue fire, and it was not a pilot light anymore.

"Do you understand what you have done?" she said.

Her voice was quiet. That made it worse.

"You have opened a crack in a wall that has held since before your Empire drew its first breath. A small one. Very small." Her gaze went to the thin bright line in the stone. "But it does not need to be large. It only needs to be _open._ Everything below has been pressing its ear to this door for thirty thousand years, Decurion. Listening for a gap. They have just heard one."

Far below the mountain, very faintly, Octavia heard something. It might have been the wind in the ruin. It might have been rockfall.

It might have been something very large, a very long way down, lifting its head.

"Seal it," Octavia said hoarsely. "You're the warden, right? Seal it."

"I _am_ sealing it." Bianca had already turned back to the door. She laid both hands flat against the black stone on either side of the crack, and Octavia saw the effort go through her like a current. The blue fire flared. The pale seam in the stone dimmed, shrank, crawled closed a few centimeters from the top. The pressure spilling out across the ruin thinned. "It will take time. Time and a great deal of life that I do not have. I have been living on scraps, Hunter. You have no idea what this door costs."

Octavia looked around at the ruin. At the fallen soldiers, grey-faced and whimpering in the cold that was not a temperature.

At Venn.

"…Yeah," she said. "I think I have some idea."

---

That was when the sky began to fall.

It started with a light. A single white point, high overhead, brighter than any star. Octavia saw it through the clouds and knew exactly what it was a half-second before Lauren told her, because every Imperial auxiliary who had ever served a tour in a contested system knew exactly what that light was.

"_Kinetic launch!_" Lauren's voice cracked across the comm. "Big sis, the lattice is awake! The squad's distress beacons have been screaming since before you landed and something about that door just pushed every threat index in the Sol grid through the roof. I've got launches from three platforms. Four. Six. They're not warning shots. Rod impacts in forty seconds. You need to _move!_"

"Move _where?_ We're on a mountain!"

"_Anywhere that isn't the target!_"

Octavia looked up at the falling lights, then back down at the door, at the woman kneeling in front of it with both hands pressed to the black stone, and she understood where the target was.

Bianca didn't look up.

"Stay close to me," she said.

"Are you _insane?_ You're what they're shooting at!"

"Yes." Bianca's voice was calm. "And when they hit me, someone near me will pay for it. I would rather it was not you, Hunter. So stand where I can see you, and I will try very hard to choose."

"_Try?_"

"I told you. I do not usually get to choose."

Asimov made a noise like a stepped-on cat. Octavia grabbed him by the back of the collar and hauled him across the snow toward Bianca, because there was nowhere else to go, and because some very old instinct from the Hunt told her that when the sky was falling, you stood next to the most dangerous thing on the field and hoped it liked you.

The first rod hit.

Octavia had seen kinetic strikes before, from orbit, on training footage and once from a safe distance on a planet whose name she had since tried hard to forget. She had never been at the bottom of one. It was not like an explosion. It was like the world hiccupping. There was a flash, and a sound so big it wasn't a sound anymore, and the whole mountain _jumped._ Octavia's feet left the ground. The snow lifted off the ridge in a single white sheet.

The rod had hit fifty meters from the door. Bianca's hands had come off the stone, and she had flung one arm upward, and the mountain's shadow had stood up.

There was no other way to describe it. The long blue shadow of the Himalayas, the shadow that lay across the snowfields below the summit, _rose._ It peeled itself off the ground like a sheet off a bed and climbed into the sky in a black wall a kilometer high, and the next three rods hit it and disappeared into it without a sound, the way stones disappear into deep water.

The fourth rod came in from the side, below the shadow's edge.

It hit Bianca.

Octavia saw it. For a fraction of a second there was a white line from the sky to the woman's upraised arm, and then there was no line, and Bianca was still standing.

Twenty meters away, one of the wounded Imperials simply came apart.

There was no other word for it. One moment he was lying in the snow, grey-faced and moaning. The next, he wasn't there anymore, and where he had been was a crater three meters deep, smoking, with the snow flash-melted to glass around its rim.

Then another. A woman by the fallen banner. Gone.

Then another.

Every rod that got past the shadow found Bianca. And every rod that found Bianca landed on someone else instead. Octavia watched the ruin empty itself one soldier at a time. The Imperials who were awake enough to understand what was happening started screaming and crawling away from Bianca, dragging broken legs through the snow. It did no good. Distance didn't seem to matter. _Near_ seemed to mean something broader than she had hoped.

Venn was the eighth.

He was the last Imperial on the ridge who was still conscious. He'd managed to get himself up on one elbow, his ruined wrist tucked against his chest, and he was staring at Bianca with his mouth moving. Octavia never learned what he was saying. The rod came in at an angle through the gap in the shadow, and Bianca's body took it without so much as a flinch, and the decurion and the patch of mountain he was lying on vanished together.

After that, it was very quiet on the summit. It was quiet in the way it's quiet after something has run out.

Octavia looked around.

The ridge was a moonscape of fresh craters, glass-rimmed and smoking. There was no one left on it except the three of them. Herself, clutching Asimov by the collar. Asimov, white as the snow had been. And Bianca, standing in front of the black door with one arm raised and her hair drifting around her head in an invisible storm, untouched.

_Someone near._

There was no one near anymore. Except them.

"Lauren," Octavia said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flat and far away. "How many more launches?"

"Two more in the chain." Lauren's voice was quiet, too. "Then a sixty-second gap while the lattice reloads. I'm coming down. I'm coming down _now_. Asimov, you're paying for the rad-shields."

"_Anything,_" Asimov whispered. "Anything, Lauren, take the whole wedding fund."

The two remaining rods came in together.

Octavia saw them. Two white points, very close together, falling out of the clouds. Both were aimed at the woman in front of the door. Neither of them would be stopped by the shadow, because the shadow had begun to sag, fraying at its edges, falling back toward the snowfield it had come from. Bianca's raised arm was trembling now.

She had run out of Imperials.

Octavia understood, with perfect clarity, exactly who was near.

She thought about the Wall, and the torch, and a name with no silver in it. She thought about the Lieutenant, fifteen years younger, putting a hand on her shoulder. _We leave space for them,_ the Lieutenant had said. _Because the Lady doesn't give up on her girls. Not ever._

She let go of Asimov's collar, took one step forward and put herself directly between Bianca and her idiot brother.

If it was going to be one of them, it wasn't going to be him.

Bianca's eyes flicked to her.

Something moved across that pale face. Octavia could not have said what it was. Surprise, maybe. Or recognition. Or simply the look of someone who has been alone for a very long time, watching someone else choose to stand beside her.

"Ah," Bianca murmured. "_There_ she is."

Then she dropped her arm, turned to the black door, and pressed both palms flat against the crack.

The two rods hit.

Octavia didn't die.

She would never know how close it came. She felt something pass through her. It was cold, colder than the pressure pouring out of the door, a cold like the bottom of a lake, and for one heartbeat every nerve in her body told her that she had been struck by a falling mountain. Her knees went. The world went white.

Then it was gone, and she was on her hands and knees in the snow, retching, and she was alive.

Behind her, very softly, she heard the black door groan.

Octavia lifted her head. The rods had hit Bianca, and Bianca had given them to the only thing on the summit she was still allowed to give them to, which was _the door._ The impact energy had gone through her hands and into the crack, and the crack had closed. Not all the way. Not quite. But the pale seam was down to a thread now, a line no longer than a finger, and the pressure spilling out of it had thinned to a trickle, a cold breath on the air, barely there.

Bianca slid down the face of the door and sat in the snow with her back against it.

She looked, for the first time, like what the scanner had said she was. Something human-sized, with a very weak lifeforce, at the bottom of a hole at the end of the world.

"Well," she said faintly, to no one in particular. "That was expensive."

---

The _Fair Weather_ came down out of the clouds with its landing thrusters howling and every alarm on its hull screaming, and Lauren put it down on the summit with the ramp already open.

Octavia got Asimov up the ramp first, mostly by kicking him. Then she went back.

Bianca watched her come. She didn't get up.

"The lattice will reload in forty seconds," Octavia said. "They'll send more. They'll send more than rods."

"Yes."

"You can't hold the door against that."

"No." Bianca looked up at the sky. "But they will not aim at the door. They will aim at me. They always aim at me." Her mouth twitched. "Four hundred years since the last squad. I had forgotten how tiresome it is."

"So come with us."

The words surprised Octavia even as she said them. They surprised Bianca more. The blue light in her eyes went very still.

"I cannot leave the door."

"The door's closing, isn't it? You closed it. With a _kinetic strike._" Octavia heard herself laugh, a little hysterically. "If you stay here, they'll drop rods on you until there's nobody left near you to catch them, and then they'll drop them on you anyway. And then the door won't have a warden at all, will it?"

Bianca looked at her for a long moment.

"You are very like her," she said at last. "Your Lieutenant. She had the same unfortunate habit of being correct."

Then she held out one pale hand.

Octavia took it without thinking, and a cold like lake water went through her fingers and up her arm, and she understood with sudden, dreadful clarity that if Bianca di Angelo wanted to, she could drink Octavia dry in the time it took to blink.

Bianca only used the hand to pull herself up.

"Your ship," she said. "Take me off this mountain, Hunter. Not far. Somewhere I can see it."

"And the bounty?" said Asimov faintly, from the top of the ramp. Nobody had asked him. "Sis. _Sis._ Lauren says there's a bounty. AtRep just posted it."

Octavia didn't look at him. "How much?"

Asimov started laughing.

He laughed all the way into the cockpit. He was still laughing when Lauren, without a word, reached over and put the figure on the main screen in letters large enough for Octavia to read it from the ramp, and Octavia stopped halfway up and stood there looking at it for so long that the lattice nearly got its forty seconds.

It was more money than she had seen in her entire life.

It was more money than most _systems_ had seen in their entire lives.

ASSET: DI ANGELO, BIANCA. CLASSIFICATION: STRATEGIC. STATUS: HOSTILE. CAPTURE ALIVE. UNDER NO CIRCUMSTANCES KILL.

UNDER NO CIRCUMSTANCES STAND NEAR.

Bianca read it over Octavia's shoulder. She sounded amused.

"They have learned _something_, at least."

"Get on the ship," Octavia said.

---

Behind them, on the empty summit, the black door breathed out once, very softly.

The thread of pale light in its face had closed to almost nothing.

Almost.

And far down, in the place where the door led, where time ran soft and slow and the years were only stones stacked on stones, something that had been pressing its ear to the wall for thirty thousand years heard the gap, and smiled, and began to climb.

**LUNA PRIMARIS — THE HOLLOW — ORPHEUS'S DESCENT, DIG-FACE NINE**

**16231.5.1.3 | 03:40**

---

The dig had been going for thirty-one years, and in all that time the Order of the Hollow Crescent had never once stopped singing.

Mother Esk had been a girl when the first shaft was sunk. She had carried water to the diggers in a tin bucket and learned the hymns by listening to them come up out of the dark. Now she was old, and the shaft was four kilometers deep, and the hymns still came up out of it in the same slow, patient cadence, relay after relay of the faithful passing the verses up the ladders the way they passed the rubble.

_The tide returns. The tide returns._

She stood at the lip of Dig-Face Nine with her hands folded in her sleeves and listened to it, and she thought, as she thought every night, that it was the most beautiful sound in the world.

Below her, in the lamplight, the shaft fell away into the moon.

It was not a natural hole. The Order's founders had found it here, in the dark side of Luna, sealed under a plug of fused regolith that the Olympian survey had marked as a meteor scar and ignored. They had known better. The old texts called it Orpheus's Descent. Before the gods left Gaia, the texts said, the singer had found a crack in the world at the edge of the moon's shadow and walked down it looking for his wife. He had come back up alone. And the crack, disliking that it had been used and then abandoned, had closed itself behind him out of spite.

The Order had spent thirty-one years persuading it to open again.

Three hundred and six of the faithful had died in the shaft. Esk knew every name. The Hunters had come twice, and the Tideless fleets of the Director once, the night the Moonbound had risen in silverlight over the bunkers and stood between the Goddess and the guns. That night had cost the Order eleven ships and twelve hundred souls, and the survivors still spoke of it in whispers, because on that night they had _seen Him._ Not a shard. Not an echo. Him.

He had not come for them. He had come for her.

Esk did not like to think about that part.

"Mother."

She turned. One of the young Chorus-novices stood behind her, a boy of perhaps fifteen with the Crescent freshly tattooed in white ink across his shaven scalp. He was trembling.

"Mother Esk. The diggers at the face. They've stopped singing."

Esk looked down into the shaft.

He was right. Somewhere in the last few minutes, without her noticing, the hymn had fallen silent. For the first time in thirty-one years, the only sound coming up out of Orpheus's Descent was the wind.

And something else.

A dry, whispering, clicking sound, like a great many legs on stone. Coming up.

---

The thing that climbed out of Dig-Face Nine had been born in Tartarus, and it did not pretend otherwise.

It was long, longer than the lift cage it clung to, with a body like a woman's from the waist up, gaunt and grey and scaled at the shoulders, and below the waist it was something else entirely. It was a mass of coils and jointed limbs, dozens of them, scorpion-tailed and serpent-headed, all moving at once. Around its waist a belt of living vipers hissed and knotted themselves. Its face was beautiful, in the way a drowned face is beautiful. Its eyes were the color of old bruises.

The Chorus knew it. The Order had allies in the pit. It had always had allies in the pit, since the first Heralds had gone down the shallow shafts with offerings of salt and come back up with promises. The things in Tartarus hated the Olympians more than the faithful ever could. They hated the Empire that fought them in the trenches and the Watchpoints that kept them penned. And they had discovered, over many centuries of whispered bargaining, that the faithful of the Hollow Crescent did not care in the least what they were, so long as they spoke of Him.

This one was a Kampeid. A daughter of the brood of Kampe, the old jailer of the pit, who had held the keys to Tartarus when Kronos was young. Her kind still remembered every door.

The Chorus gathered at the lip of the shaft. There were nine of them tonight, the senior voices of the Order, robed in grey and white. They spoke, as the Chorus always spoke, together.

"_Daughter of the Coils. Thou art far from the Undertow._"

The Kampeid's head swung toward them. When she spoke, her voice came from several of her mouths at once, not quite in unison.

"_We come with news,_" she said. "_News of Him._"

The Chorus went very still.

"_Speak it._"

"_There is a hole in the pit,_" said the Kampeid. "_In the Twelfth Segment of the Tideless trenches, where your Empire feeds its children to our fathers. The wall tore. A floor gave way. The sound that came out of the Chorus then was not a word.

Esk had never liked the Arenberg twins.

She would never have said so. It would have been blasphemy. They were His half-blood, the children of His father's scattered seed. Their bloodline was the closest thing to Him that still walked in this age, and the Order's scripture was very clear on what was owed to His blood.

But she had never liked them. She had watched them, over the three years since they had come to the Order, the way you watch a fire someone has lit too close to the house.

It wasn't their violence. The Order was a violent faith. It had to be. Every Herald Esk had ever known had blood on their hands, and she had some on her own. It wasn't the stories out of the barracks either, the ones about Virre and what her squad ate on the nights the supply shuttles didn't come. Esk had heard worse about better people.

It was the _faith._

When the Order had sent them against Luna Secundus, they had walked through the Hunt's artillery singing, and when the Heralds had asked afterward how they had survived it, Orran had looked at them with honest confusion and said, _He was watching._

A faith like that was what the Order had prayed for since the Descent was found.

It frightened Esk more than the Hunters ever had.

Now the twins knelt on the lip of the shaft, side by side, with their faces lifted to the Kampeid, and they were weeping. Not the way the Chorus wept. The Chorus wept like people who have heard good news. The twins wept like people who have heard their names called.

"_He walks,_" said Orran.

"_He walks,_" said Virre.

"_Then we go to Him._"

"_Tonight._"

They said it together, the second half of each thought landing a breath after the first. The way they always spoke. Esk had long ago stopped being able to tell which of them started a sentence.

"_The road is not open,_" the Kampeid said. She did not sound displeased. She sounded interested, the way a cat is interested. "_The pit is wide, little half-bloods, but its doors are few, and the Tideless have nailed shut every one they know of._"

"_Then we find one they do not know._"

"_And if there is none?_"

Virre smiled up at the Kampeid through her tears.

"_Then we make one,_" said Orran.

"_Out of whoever is standing in front of it,_" said Virre.

**THE PRINCESS ANDROMEDA — LONG ISLAND SOUND, OFFSHORE**

The hold of the _Princess Andromeda_ had never smelled good. It had been a cruise ship once. It smelled of old diesel, older carpet and the particular musk of thirty monsters sleeping in close quarters. Luke Castellan had long since stopped noticing it.

He noticed the new smell.

It came up through the deck plating in the aft cargo bay, where Kronos's golden sarcophagus sat under guard, and it was cold and wet and faintly metallic, like a mine shaft after rain. The dracaenae on watch smelled it too. They backed away from the sarcophagus with their tails lashing and their tongues flickering. The hellhound in the corner put its head down on its paws and whined.

Something was climbing up out of the dark between the deck plates. Out of the place where the ship's hold touched, very thinly, the deep places of the world. Kronos's places.

Luke stood very still and let it come.

It was small. Smaller than he had expected. A thing the size of a large dog, grey-scaled, with the torso of a gaunt child and a nest of jointed legs underneath, and a single serpent-headed tail curled above its back like a scorpion's. It looked young. It looked, he thought with a chill, like it had been sent because it was expendable.

It had a voice like wet gravel.

"_Son of Hermes,_" it said. "_Captain of the golden lord's ship. We bring words from the faithful._"

"The faithful," Luke said. "Whose faithful?"

"_The faithful of a far age. Of the Hollow in the moon. They have no gods of your Olympus. They despise your Olympus._" The thing's tail twitched. "_They send us down, and we carry their words through the pit, to wherever the words must go. The pit has many floors, son of Hermes. On some of them, it is now. On some of them, it is very long from now. We climb between._"

Luke thought about that for a long moment.

He had been told, when he first swore himself to Kronos, that Tartarus was only a prison. A pit at the bottom of everything, where the old things were kept. He had come to understand since then that the old things had kept their own counsel down there for a very long time, and that _bottom_ was not a word they used in the same way he did.

"Say your words," he said.

"_An army of the gods' future children fell out of the pit onto the red star,_" the thing said. "_Tideless soldiers, from fourteen thousand years ahead of your day. They will come down to your Earth soon. They will fight your Olympians' children, because they do not know yet who is who. They will bleed. They will want to go home._"

Luke's breath had gone very slow.

"_The faithful say: the pit is the only road home, and your golden lord holds its keys. Offer the Tideless the road. Some will take it. Those who take it will owe your lord everything._"

"And what do _your_ faithful get out of that?"

The thing tilted its head. It looked almost pleased to be asked.

"_When the road is opened, the faithful will walk it. Down, and back. To the age where He walks._"

"He," Luke repeated.

"_The One Son. The Tidebreaker. The Gentle Storm. The Not-God-Who-Walked._" The child-face split in a grin that went too far back. "_You know Him, son of Hermes. You have fought Him. He is the son of the sea, and in the age of the faithful, the moon itself remembers His name._"

Luke stared at it.

Then, slowly, he began to laugh.

It wasn't a happy sound. It echoed off the cargo bay's steel walls and made the dracaenae hiss uneasily, and it went on for longer than he meant it to.

"_Jackson?_" he said, when he could speak. "You're telling me that fourteen thousand years from now, there are people who _pray_ to Percy Jackson?"

"_Pray,_" said the thing. "_Bleed. Wait._"

"Gods." Luke wiped his eyes. He was still half-laughing, and underneath the laughter there was something that wasn't amused at all. Something cold and considering. "Of course they do. Of course it's him. It's always him."

He looked down at the golden sarcophagus. For a moment, he thought he felt the thing inside it stir, the way a sleeper stirs at a familiar voice.

"Tell your faithful," he said, "that Lord Kronos hears them. Tell them the road will be offered. And tell them that if they want to come down it after the soldiers, they'll have to find their own door. Ours are spoken for."

"_The faithful will find a door,_" said the thing, and it sounded very sure. "_There are two among them who will find a door if they must dig it with their hands._"

It sank back between the deck plates, and the cold wet smell went with it.

Luke stood in the aft bay for a long time afterward, alone except for the hissing dracaenae and the whining hound and the patient golden coffin, thinking about armies falling out of the sky, and roads home, and a kid with a bronze sword who, somewhere down the long dark of the years, had become a god that people bled for.

_It's always him._

He went to find a map of Long Island.
