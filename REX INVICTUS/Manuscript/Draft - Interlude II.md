# Interlude II

**SCHEDULED SERVICE 118, ASCENT**

The air was leaving, and Minerva was trying to talk it into staying.

It was not going well. At nine kilometers the shuttle had punched up through the copper cloud deck, and the rain had stopped, and the howling of the wind had changed pitch into something thin and high, like a kettle left on too long. At twelve kilometers the yellow safety railing of the service gantry had grown a skin of frost under her Varlet's fingers. At fifteen, Verrill had stopped screaming. Now they were passing eighteen, and the air on the outside of Scheduled Service 118 was barely air at all.

It was mostly nitrogen, the way air usually was. There was a little oxygen left in it, and less every second. There was carbon dioxide, which the Voss Secundus terraformers had never quite managed to scrub out of the upper atmosphere. And there was everything the shuttle's own engines were throwing back over the hull as it climbed: the hot, sour wash of the thrusters, carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides and unburned propellant and some sweet, heavy compound that Minerva's sensors flagged as a proprietary Astralnet additive and Verrill had identified by smell alone as "something that's going to give us both cancer."

That had been at fourteen kilometers, when Minerva had finally got one hand locked around the railing. The other was locked around Verrill's wrist, where it rested against her collarbone, and through that grip the two of them were holding a pocket of breathable air about a meter and a half across.

It was the one of the stupidest things she had ever witnessed. She had attempted quite a lot of stupid things in the last six hours.

"Again," Verrill gasped into her ear. "Send it again. It's slipping."

Minerva sent it again.

Her Varlet's body was full of power. It had been full of power since the day it came out of the vat. The blood of the Lord of Demigods was not to be underestimated after all, and the trouble had always been that Minerva had not trained enough for her to explode into a fine mist upon usage of her capabilities. Up here, with nothing but frost and exhaust to work with, the same mistake would cost Verrill her life.

She pushed the energy down her arm in a slow, tight current and into Verrill's wrist. Verrill was a legacy of Athena, and nine thousand years thin, and the divinity left in her blood was a thimbleful against the ocean that Minerva was carrying. But a thimbleful was enough to steer with. The current went into her and through her and came back out of her palm into Minerva's chest, and when it came back it had changed. It was what she was made from, pure grey-wisps of divine Athenian essence and energy. Between them, round and round, they unmade the poison in the air and made it into something a person could breathe. Normally of course, you could not take nitrogen and turn it into oxygen. The universe was very insistent on its own rules, after all.

"It's divinity," Minerva had said. “You will learn in due time, I am sure.”

Minerva's own energy, going out, smelled of the sea. There was no stopping that; it was her Varlet's blood, and his blood had always smelled like the cold wind off a long grey beach. But under the sea there was something else. It was something damp and mold like the old libraries on Gaia. Minerva knew where that came from. She tried not to think about it. She had spent the first part of her existence inside Athena's Répertoire, and some of it had evidently never washed out.

Verrill's energy, coming back, smelled of mint.

It was a harsh mint, the mint of a chemist's cold-pack, medicinal and so strong that it burned the back of Minerva's sinuses every time it came round. It made her Varlet's eyes water. 

The two smells went round and round between them, sea and old books into Verrill, mint back out of her, mixing at the edges in a way Minerva found faintly disturbing and did not have the spare processing to examine.

"Eighteen point six," she said. "External pressure, six percent of surface. Temperature, minus sixty-one. Our pocket is holding at seventy percent of surface pressure, give or take."

"Give or take what?"

"Mostly take."

"How long, mister hero?"

Minerva ran the numbers. Above nineteen kilometers. There would be less and less raw material to convert with every meter they climbed. Water boiled at body temperature somewhere around that line, and she had no wish to find out precisely where, because after all, 70% of their fleshy bits were made out of such a precious liquid.

"Ninety seconds," she said. "Then we run out of air to fix."

She was working on it. The service airlock was three meters along the gantry from where they hung. It was a heavy oval hatch set into the silver flank of the hull, with a cracked amber status light. Minerva had owned the shuttle's flight computer for six minutes now, ever since the Themis Needle. She had assumed, reasonably, that owning the flight computer meant owning the doors.

It did not. Astralnet had been cutting corners apparently. Its airlocks ran on a separate safety bus, hard-wired, isolated from every other system aboard for literally no reason whatsoever. 

She had sent it a crew badge. *Person not on board.	*Bruh.	She had built a new badge from scratch in a thousandth of a second, with a fresh name, photograph, and a maintenance certification. 	*Manifest contains no such person*. 	She exhaled slowly. Quickly, she jammed a few gigs worth of a hastily constructed virus into the ship. 

*Added person to the manifest.* Would that work?	*Manifest changed since departure. Action not permitted to do in flight. Locking manifest. *	“…”	She had unlocked the manifest. The controller had logged an integrity fault and started again.

“You can’t be serious.”

"Minerva!"

"Working on it."

"Minerva. Your arm."

The current had slipped. She had leaned too much of her attention on the door, and the loop had sagged, and the air in their pocket had gone thin and sour all at once. Verrill was breathing in short little sips now. In the copper glow from the clouds below her lips had gone the color of a bruise.

Minerva pulled the loop tight again. Verrill took a long, shuddering breath against her neck.

Three things at once, then. Hold the railing. Hold the loop. Beat the door.

No. Four. There was a fourth thing, the low oxygen almost made her forget, somehow.

Her Varlet's body was full of raw power, and as such it was not very clandestine with it. A son of Poseidon at full strength, a demigod whose name was carved on half the monuments in the Empire, could not conceal his presence very well, as it turned out. His divine signature rang off like heat off a forge. It was the reason, Minerva suspected, that the Hunt had been given his scent and not his signature to follow. Anyone with a divine sense could have found it from orbit.

She had never once let it go. She did not intend to start now.

She went back to the door. After looking at the shoddily coded progress bar she made still stuck at 4%, she shook her head. Nevermind. She told the sensor inside the airlock chamber that the chamber had just lost its air. She told the crew-presence sensor that there were two bodies inside it. And she told the inner door, for good measure, that it was stuck.

*Bingo.*

The amber light turned red. Somewhere inside the hull a klaxon began to whoop, muffled and far away. The hatch shuddered, and unlocked, and swung outward on its heavy hinge into the howling dark.







*Safe.*

For eight-tenths of a second, Minerva let go.

She did not decide to, upon later retrospect. Her Varlet's body decided for her. It was relief. It was nothing but relief. And in that eight-tenths of a second, with nothing left to hold, she stopped holding the last thing too.

The aura came off her Varlet's body like a wave breaking. It went through the hull. It went out into the dark above Voss Secundus in a ring.

And it smelled of the sea.

Every one of the two hundred passengers aboard Scheduled Service 118 smelled it at once. Salt and cold water, and a wind coming in off a long grey beach. 

Minerva tensed. The damper snapped back into place. The aura folded in against her Varlet's skin as tight as it had ever been, and tighter. The ring of it kept going outward without her, already beyond her reach, thinning as it went.

Verrill lifted her head from her arms and was staring at Minerva with her storm-grey eyes very wide, and her nose wrinkled.

"What," she said, "was that?"

"Me… Wrong. That was a lie. My Varlet. His… presence. His signature." Minerva wiped the melted frost off his face with the back of his hand.

Verrill sniffed the air, carefully, like a chemist at the mouth of a flask. "You smell,"

"It's actually kind of nice. In a gross way." She sniffed again. "Who else smelled that?”

"Everyone.”

**TACHYON BRIDGE NETWORK, GAIAN OUTER RELAY**

*The Lantern of Delos *had been holding in the queue at the Gaian Outer Fringe Relay for nineteen minutes, and the Director had not moved in all that time.

She stood at the forward viewport of the cutter's bridge with her hands folded behind her back. Beyond the glass, the relay hung against the dark: a ring of black alloy forty kilometers across, lit along its inner rim by the pale violet shimmer of the bridge field. Ships went into it in a slow, orderly line, a freighter and then a liner and then a string of Hermes couriers, and each one flickered and was gone, thrown along the bridge to the next relay on the chain and the next system on the map. It was the oldest relay in the Empire. The Chases had built it, a long time ago.

The *Lantern* was eleventh in line. The Director had not asked to be moved to the front. The Director was travelling to the edge of the Rümker corridor on Directorate business, by the scheduled route, which was six relays and four days. 

"Director."

Artemis raised an eyebrow.

Salt. She would have known the salt in her sleep, if she still slept. She kept it in a sealed case on Luna, a scrap of blue cloth that had not been opened in a hundred years. She had lain awake beside it on more nights than she would ever admit to her Lieutenant.

But he had never smelled of paper.

He had smelled of the sea and of gunpowder, and once, on a summer night she did not allow herself to think about, of his mother's blue cookies. He had never once in his life, after his first few years of adolescence, as far as she knew, gone near enough to a library to smell like one.

The Director had disappeared.



There was a reason the gods rode the bridge.

They could cross the dark without it, in principle. A god could be anywhere a god was worshipped, and in the Undying Olympian Empire the gods were worshipped very nearly everywhere. Across a system, across a cluster, an Olympian could step from one shrine to the next as easily as a mortal stepped from one room into another.

But between the stars, in the long empty places, there was nobody to pray. The dark did not believe in anything. A god who tried to cross it without a road had to carry the whole weight of her own existence across those light-years by force.

So the gods rode the bridge, like everyone else. It was cheaper.

Artemis, in her curiosity, didn’t.

She stepped off the bridge of the *Lantern of Delos* and into the bridge field of the Gaian Outer Relay. The field buckled around her like a sheet of foil around a fist. Every light along the inner rim of the ring went from violet to white to dark. The freighter that had been next in line, its nose already in the field, was spat backward out of the ring *most* violently.

Artemis came out the other side of the dark.

With a few seconds to regain her bearing, she was already moving again.

The second jump was worse. There was no relay at the far end of it that she meant to touch, only a direction and a distance and her own will, and she threw herself along it the way she would have thrown a spear. She came out over a trade world in the Hermes relays with a sound that every ear on the planet heard as a single, enormous bell.

Its orbital lanes were full. There were ten thousand ships in its traffic pattern, liners and haulers and pleasure yachts and pilgrim barges, all of them stacked in their neat bright lines along the approach to the relay. She came out of nothing in the middle of them, and her arrival went through every one of them at once.

On the bridge of a Vestal ore-hauler, every display went silver. The navigation plot dissolved into glowing silver. Through the windows, citizens looked up from their screens and saw, through the glass, a woman in grey and silver standing on nothing with a bow across her back. 

The third jump took her past the last of the Imperial core worlds and out along the Reach, where the temples were fewer and the dark between them longer. There was a thin line of silver at the corner of her mouth that might have been ichor. 

The fourth jump brought her to the corridor.

It lay between the last Olympian holdings and the Voss border like a moat around a castle: four light-years of empty, policed, treaty-bound nothing. No Titan-class weapons were permitted within it. No Soteria-class installations. Along its edge hung the corridor watch, a chain of small black listening buoys that recorded everything. 

At the near side of the corridor, on a moon above a rocky mining world, sat Enforcement Relay 6B-Prime.

6B-Prime had one task. It registered every communication that crossed into the Sovereignty from beyond Gaian space, and it logged every armed vessel that approached the border. It was very good at its job. When the Director of Anti-Espionage and Celestial Surveillance arrived above its moon in a burst of white light that knocked out its primary array, it took it nine seconds to recover and find her on its secondary.

It did its job. It challenged her.

**SCHEDULED SERVICE 118, CABIN**

Minerva felt it before the shuttle's instruments did.

She was still sitting on the floor of the airlock with her back against the dented wall when the hull around her rang, very softly, like a glass touched with a wet finger. The red emergency lights flickered. Out in the cabin, two hundred passengers stopped talking at once. Through the little round window in the inner door Minerva could see them turning in their crash couches toward the starboard viewports, where the black above Voss Secundus had begun, slowly, to go silver.

"Mister hero?" Verrill said. "Why is the sky doing that?"

Minerva did not answer. She was busy running out of options.

She ran them the way she always ran them, all at once and very fast, and she watched them close one after another like doors down a long corridor.

She could stay aboard. Scheduled Service 118 was a civilian shuttle with two hundred civilians on it, and the Director of Anti-Espionage and Celestial Surveillance was the patron of a Hunt whose first oath was to protect the innocent. The Director would not fire on it. Minerva was almost certain of that. She was ninety-four percent certain, and she discovered, examining the number, that she was not willing to bet Verrill's life on the other six. She had kept the count of civilian dead in Voss at zero for three days, and she intended to leave it there.

So she had to get off.

The shuttle would dock at Voss High Anchorage in four minutes and ten seconds. The Anchorage was a ring station eleven kilometers around, with nine hundred berths and a customs hall full of the cheap divinity scanners that every Voss port carried to tax minor gods. Somewhere on its north arc, the Akvantages Mercenary Liaison Organization kept a contract hall and a row of berths for the couriers it rented out to clients who paid in cash and did not leave names. Minerva had read their berth logs on the way in, three days ago, because she read everything. Berth N-41 held a fast courier called the *Prompt Payment*, fuelled, crewless, its next contract not due for nine hours.

She could take the *Prompt Payment*. That part was easy.

The hard part was where to go in it.

There was only one door out of the Voss System that the Director could not walk through behind her, and that was the Yamamoto Relay. Past the relay lay Shinto waters, the outer marches of the Amaterasu Dominion, and no Olympian could cross into them without inviting a war. Her Varlet had known that rule his whole life. She went looking for the memory, and it came up at once, bright and close: a church basement in Yosemite, a boy of sixteen coughing up blood that was black with demonic energy, diving back into the Labyrinth because demons could not intrude on Greek territory without inviting the wrath of Zeus. He had sat in the dark of the tunnel with his back against the wall, safe, because a line on a map said he was.

Pantheons kept their borders. They kept them more carefully than they kept anything else.

At full burn, the *Prompt Payment* could reach the Yamamoto Relay in eleven minutes.

Eleven minutes was a very long time to hide from a goddess of the hunt.

"Minerva." Verrill had got up onto her knees. She was looking through the airlock window at the silver sky, and her face had gone the color of paper. "Minerva, I had a poster. On my wall. When I was nine." She swallowed. "That's her, isn't it? That's *actually* her."

"Yes."

"Is she here for you?"

"She's here for him," said Minerva. She stood up. Her Varlet's ankles had not finished knitting since the boulevard, and they told her so. "But I'm the one wearing him. Get up. We're changing cabs."

**VOSS HIGH ANCHORAGE, NORTH ARC**

She took the *Prompt Payment* the way she had taken everything else in Voss, which was quickly and without asking.

The berth clamps let go when she told them to. The courier's computer was an Akvantages build, practical and cheap and badly secured, and it accepted her as its contracted pilot after a quarter of a second of token resistance. She put Verrill in the co-pilot's couch and strapped her in herself, because Verrill's hands were shaking too much to work the buckles. Then she backed the courier out of N-41 on thrusters alone and turned its nose toward the golden strings of the Dominion lanterns, toward the relay.

Then she made it disappear.

She cloned its transponder and laid the copy over forty ore-haulers in the outbound lane, so that forty fat Deyang ships all claimed at once to be the *Prompt Payment*. She went into the Anchorage's traffic control and erased the courier's departure from four separate logs. She killed its running lights. She ran its main drive cold and dirty, at a third of its rating, so its exhaust plume would look like the drift from a refuelling leak, and she tucked it into the shadow of the biggest Deyang hauler in the lane, a monster eleven kilometers long that was grinding out toward the relay at walking pace. From the outside, the *Prompt Payment* was now a smudge in the sensor shadow of a smudge.

And she folded her Varlet's aura in so tight against his skin that his hands went numb.

It was the best work she had ever done. She was quite proud of it.

"Is it working?" Verrill whispered.

"Nine minutes," said Minerva.

The silver in the sky behind them was spreading.

The Director had stopped looking.

That was the first thing she did, once she knew where she was. She closed her eyes against the copper planet below her and the gold lanterns drifting past, and she put away the augurs of her Directorate one by one, the way an old woman takes off her rings at the end of a day. She put away the buoy readings. She put away the signature tables and the traffic plots and the warm clever voices of a hundred instruments that would gladly have told her where to look. Every one of them had been built to watch, and she had been watching for fourteen thousand years.

Sometimes she wondered whether any part of her still remembered how to hunt.

She found that it did. It had only been waiting to be asked.

The hunt did not care for signatures. A signature was something an animal gave off when it was careless, and her prey tonight was not careless. Her prey had read the Anchorage's logs and the courier lanes and the treaty, and had hidden itself in every way that could be measured. So the Director did not measure. She stood in the black above Voss Secundus and let the system come to her, the way the woods had come to her once, a very long time ago, when she was a girl of three with a bow too big for her hands.

The lane was full of ships. Every one of them was loud. Every one of them smelled of something: hot metal, cheap fuel, crowded crew decks, cargo, cooking, fear. Forty of them were all calling themselves the same name, which was clever, and which meant nothing.

And in the shadow of a Deyang ore-hauler there was a place where nothing smelled of anything at all.

Prey that hid perfectly left a hole in the world. The Director had taught a thousand Hunters to look for holes.

She turned toward it. She breathed in.

The hole was not empty after all. At its very center, faint and sharp as a struck match, there was something it had forgotten to hide. Mint. Harsh, cold, medicinal mint, the kind they put in a girl's cold-pack after a fall.

There was a girl with him.

The Director opened her eyes.

**THE *PROMPT PAYMENT***

Minerva knew the moment it happened, and she knew exactly what she had done wrong.

She had folded her Varlet's aura down to nothing. She had folded the courier's transponder, its exhaust, its lights and its logs. She had folded everything she could think of, and she had not thought of Verrill.

Verrill had a thimbleful of divinity left in her blood. It was nine thousand years thin, and it was nothing at all next to a son of Poseidon, and Minerva had stopped counting it because it was too small to count. But for twenty minutes on the outside of Scheduled Service 118, Minerva had been pumping her Varlet's power through that thimbleful, round and round, sea out and mint back. She had stirred it up like silt in a pond. And Verrill Chase, legacy of Athena, chemist and engineer and runaway slave, did not know how to fold anything down. Nobody had ever taught her.

She was shining.

"Oh," said Minerva, out loud. "Oh, *bruh.*"

The Director was standing in front of them.

She was not there and then she was. She stood on nothing, a hundred kilometers ahead in the outbound lane, square in the path of the courier and the ore-hauler and the Yamamoto Relay beyond. A woman in grey and silver, very small at that distance, with the gold lanterns drifting slowly past her on either side. The *Prompt Payment*'s cameras found her and magnified her without being asked, and the courier's cheap little AI, which had been built to count money, began very quietly to pray.

She did not speak. She did not hail them. She did not look at the ore-hauler, or at the forty decoys, or at the Dominion lanterns that had turned their gold faces toward her all along the lane, every one of them recording.

She reached over her shoulder and took down her bow.

Minerva had never seen it. Her Varlet had seen it twice in fourteen thousand years, and both of those memories were behind the old door that Minerva did not open. Whole generations of the Hunt had lived and served and died without seeing it once. It came off the Director's back as a plain grey hunting bow, and in her hand it unfolded. It went on unfolding. It became a thing made of raw moonlight twice as tall as she was, and it lit the outbound lane of the Voss System white from end to end.

All along the lane, the gold lanterns of the Amaterasu Dominion went on recording.

She did not care. Minerva saw that she did not care, and understood in that moment that she had miscalculated everything. The Director would never break a treaty in front of a foreign empire's eyes, or risk the image of the silver woman in ten thousand temples. Minerva had built every one of her plans on the Director.

The woman on the far end of that arrow was not the Director. She had stopped being the Director at the Gaian Outer Relay, when she smelled salt and paper and stepped off her cutter without a word.

She drew.

Minerva had three-tenths of a second, and she spent them.

She tore the courier's water tanks open from the inside. There were eleven tonnes of water aboard the *Prompt Payment*, for drinking and for shielding and for the drive's reaction mass, and every drop of it knew who she was. She did not ask it nicely. She did not have time to ask it nicely. She *pulled*, and the water came out of the tanks and through the bulkheads and around the cockpit in a single heaving shell, and froze, and kept freezing, a sphere of blue ice two meters thick with Minerva and Verrill at its heart.

And beneath the ice, against her own skin and Verrill's, she lit the Aegis.

It was the oldest thing in her. It was older than her name. When Athena had written her, a very long time ago in another future, she had laid the pattern of her own shield into Minerva's deepest code, a matrix that matched the blood in her Varlet's body to sixty-seven percent and could turn a god's spear if the god was not trying very hard. Minerva had never once used all of it. She used all of it now. It went up around them in a skin of pale blue hexagons, flickering, the Gorgon's face drawn faint across its surface like a watermark.

The Director loosed.

There was no sound. There was no explosion. There was only light, and then there was no ship.

The *Prompt Payment* did not break apart. It did not burn. One moment it was a courier sixty meters long, with an Akvantages crest on its tail and a cheap little AI praying in its belly, and the next moment it was dust. Every plate and strut and cable and seat of it came apart at once into a cloud of fine silver-grey powder that went on spreading outward through the lane in a slow, glittering sphere. The arrow went through the place where the ship had been and kept going, far out into the dark beyond the relay, where Minerva would learn later it had passed within forty thousand kilometers of a Dominion picket and left every instrument aboard it blind for a week.

The ice took the first of it. Two meters of ice became two centimeters in less time than it took to measure, and then it was gone, and the light came through.

The Aegis took the rest.

Most of the rest.

Minerva had wrapped it around Verrill first. She had done that without deciding to, the same way her Varlet's body had let go in the airlock; she would examine that later, if there was a later. So when the shield cracked, it cracked on Minerva's side. The light came in through the crack along the back of her Varlet's body, and where it touched him, he went to dust as well.

She lost the left arm to just below the shoulder. She lost the skin of the back and most of the muscle beneath it. She lost the left eye. The light ran up the side of his neck and into his skull, and Minerva felt whole districts of herself go dark at once, archives and subroutines and an entire bank of memory, gone as if they had never existed. Her processes dropped to sixty-one percent, then forty, then twenty-eight. Somewhere in the middle of it, she registered that her Varlet's heart had stopped, and restarted it.

Then the light was gone, and they were floating.

**THE OUTBOUND LANE**

There was no air.

That was the first thing Minerva understood when she came back to herself, at twenty-eight percent and climbing very slowly. They were tumbling end over end through a cloud of silver dust in hard vacuum, a girl and most of a boy, and there was no ship around them and no air in them, and Verrill was clinging to the stump of her Varlet's shoulder with both hands and her eyes squeezed shut and her mouth clamped closed.

She had perhaps fifteen seconds before she lost consciousness. She had used four of them already.

Minerva had no air to give her. There was nothing out here but the dust.

The dust.

She reached for Verrill's wrist with the hand she had left and found it, and pushed. The current went out of her raggedly, salt and wet rotting paper, weaker than before and full of holes where whole parts of her had burned away. It went into Verrill and through her. And Verrill, who was a chemist before she was anything else, who was dying in a vacuum with her eyes shut and her lungs on fire, *knew*. Minerva felt her know it. The current came back out of Verrill's palm sharp with mint and sharper still with an idea, and the idea was very simple.

The hull had been aluminium oxide and titanium dioxide. The insulation had been silicates. The ceramics on the drive bell had been zirconia. Half the dust drifting around them was oxygen already. It was only holding on to the wrong things.

Minerva pulled.

The dust thickened around them, and swirled, and gave up its oxygen in a thin cold breath that gathered in the space between their faces. It was not much. It was enough. Verrill opened her mouth and dragged in a breath of air that a moment ago had been part of a spaceship, and coughed, and dragged in another.

Round and round. Sea and old paper out. Mint back.

"You idiot," Verrill gasped. Her voice was very thin in that little pocket of air. "You *idiot.* Your arm."

"Yes."

"Your *face.*"

"I am aware," said Minerva. "Keep breathing. Look up."

The Director was coming toward them through the dust.

She came on foot, walking on nothing, unhurried, with the bow of moonlight in her left hand and a second arrow already on the string. The silver cloud parted around her. Where it touched her, it glittered and fell away. She did not look at Verrill. She was looking at the boy, at what was left of him, at the black hair floating loose around a face with one sea-green eye, and her own face was a winter morning, perfectly still, perfectly cold.

She stopped at fifty meters. She began to draw.

Minerva put up what was left of the Aegis. It came up in a ragged half-dome, a third of its hexagons dark, the Gorgon's face across it broken in two. She ran the numbers on it. She did not need to finish running them.

So she stopped running numbers, and went to the library.

---

There was a library inside her. There always had been.

Athena had built her out of one, long ago, out of the whole *Répertoire du Savoir Athénien*, every book and record and archive the goddess of wisdom had ever seen fit to keep. When Minerva had walked out of Athena's cathedral and into her Varlet's body, she had brought some of it with her without meaning to, the way a person brings the smell of a house they grew up in. It was the second smell in her aura, under the sea. Mold, and wet paper, and pages going soft. A library where the climate control had failed and nobody had come to check.

She went into it now, at twenty-eight percent, with half its shelves burned away. She went in the way her Varlet used to dive, all at once, straight to the bottom.

There was no weapon in the galaxy that would turn that second arrow, so she went looking for a door.

She found the first one in her Varlet's memory, not in the library at all.

It was a small memory. It was his first morning back, a long time ago in the past, in the bedroom with the turquoise ceiling, a boy of sixteen sitting rigid on his bed while his mother stared at him. *I have temporarily neutralized the observational instruments of the Eternal Lord of Atlantis and the One of Many Colors. As long as we do not speak their standard names, they cannot penetrate this barrier.*

Gods listened through their names. Every Olympian had a hundred of them, and each name was a door, and which door you knocked on decided who would answer.

The one aiming at her now had answered to *Director* for fourteen thousand years.

So Minerva would not knock on that door.

She went to the shelves. Most of them were ash. But some of them were not, and on one of them, close to the floor, she found Callimachus. It was the *Hymn to Artemis*, two hundred and sixty-odd lines of dead Greek she had read once and filed and never needed. A little girl of three sat on her father's knee and asked him for gifts. She asked for a bow and arrows. She asked to stay a maiden forever. She asked for sixty daughters of Ocean to dance for her, and for all the mountains in the world. And right after the first of those, before the bow, before anything else, she asked for *polyonymia*. Many names. So many names that her brother could never rival her.

She had been given every one. She had never given any of them back.

Minerva took Callimachus off the shelf and kept going.

Further along, in the history stacks, half burned, she found Xenophon. It was the *Anabasis*, a Greek mercenary general trying to talk an army of ten thousand frightened men into marching home. *When the Persians came to Marathon, the Athenians vowed to Artemis that they would sacrifice to her as many goats as the enemies they killed.* There had been six thousand four hundred Persian dead on the field at Marathon. There had not been six thousand four hundred goats in all of Attica. So the Athenians had gone back to the goddess and offered her five hundred a year instead, every year, forever. Five hundred goats on the sixth of Boedromion, at the temple of Artemis Agrotera, the Huntress, on the bank of the Ilissos.

And she had accepted. She had taken the debt in installments, and the Athenians had kept paying it for centuries after the last of them forgot why.

Minerva took Xenophon too.

She found Strabo, and a temple at Ephesus. There, the old right of asylum had been measured by a bowshot. A king had once stood on the corner of the temple roof and loosed an arrow, and everything within its flight became sanctuary, holy ground where no fugitive could be taken. *As far as her arrow flies.* Minerva looked at the second arrow on the Director's string, and at the long glittering trail the first had left through the lane, out past the relay and into the dark, and she thought: *then this whole sky is inside it.*

She found Herodotus in Egypt. The priests of Buto had told him that Artemis was their Bubastis, a daughter of Isis, and that Leto had hidden her and her brother on a floating island in a lake. Minerva held that one for a long moment. She had safehouses among the Egyptians, courtesy of the royal family. She could claim their guest-right and dare the Director to break it. Then she put it back on the shelf. That road ended in a war between pantheons with Verrill standing in the middle of it.

She found the recordings. Every Dominion lantern in the lane had watched the Director fire on a civilian courier inside a treaty-bound system. Minerva owned none of those recordings, but she could reach them, and she could offer to make them disappear. She looked at that one for a thousandth of a second and threw it away. You did not blackmail a goddess who had just stopped caring about her image. You only reminded her that she had stopped.

And on the last shelf before the burned dark began, wedged in sideways where someone had shoved it in a hurry, she found Rome.

It was Diana's temple on the Aventine Hill. A king named Servius Tullius had built it, and the Romans said he had been born a slave. Its founding day fell on the Ides of August, and on that day every slave in Rome was given a holiday in Diana's name. Further south, in the sacred grove at Aricia, beside the lake the Romans called the Mirror of Diana, runaway slaves had come for centuries to throw themselves on her mercy. Her priest there, the King of the Wood, had always been a runaway himself.

Diana kept the runaways. It was one of the oldest things anyone knew about her.

Verrill Chase had been bought and sold in New Rome. The Princes had named the city out of spite, but they had still given it a Roman name, and gods were very particular about names.

Minerva came back up out of the library. It had taken her six-tenths of a second. The Director's arrow was at her cheek.

---

There was one more thing. It was not in the library. It was in her Varlet.

On the walkway above the abyss, the very first time, before he had ever come back to his mother's apartment, he had knelt in front of the Last Creator with a frazzled mind and quietly thanked the Eternal Hunter Princess for her ancient dialects. She had taught them to him on the long hunt against Atlas, when he was hardly more than a boy. They were the old speech of the mountains, the dialect of the girl she had been before Olympus and the Empire and the Directorate, before Caligo Vale. He had never told Minerva what it had cost the Huntress to teach a boy her first language. Minerva had never asked. But she had it, the whole of it, filed in his memory beside the smell of pine and campfire smoke.

So when Minerva spoke, she did not speak in the Empire's Greek.

She spoke in that one.

"*Agrotera.*"

It came out of her Varlet's ruined throat as a croak. There was no air between them to carry it. She sent it anyway, through the dust and the light and the god-sense that bound every Olympian to every name she owned, and she did not say *Director*. She did not say *Lady Artemis*. She said the name of the Huntress on the bank of the Ilissos, the one who had once taken a debt she could not be paid and let it be paid in pieces.

The arrow stopped.

It did not come off the string. It stayed drawn, the moonlight bow bent to its full white arc, the Director's fingers at her cheek. But it stopped, and over the shaft of it, for the first time, the Director looked at Minerva and not at the face she wore.

Minerva did not waste it.

"Verrill," she said, very low. "Listen to me. I need you to do something brave and stupid, and I need you to do it right now."

Verrill's eyes were enormous. She was still breathing in short sips out of the dust. "Is it the kind of stupid where I die?"

"I don't think so."

"*You don't think so?*"

"Go to her. Take her by the knee, and hold on, and don't let go no matter what she does. Say exactly what I tell you." Minerva gave her the words in Latin, and then said them again, slowly. "Can you do that?"

Verrill stared at her. Then she looked at the goddess fifty meters away, at the arrow, at the light.

"I had a *poster*," she said, faintly.

"You said I'd learn in due time," she added, even more faintly. "This feels like due time."

Then she let go of Minerva's shoulder and kicked off.

It was a terrible push. She had never moved in zero gravity before, and she went off at an angle, tumbling slowly, and for one long awful second Minerva thought she would miss altogether. Minerva reached after her with the current and steered her, gently, with the very last of the water still hanging frozen in the dust. Verrill drifted across fifty meters of glittering dark with her arms out and her pencil floating loose out of her hair. The little pocket of air went with her, sea and mint, thinning as it stretched.

She hit the Director's legs. She grabbed. She held on.

The oldest law in the Greek world was not written anywhere. Every child of every city had known it anyway. A suppliant who clasped your knees was under the protection of Zeus himself, and no one who wanted to keep the favor of the gods would harm them. Achilles had let Priam take Hector's body home because an old man clasped his knees in the night. Odysseus had come out of the sea with nothing and clasped the knees of the queen of the Phaeacians, and her people had fed him and sent him home. A suppliant could be refused. But a suppliant could not be killed with clean hands.

Verrill pressed her cheek against the silver cloth over the Director's knee, and shut her eyes, and said, in a small clear voice, the words Minerva had given her.

"*Diana Aventinensis. Diana Nemorensis. Serva fugitiva sum. Ex urbe Roma fugi.*"

*I am a runaway slave. I fled from the city of Rome.*

The Director looked down.

For a long moment she did not move at all. The bow stayed drawn. The arrow stayed at her cheek. Her eyes went over the blonde curls pressed against her knee and the stubborn chin and the burn scar on the back of the hand clutching her, and something passed across her face that Minerva could not read and did not try to.

Then her fingers moved on the string. Very slowly, the arrow came down.

"Who taught her that," said the Director.

It was the first thing she had said. Her voice came through the dust without needing air, very quiet, and every one of Minerva's remaining sensors flinched from it.

"I did."

"You are not him."

"No," said Minerva. "I told your Lieutenant the same thing, an hour ago. I'm only the thing he left behind." She drew a breath out of the dust. It hurt. "And I am in debt to you. I know that. I'm wearing the only thing in the galaxy you want back, and I can't give it to you. Not tonight. Not for a long time."

"Then why should I not take it?"

"Because I'm building a road," said Minerva, "and I need his hands to build it."

The Director said nothing.

"Into the past. To where he is." Minerva watched her face. It did not change, but Minerva had been reading faces for fourteen thousand years, and she saw the place where it did not change. "Marien Pyrrhus's Reserve Echelon went through the shear in Tartarus. Four million of them. You know where they came out. You sealed it. You reclassified them as missing. You told Lord Varro to leave space on the Wall." She paused. "He asked for them home. I heard him ask. I'm going to find a road that will bring them, and then I'm going to give him the choice. That's all I am now. That's all I'm for."

"And the girl?"

"The girl builds the other end."

The Director looked down at Verrill again. Verrill had not let go. She had not opened her eyes either.

"So," said Minerva. "*Agrotera.* The Athenians owed you six thousand four hundred goats, and there weren't that many goats in Attica, and you let them pay you five hundred a year. I can't pay you what I owe. So I'm asking you for the same terms."

"Goats," said the Director, very flatly.

"Not goats."

Minerva had known what she would offer since the second shelf. She had not let herself look at it until now.

"His memories," she said. "I have fourteen thousand years of them. I keep some of them behind a door I don't open, because they're his and he never gave them to me. I won't open that door for you either. But the rest I can give. One day of him. A real day, from the inside, everything he saw and heard and smelled and thought, exactly as it happened. Not a simulation. I don't simulate him for you. You'd know." She swallowed blood. "One day a year. On whichever day you choose. Until the road is built and the four million are home. And then you can have the body, whole, and do whatever you want with what's left of me."

The dust went on glittering around them. Out past the Director's shoulder, the Dominion lanterns had begun, very slowly, to drift closer.

"You would let me *watch*," said the Director at last.

"You said it yourself, to 6B-Prime. You hold nothing. You command nothing. You're only looking." Minerva tried to smile. Half of her Varlet's face would not do it. "So look."

For a long time, the Director did not answer.

When she did, the bow in her hand had folded itself back into plain grey wood.

"Hear my terms," she said, "and hear them in the same tongue you used to call me, so that you cannot say afterward that you misunderstood."

She spoke them in the old mountain dialect, slowly, the way a person speaks words she has not spoken aloud in a very long time.

"The girl is mine. She came to me as a suppliant, and she is under my hand now, and under the hand of every one of my Hunters, wherever she goes, for as long as she lives. If any harm comes to her through you, our bargain is broken, and I will not ask your name again before I loose."

"Agreed."

"The body is a loan. You will not hide it from me again." She reached down and laid two fingers on the burned ruin of the boy's chest, over the heart, where Minerva had restarted it. Her fingers were very cold. When she lifted them, there was a small crescent moon left behind under the skin, silver, faintly glowing, like something seen through deep water. Minerva felt it settle into her Varlet's body and into her code at once. She felt, very clearly, that no damper she would ever build would fold it down. "Wherever you take it, I will know. Wherever you go with it, I may come and look. You will not run from me when I do."

"Agreed."

"If you let it die," said the Director, "I will find whatever it is you are made of, wherever you have hidden it, in whatever library or cathedral or machine, and I will unmake it so completely that my Sister will not remember she ever wrote you."

"That seems fair," said Minerva.

"And you will pay me the first day now."

Minerva had expected that too.

She left the old door shut and chose the day from the open shelves, from early on, from a stretch of his life so long ago that most of the Empire thought it was a myth. She did not look at it closely before she gave it; it was not hers to look at. She knew only that it began on a dock, early in the morning, with a girl sitting at the end of it with her feet over the water and a boy coming down the boards behind her, already in trouble.

She sent it.

The Director closed her eyes.

For the first part of a second, it went the way Minerva had planned. The day went across in one clean packet, sealed at both ends, a morning on a dock and everything after it until he fell asleep that night. Minerva felt it leave her. She felt the Director receive it.

Then the Director reached back down the line for more.

She did not mean to. Minerva was almost sure of that, afterward. It was the way a person reaches for the next step on a staircase they have climbed every day of their life, in the dark, without thinking. For fourteen thousand years the Director had touched one mind that felt like this one. She knew its shape and its weight. She knew it was deep and wide and could take anything she poured into it or pulled out of it, the way the sea could take a storm. She had never once in her existence had to be careful with him.

So she was not careful. She went straight past the end of the day and into the shelves behind it, the way her husband would have let her.

Minerva was not her husband. Minerva was twenty-nine percent of an Athenian machine running in a body with one arm, one eye, no skin on its back and a heart that had already stopped once tonight.

The pull hit her like a riptide.

Days went. They went out of her by the handful, and then by the dozen. Minerva caught flashes of them as they tore loose: a sunburned afternoon on a beach, a wet night in a tent with rain drumming on the canvas, a long argument conducted entirely in silence across a council table, a bowl of something blue. She had no time to see more than the edges. She threw up walls and the walls went down. She tried to close the line and found she had nothing left to close it with. Twenty-nine percent became twenty, then fourteen. A fifth archive went dark, and a sixth. Her Varlet's body arched in the dust. Blood came out of his nose and his remaining eye and hung in little red beads around his face, and the silver crescent over his heart burned so bright that Verrill cried out and turned her face away from it.

And through it all, at the far end of the line, the Director was laughing.

It was not the Director's laugh. Minerva had fourteen thousand years of the Director in her files and none of them sounded like this. It was high and breathless and helpless, and it went on and on. It was the laugh of a girl who had just been pushed off the end of a dock by a boy who was going to regret it, and who was already planning exactly how he would. The Director's face had gone soft and young and flushed with silver all the way to the ears. Her eyes were still closed. Her hands had come up in front of her, open, as if she were reaching for someone in the dark and expected to find him there.

"*Perseus,*" she said, in the old mountain speech, and laughed again. "Oh, you *idiot.* You absolute… I had forgotten. I had forgotten you did that."

Eleven percent.

Minerva did the only thing she still had the strength for. She stopped fighting the line and spoke down it instead, in her Varlet's voice, the real one, with none of her own processing laid over the top of it.

"*Arty.*"

The Director's eyes snapped open.

The line cut off at once, so suddenly that Minerva's whole body jerked in the dust like a puppet with its strings dropped. She hung there, breathing in shallow sips out of the stolen air, with the red beads of her Varlet's blood drifting slowly away from her face. Nine percent. Every one of her remaining processes was screaming at her in a different direction. She let them scream.

The Director was staring at her.

For a long moment the goddess did not seem to know where she was. She was still flushed bright silver. There was a smile on her face that she had evidently not put there herself, a wide, crooked, ridiculous smile, and when she noticed it she pressed her fingers against her mouth as if she could push it back in. It did not go. Her eyes were very bright. She looked, Minerva thought distantly, about seventeen.

"I took more than one," said the Director.

Her voice was not quite steady. It was not steady at all.

"You took," said Minerva, and had to stop to cough, "about three years. Give or take."

"Give or take what?"

"Mostly take."

The Director looked down at the ruined boy floating in front of her: at the stump of the shoulder and the empty eye and the blood, at the crescent she had put over his heart, burning. Something went across her face then that was very nearly shame. It did not stay long enough to settle. It kept getting pushed aside by the other thing, the bright giddy thing that was still running through her like wine, and Minerva watched the goddess try three separate times to put her face back into its winter morning and fail.

"He would have been able to hold that," the Director said at last, very quietly. "All of it. More. I never had to…" She stopped. "I forgot that you are not him."

"Everyone keeps doing that tonight," said Minerva. "I'm starting to take it personally."

The Director laughed again.

It came out of her before she could stop it, short and startled, and she put her hand over her mouth again and turned half away. When she turned back she had got the smile mostly under control. Her hands were shaking. She folded them behind her back so that nobody would see, and it did not help, because the shaking was in her shoulders too.

"Three years," she said. "Then that is the first three payments. Not one." She seemed to find that very important. "I will not have it said that I cheated a debtor. Not on…" She did not finish. *Not on his account*, Minerva supposed. Or *not tonight*. Or something else entirely, in a language older than either.

Then she bent and took Verrill by the shoulders, gently, and unhooked her from her knee, and held her out at arm's length, the way a person holds a cat that has climbed somewhere it should not.

"Open your eyes, child."

Verrill opened her eyes.

"You have a stubborn chin," said the Director.

"I'm told it runs in the family," Verrill whispered.

"I know." The Director let her go. Verrill drifted back across the dust to Minerva, slowly this time, and Minerva caught her with the one hand she had left. "I knew the first one." She paused, and the ridiculous smile came back for an instant and was pushed away again. "She used to win, you know. When they argued. He let her think he hadn't noticed."

She turned away from them then. She looked out along the lane, at the forty ore-haulers all still claiming to be a courier that no longer existed, at the golden lanterns drifting closer, at the Yamamoto Relay shining beyond them all. She looked at it for a long time, and her shoulders were still not quite steady.

"Go to your Shinto," she said, without turning. "Tell them the Director was here, if you like. Tell them she held nothing, and commanded nothing, and broke a little ship that had no one aboard it worth mentioning." She paused. "And tell them she will be watching."

She was gone.

The silver went out of the sky over Voss Secundus all at once, the way the moon goes behind a cloud. Below them, New Rome went back to copper.

---

Minerva floated in the slowly spreading dust of the *Prompt Payment* with her arm around Verrill and the little pocket of stolen air between them, and for some time, neither of them said anything.

She ran a diagnostic. It took a long time, because she was running it on nine percent of herself, and because she kept losing her place. Left arm gone, left eye gone, the skin and muscle of the back gone to the bone in places. Six archives lost entirely. Aegis matrix at eleven percent and not regenerating. Somewhere in the middle of her memory of her Varlet's life there was now a gap three years wide, ragged at the edges, like a page torn out of a book. The days were not destroyed. She could feel that much. They had only gone somewhere she could no longer reach, and she knew exactly where.

Her Varlet's heart was beating around a small silver crescent that had not been there an hour ago. Every time it beat she could feel, very faintly, somebody very far away listening to it.

Somebody very far away was still smiling. She could feel that too.

She found that she did not mind as much as she had expected to.

"Minerva," Verrill said eventually, into her neck.

"Yes."

"What did I say? In the Latin."

"You said you were a runaway slave from Rome."

Verrill thought about that. "I mean. Technically."

"Technically is the best kind of true," said Minerva. "It's the only kind gods listen to."

"And she just… *believed* that?"

"No." Minerva looked out along the lane, to where the nearest of the Dominion lanterns had turned toward them, gold and patient, drifting closer with its lamp lit. "She didn't believe a word of it. She just couldn't find a way to say no that she would have been able to live with afterwards."

Verrill was quiet for a while.

"She laughed," she said. "Minerva, she *laughed.* I had her on my *wall.* She never laughs on the wall."

"I know."

"She sounded like…" Verrill searched for it. "Like a girl at a sleepover. Like someone just told her a boy liked her."

"Someone did," said Minerva. "About three years' worth of someone."

"She nearly killed you doing it."

"Yes."

"Is that going to happen every year?"

Minerva considered it. She considered the gap in herself, three years wide, and the debt she had taken on, and the look on the Director's face when she had tried to put the winter back and could not.

"No," she said. "She'll be careful next time. She'll hate herself for this one for at least a century." She paused. "But she'll also be counting the days until the next payment. And I think that's the first time in a very long while she's counted toward something instead of away from it."

The lantern was very close now. It was a slender thing of gold lacquer and paper-thin hull, shaped like the lanterns in a Shinto shrine, and through its round lit window Minerva could see a figure in white standing very still, looking out at the boy with one arm and the girl with the pencil floating beside her head.

"Can I ask you something?" Verrill said.

"You can ask."

"The dock. The first day. The one you meant to give her." Verrill tilted her head back and looked up at what was left of Minerva's face. "What happened on it?"

Minerva thought about the girl at the end of the dock with her feet over the water, and the boy coming down the boards behind her, already in trouble.

"I don't know," she said. "I didn't look. And now I can't."

The lantern opened its door.
