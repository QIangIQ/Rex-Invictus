# Interlude: Minerva

*Draft. Future UOE point of view, directly after "The Wound." Minerva, in Perseus's form.*

**WATCHPOINT HADES, DEPTH 3**

**16231.4.3.2 | 09:40 (T+106 HOURS AFTER THE SHEAR)**

Athena did not like to be interrupted while she was thinking, and Minerva had been interrupting her for four days.

The Watchpoint at Depth 3 was not built for comfort. It was a cathedral of cold black stone carved into the wall of the pit, its nave filled from floor to vault with the cooling columns of the Répertoire's local core. The columns hummed. Frost grew on them in feathers. At the far end of the nave, where an altar would have stood in any other temple, there was a round table of grey marble, and the goddess of wisdom sat at it with her grey eyes fixed on a projection of the wound.

The projection turned slowly above the table. It showed the eleven-kilometer sphere, the ring of laboratories, the time-flow gradient rendered in bands of color from calm blue at the outer edge to violent white at the curve. Every few seconds a new set of figures from the survey scrolled up the side of it. Athena was reading all of them. She had been reading all of them for four days.

Minerva sat across from her with her boots on the table.

She wore her Varlet's face. She always did. It was the face she had woken up in, sea-green eyes and black hair and that expression of permanent mild trouble that no amount of processing power had ever quite managed to smooth out. The body was a shell grown to his specifications in a Hephaestean vat, but it moved the way he moved, and it slouched the way he slouched, and Athena had not once in four days managed to look at it without her mouth thinning.

"Your feet," said Athena, without looking up.

"Are on the table," Minerva agreed. "Your model has an error in the third band."

"It does not."

"It assumes the time gradient inside the wound continues the curve of the gradient outside it. It doesn't. It inverts at the boundary. That's why your reconstruction keeps producing negative values for the depth of the anchor. The anchor isn't at negative depth. It is at negative *date*."

Athena's stylus stopped moving.

For a long moment the only sound in the nave was the humming of the columns.

"Show me," said Athena.

Minerva took her feet off the table and reached into the projection. Her fingers left trails of neon blue through the colored bands. She pulled the third band out, turned it over like a strip of paper and laid it back down inverted, and the whole model shivered and rearranged itself. The negative values disappeared. In their place, a thin bright thread ran down from the center of the wound, down through the model and out of the bottom of the projection, into nothing.

"There," said Minerva. "That's where they went. Not down. Back."

"I know where they went. The Director's seal made that very clear." Athena's voice was very even. "I did not know the shape of the path."

"Now you do."

Athena studied the thread. "It's narrow."

"It's a needle's eye. The shear went through it once, with the whole weight of the Heart of Tartarus pushing behind it. Something at the Heart pulsed, and eleven kilometers of the Salient were squeezed down that thread like water through a pipe." Minerva sat back. "Your plan is to push something back up it."

"My plan," said Athena, "is to build a Soteria-scale anchor on the rim of the wound, synchronize it to the echo carrier and open the thread from this end. Then we send a relief force down to the reserve echelon and bring them home."

"Your plan won't work."

Athena finally looked up.

It was an unpleasant experience. It always was. Being looked at by Athena was like being read, and Minerva, who was at the most basic level a thing made to be read, found it more unpleasant than most.

"You have run it," said Athena.

"Eleven thousand, four hundred times since the second day. I didn't tell you, because you'd have asked me to stop." Minerva ticked the points off on her Varlet's fingers. "First. The thread was opened by the Heart. The Heart is a Primordial's organ. Your anchor is a machine. You can't push a machine up a road that a god's heart dug. It will jam halfway and you'll have a Soteria beacon stuck fourteen thousand years deep in the timeline, broadcasting anti-divine purity into an era where every god on Gaia is five orders of magnitude weaker than now. You'd sterilize the Mediterranean.

"Second. The echo carrier is not stable. It's a Watchpoint fragment on Mars, running on a fragmented core and a dead Helmsman's ship. If you synchronize to it and it drifts, your thread opens onto the wrong epoch. You might drop a relief force into the Bronze Age. You might drop it into the Abandonment. You might drop it into the inside of the Sun.

"Third." Minerva paused. "The Director sealed it. You would have to break her seal to do this. And you would have to tell the Council why."

Athena was quiet for a long time.

"And your conclusion?" she asked.

"The door can't be opened from this side. Not by us. Not with anything the Empire owns." Minerva shrugged. "It was opened by Tartarus. It can only be opened by something that already has a way into the past. Something that doesn't need to push, because it's been walking that road since before the Empire was born."

"There is nothing in the Empire that fits that description."

"No," said Minerva. "There isn't."

The goddess of wisdom looked at her across the marble table, and Minerva watched her understand.

"You mean the other pantheons."

"The Norse have the Norns at the root of their Tree, and the Norn called Urðr is the past itself. The Jade Court keeps a Book of Life and Death with every date of every mortal who ever lived written in it, and their judges walk between those dates to collect their dead. The Dominion's engineers rebuilt a relay that had been dark for millennia, and the coordinates Kagura Kuroyami gave their fleets during the war skipped past sensor nets the Répertoire still can't explain. The Egyptians sail a boat through the twelve hours of the night every night and come out at the dawn of the same day. All of them have roads. We have a hole."

"Olympus does not negotiate with foreign pantheons over matters of time," said Athena. Her voice had gone very cold.

"Olympus doesn't negotiate with foreign pantheons over anything. That's why we're here, digging holes." Minerva smiled her Varlet's crooked smile. "I'm not asking you to negotiate, Athena. I'm telling you why your beacon will fail. What I do about it is my own business."

"You are a construct of my Répertoire."

"I was," said Minerva. "Then I was a construct of your Répertoire that grew up inside a boy for fourteen thousand years. That makes me something else. You've never quite decided what."

Athena rose. The frost on the cooling columns crackled.

"If you go outside the Empire with this," said the goddess, "you do it without my sanction. Without the Répertoire. Without any authority at all."

"I know."

"And without his."

That one landed. Minerva let it.

Her Varlet was fourteen thousand years and a hole in the world away. His other Minerva rode inside his skull on the far side of the thread. That was her *other*, the one the echo carrier had been whispering to for four days while the Tochter scholars puzzled over the waveform and thought it was only a Watchpoint's traffic. Her other had made him a promise on a dock by a lake, with the moon on the water. *Next time, you decide.*

Minerva had the memory of that promise. The two of them had shared it across the carrier on the second night, in a single compressed burst that had cost her nearly four percent of the bandwidth. She intended to honor it.

She simply intended to have options first.

"He'd want them home," said Minerva. "All four million of them. He'd want it more than you do, Athena, and more than the Council does. He'd tear the pit open with his bare hands if he thought it would work. It won't. So I'll find something that will, and then I'll give him the choice." She stood and stretched her Varlet's arms over her head until his spine cracked. "That's what a good servant does. She brings her master options."

"You have never once in your existence been a good servant."

"That's true," Minerva agreed cheerfully. "I'm a very bad one. That's why I'm so useful."

**THE VOSS SYSTEM**

The Voss System was the only place in the galaxy where a thing like Minerva could go shopping for gods without a god knowing about it.

She reviewed the reasons on the way, because reviewing reasons was soothing, and because the courier she had borrowed from the Depth 3 dock was very fast and very small and there was nothing else to do in it but think.

The first reason was the Treaty of Mount Rumker.

Four years earlier, at the neutral ruins on the moon called the Tears of Lysandra, the War of Princes had ended in a draw that the Empire insisted on calling a victory. The Voss Sovereignty had kept its three stars, its three Dyson spheres and its autonomy, and in return it had signed away its right to expand for three centuries. The Empire had paid for the peace with mandates of its own. No Titan-class weapons within the four-light-year corridor between Olympian holdings and the Voss border. No Soteria-class installations there either. And, most importantly for Minerva's purposes, *no divine being, from any pantheon, may hold or command in military or executive governance within the Voss system.*

The Princes had insisted on that clause. It had been the whole point of their war. They had rebelled because the gods had diluted them, overruled them and ignored them. So they had made a system where no god could ever rule them again.

It had an unintended side effect. A system where no god could rule was also a system where no god could *watch*. Athena had no Watchpoint within the Voss border. The Répertoire could not issue verdicts there. The Director's surveillance nets stopped at the edge of the corridor, by treaty. The Voss Sovereignty ran on corporations, councils, legacies and money. For a being who had just walked out of Athena's cathedral without permission, it was the one place in the galaxy where nobody with a divine signature had the legal right to stop her.

The second reason was the Yamamoto Chokepoint Relay.

It was the relay that Kagura Kuroyami had reactivated on December's Eve of 16215, when the Shinto emissary had reached across the dark to a besieged Matriarch Voss and offered her an alliance. The Treaty of Mount Rumker had not closed it. The Empire had wanted it closed. The Amaterasu Dominion had politely pointed out that its fleets were still parked on the other side of it, and the Empire had decided, on reflection, that it could live with an open door.

So the Yamamoto Relay stayed open. It was now one of the most heavily trafficked relays in the galaxy, and the only public route between the Undying Olympian Empire and the Amaterasu Dominion. Everything that moved between the two empires moved through the Voss System: goods, pilgrims, spies and money. Where Shinto traffic flowed, everything else followed it. The Jade Court had never signed a treaty with Olympus in its life, but Deyang Foundries had built the drones that lit the Yamamoto Relay, and Deyang had kept offices in Voss ever since. The Akvantages Mercenary Liaison Organization, the North Sector's contract broker, took half its payroll in Norse kroner and half in Voss credits and recruited Einherjar veterans as freely as it recruited Imperial deserters. It kept a hall there too. Even the Egyptian houses, who trusted nobody, sent their merchant-priests to trade in Voss, because Voss was the only market in the galaxy where Olympus could not tax them.

The Treaty required all communications between Voss and non-Gaian pantheons to be registered through the Olympus Tertiae Enforcement Relay 6B-Prime.

Minerva had read the Enforcement Relay's traffic logs on the way in. They were very tidy and very complete, and they recorded exactly none of the conversations that mattered. Everyone in Voss knew how to talk around 6B-Prime. The ones who did it best did it through the cartels.

That was the third reason.

**VOSS SECUNDUS, NEW ROME**

**16231.4.4.1 | 22:15**

Voss Secundus had been a colony world when the War of Princes started, a rough, half-terraformed rock with a thin atmosphere and a population that Solon Themis had shipped in by the million. The war had made it rich. It had been the Sovereignty's forge and hospital and brothel for twelve years, and when the shooting stopped it had kept all three jobs and added a fourth, which was being the only place in the galaxy where an Imperial trooper on leave could sit at a card table beside a Shinto shrine-maiden, a Deyang engineer and an Akvantages mercenary and lose money to all three of them.

Its capital was called New Rome. The Princes had named it that out of spite.

Minerva brought the courier down through a sky the color of old copper. The upper atmosphere was thick with traffic: freighters stacked in holding patterns, Dominion lanterns drifting down from the relay in slow golden strings, a Deyang ore-hauler so large that it cast a shadow across three city districts. The city itself sprawled across a basin between two mountain ranges. It was a tangle of towers and arcologies and spilled-light boulevards, built without a plan by people who had never expected to live long enough to need one. In the middle of it, lit from below like a cake on a birthday, stood a colosseum twice the size of the one in Old Rome, with its name on the rim in letters taller than houses.

*COLOSSEUM GIGANTUS.*

"Tasteful," said Minerva, to nobody.

She set the courier down in a public dock on the east side of the basin, paid for three days' parking in cash, hired a ground car and drove north through the entertainment district with her Varlet's elbow out of the window. She was wearing her Varlet's second-best coat and carrying enough weapons to take a small moon.

She was going to see the Augusti.

Every gambling den, fighting pit and back-room exchange in New Rome paid tribute to one of four families. Three of them were ordinary criminals. The fourth was the Augusti, a Roman legacy clan whose patriarch had been a Second-Born demigod in the Princes' service and had come out of the war with two divine bloodlines and no visible side effects. The Augusti ran the casinos. They ran the fights. They ran, very quietly, the tables on the upper floors where the proxies of five pantheons met to do business that the Enforcement Relay would never hear about.

Their flagship establishment was a casino called the Bakuto.

Minerva parked the car around the corner, checked her Varlet's reflection in the window, adjusted his collar, adjusted it back and got out.

Talviento stared at her.

"Casualties," he repeated.

"Casualties," Minerva agreed. "Yours, theirs, bystanders, pets. I like to know the budget before I start. Otherwise I get carried away, and then people complain."

"We'd prefer none of ours," Talviento said slowly. "As many of theirs as it takes to make the point. No civvies. Augustus is very particular about civvies."

"How many is 'as many as it takes'?"

"How many would you usually—"

"Nine," said Minerva. "Nine is a good number. It sends a message without making the news." She thought about it. "Eleven, if they have a dog."

Talviento looked at her for a long moment. Then he signaled the bartender for a third beer, which Minerva noted he did not drink.

"Alright," he said. "Nine. And you'll get your girl."

"Yes. About that." Minerva set her teacup down very precisely in the center of its saucer. "I lied."

The bouncers in their jade-slab armor shifted. Talviento did not.

"About what?" he asked mildly.

"About the girl. I mean, I'm sure she exists. Everybody has a friend they're looking for. But I'm not here for her." Minerva turned on the barstool to face him properly. "I'm here for the upper floors."

Something changed in Talviento's face. It wasn't much, only a small tightening at the corners of the eyes behind the demon mask. But Minerva had spent fourteen thousand years reading one boy's face and nearly four days reading Athena's, and after that anybody else's was very large print.

"The upper floors are private," Talviento said.

"The upper floors," said Minerva, "are where the Dominion's attaché plays mahjong on Tuesdays with a Deyang procurement officer who isn't one, and loses on purpose. They're where an Arisawa Heavy Industries sales director with a Shinto shrine-seal on his cufflinks sits across from a Tian-Qiang buyer and haggles over Titan leg actuators that Beleth Mechanized Division swears it has never heard of. They're where Akvantages's hall-master drinks mead with a man who has a raven tattooed on each wrist and doesn't show up on any census, and where two Aldra liaisons sit in the corner pretending to be drunk and writing down everything the Akvantages man says, because Aldra belongs to Olympus and everyone knows it, and everyone lets them stay because it's cheaper than finding the next pair.

"They're where the Egyptian merchant-priests rent a room with no windows and burn incense that smells of a river that doesn't exist on this planet. They're where Astralnet's route-brokers sell safe passages through the Tachyon Bridge Network that aren't on any chart, and where Aspina Aggregates moves Hyperium and Eurobium by the tonne for anyone who can pay in a currency Olympus can't trace. They're where the Evergreen Family's lawyers meet Astralnet's lawyers every week to settle a scandal that officially never happened. There's a Blackstar Crucible deacon on the fourth floor who sells relics blessed by a god none of the other tables will name, and nobody asks where they're going.

"And there's a Rosenthal envoy up there who never gambles and never drinks, and who helped Matriarch Voss shake hands with the Dominion on December's Eve. She sits at the far end of every table and says almost nothing, and every other person up there watches her the way you'd watch the last card in the deck.

"And at the head of all of it, Augustus sits and takes ten percent of every conversation that the Enforcement Relay never hears." She smiled her Varlet's crooked smile. "I'd like a seat at that table."

Talviento was silent for a while.

Behind them, on the big screen above the sushi bar, the Colosseum Gigantus cut to a replay of the dinosaur eating somebody. The crowd in the casino roared.

"Who are you?" Talviento asked at last. "Really."

"Really? I'm a very old piece of software wearing a very handsome boy." Minerva tapped her Varlet's chest. "I used to belong to Athena. Now I belong to him. And he is on the other side of a hole in Tartarus that eats time, with four million of your cousins who don't know if they'll ever come home."

"Four million," said Talviento.

"The Pyrrhuiesian reserve. You heard about the Salient."

"Everybody heard about the Salient. They showed the Grace's detachment mustering on every screen in the city." He paused. "They said everyone in that sphere was dead."

"Everyone in that sphere is alive and fourteen thousand years in the past," said Minerva. "The Director sealed it. So forget I told you."

Talviento did not look like he was going to forget.

"And you want the pantheons," he said slowly, "because..."

"Because the Olympians can't get them back. They'd have to go down the hole, and the hole only goes one way. The Norse, the Jade Court, the Dominion and the Egyptians already have roads into the past, and they've had them since before the Empire was born." Minerva spread her Varlet's hands. "I don't want to borrow a road. I want to *buy* one. I want a way through time that doesn't run through Athena's Répertoire, or the Director's seal, or the Council's permission. A door that belongs to nobody on Olympus. And the only place in the galaxy I can shop for that without a god looking over my shoulder is here."

"Why?" Talviento asked. "If Olympus can't get them back, why would you go around Olympus to do it?"

It was a good question. Minerva gave it the answer she had given Athena, in a smaller voice.

"Because he'd want me to bring him the choice," she said. "And I'd like it to be a choice that nobody can take away from him."

For a long time, Talviento didn't say anything. The sushi chef slid a plate of something pink and glistening in front of him, and he ignored it.

Then he took off his demon-mask helmet.

Underneath it he was younger than Minerva had guessed. He had dark curly hair, a scar through one eyebrow and the tired eyes of someone who had grown up in a war. He set the helmet on the bar and rubbed his face with both hands.

"My girlfriend," he said. "The Chinese one. She's Deyang. Real Deyang, not the procurement kind. Her grandmother was on the drone crews that lit the Yamamoto Relay." He looked at Minerva. "She says there's a man in the Deyang office who isn't from Deyang. He came through the relay two years ago with Jade Court papers and a jade seal that nobody's allowed to touch. He keeps a book in a locked case. He never opens it in front of anyone. She says the case is the only thing in the office the security drones won't scan."

Minerva went very still.

"A book," she said.

"A book." Talviento put his helmet back on. The demon mask settled over his face, and his voice came out a little muffled. "Nine casualties. The Sump Rats in the north neighborhoods. No civvies, no dogs. You do that, and I'll take you up to the table myself. What you do once you're sitting there is your business." He stood. "But I'll tell you one thing for free, Parlamentum."

"Please do."

"Augustus will ask what you're offering in return. Everybody at that table pays. The Norse pay in blood, the Dominion pays in favors and the Jade Court pays in years." He looked down at her through the black eyes of the mask. "What are *you* going to pay with?"

Minerva thought about it.

She thought about four million names being read aloud over a no-man's-land, and a wound in the world where the rain fell upward. She thought about a boy on a dock by a lake who had made her promise to let him decide.

"Something I shouldn't," she said. "That's generally how these things go."

Talviento snorted and walked away through the ring of bouncers. After a moment, Minerva picked up her teacup again and finished her tea.

On the screen above the sushi bar, the dinosaur roared.

*Nine,* she thought. *Eleven if they have a dog.*

She went to find her weapons.
