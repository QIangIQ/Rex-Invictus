**VOSS SECUNDUS, NEW ROME, THE UNDERLANES**

**16231.4.4.2 | 01:30**

The bouncers gave her back her weapons at the door. That is, they gave her back most of them.

"The Folly stays," said the one who had found it. He was holding the little black cube in both hands, very carefully, the way a man holds a baby that might go off. "Boss's orders. You get it back when you come back."

"And if I don't come back?"

"Then I guess it's ours."

"Don't drop it," said Minerva, and walked out into the night whistling.

The Rattlebone Rustabouts held the north neighborhoods, which meant a district called the Kilns. It was eleven kilometers across the basin from the Bakuto, and every road into it was watched. Talviento had given her the addresses of nine marked targets on a data-slip, and a warning: the Rustabouts had eyes on every surface street, and any car with Augusti plates, or anything that looked like Augusti muscle, would be spotted before it crossed the canal.

So Minerva didn't take a surface street.

New Rome had been built fast and built badly, and underneath it ran the Underlanes. They were a knot of maglev tunnels and freight tubes and flooded service shafts, dug during the war to move munitions from the orbital lifts to the forges without exposing them to Imperial bombardment. When the war ended, nobody bothered to map them. The only people who knew them properly were the Underlane Regulars, a gang of former tunnel engineers and tram drivers who had simply kept running the trains after the Sovereignty stopped paying them. They charged tolls. They moved anything, for anyone, anywhere under the city, and they owed nothing to any of the four families.

Minerva found them in about nine minutes. It took her four of those minutes to locate the right service hatch, and five to talk her way down it.

"Kilns," she told the woman who met her at the bottom of the ladder.

The woman was old and scarred and wore a conductor's cap over a welding visor. She looked Minerva up and down, lingering on the rifle, the frags, the three knives and her Varlet's face.

"You're Augusti muscle."

"I'm freelance. The Augusti are a client."

"Same thing, in the Kilns." The conductor spat. "Rustabouts don't like Augusti. Rustabouts don't like us either. Took three of my trams last month. Took the drivers too." She squinted. "What are you going to do in the Kilns?"

"Nine things," said Minerva. "Maybe eleven."

The conductor thought about that for a while.

"Toll's forty drachmae," she said finally. "Or free, if one of those nine things is a Rustabout called Coil."

Minerva checked the data-slip. Target number six. *Coil. Bloodlines: Hephaestus, Hecate. Unstable.*

"Free it is," she said.

---

The tram was a war-surplus munitions hauler, black iron under a skin of rust, with a cattle-guard welded to its nose and the word *ESPERANZA* painted along its flank in someone's careful, faded handwriting. It ran on a single rail through the dark, its maglev coils humming, its headlamps throwing a narrow cone of yellow light a hundred meters ahead into the tunnel.

Minerva rode on the roof. The Regulars had offered her a seat inside, and she had declined, because the roof had a better view and more interesting air. She sat cross-legged with her Morrow across her knees and watched the tunnel walls slide past. They were covered in wartime graffiti: unit numbers, names, prayers to gods who had not been allowed in the system for four years, and one very detailed and anatomically improbable drawing of Solon Themis.

Her Varlet would have laughed at that.

She spent the ride running the targets.

The Rattlebone Rustabouts were forty-some divine junkies. Most of them were legacies who had injected a second bloodline from a black-market vat and survived it. The survival was the problem. A human body could not hold two divine lines for long without tearing itself apart, and the Rustabouts stayed alive on a drug called brain-ark, which stabilized them for a few hours at a time and made them loud and cruel and very hard to kill. They had moved into the Kilns six months ago. Since then, they had taken over two chemical plants, a Chem-Dyna warehouse, a hospital and the Regulars' trams.

The nine names on Talviento's slip were the ones who gave the orders. Kill them, and the rest would be forty junkies with no leader and no supply, which would make them the Augusti's problem to finish at their leisure.

Nine, Minerva thought. It was a clean number.

She checked her Varlet's body. Tendons, blood oxygen, the slow, patient tide of divine power that still ran through his veins even though he wasn't there to use it. The water in the tunnel walls answered her when she reached for it. She wasn't him. She had never been him. But she was *made* of him, and the sea still recognized its son's blood when it felt it.

"Kilns junction," called the conductor from the cab below. "Two minutes. This is as far as we go, freelance. Rustabouts own the platforms from here on."

"Thank you for the ride."

"Kill Coil," said the conductor, "and the ride's always free."

**THE KILNS**

**16231.4.4.2 | 02:04**

The Kilns had been a foundry district, and it still looked like one. Brick chimneys stood against a copper-colored night sky, and cooling towers and slag heaps rose between them. The Rustabouts had painted everything they owned with a bone-white skull, and they owned a great deal. The streets were empty. Every window was dark, except in the chemical plant at the center of the district, where every window was lit.

Minerva climbed out of a drainage shaft two streets from the plant and went to work.

*One.* A sentry on a rooftop, called Gristle, who had the bloodlines of Ares and Hermes and had been bragging over an open channel for the past ten minutes about how fast he was. He wasn't fast enough to outrun a MORROW-IX round from four hundred meters. The rifle was suppressed. His body fell into a rain barrel, and nobody heard it.

*Two* and *three* were a pair called the Kettle Twins. They were not actually twins. They shared a guard post at the plant's east gate and the same unfortunate mix of Apollo and Demeter, which had given them both glowing skin and a constant, faint smell of burned bread. They saw her coming. They even got their weapons up. It did not matter, because the water main under the gate burst at that moment and threw both of them forty meters into the side of a chimney.

Minerva stepped through the gate.

The alarm went up.

It turned out that forty-some divine junkies on brain-ark made a great deal of noise when alarmed. They poured out of the chemical plant in a flood, shrieking and howling, and every one of them was lit with a strange, doubled glow of two gods at once, colors that did not belong together, sparking and bleeding into each other. Fire and frost. Lightning and vine. One of them was on fire and did not seem to have noticed.

Minerva ran the numbers. She dropped a smoke, then a chaff-dissipator, and went into the cloud.

*Four* was the Rustabouts' quartermaster, a woman called Tinplate with the bloodlines of Hephaestus and Nemesis. She was waiting inside the smoke with a pair of cleavers that burned blue and an expression of perfect confidence, because Nemesis-blood meant she could see her enemy's next mistake before they made it.

Minerva didn't make one. She hadn't made a mistake in a fight since she was installed, and she had no intention of starting now.

*Five* was their chemist, a thin, twitchy man called Flask, who cooked the brain-ark and did not fight. He tried to run. She let him get to the loading dock and then shot him through the knee. After that she asked him three questions. He answered all three. The third answer was the location of the plant's cold-storage room, and the thing kept inside it.

Then she shot him again.

*Six* was Coil.

He came out of the plant's main hall on a platform of welded scrap that floated a meter above the ground on crackling violet lightning. Hecate-blood let him fly. Hephaestus-blood let him build the thing he flew on. He was huge and scarred and grinning, and the metal around his arms was flowing like mercury, reshaping itself every few seconds into blades and hooks and barrels.

"Augusti *dog*," he said. His voice was full of static. "Did the fat bastard send you alone?"

"Yes."

"*Just* you?"

"Yes."

Coil laughed. It came out of him as a sound like tearing metal. "Then you're dead, boy. Coil doesn't die."

"I have a note here that says you do," said Minerva, and tapped the data-slip in her pocket. "Also, the Underlane Regulars say hello. And they would like their trams back."

The fight lasted fourteen seconds. Most of that was Coil shouting.

At the end of it he lay in the rubble of his floating platform with a Greel-deflector buried in the plate over his chest, looking up at her with the slow, puzzled expression of a man who has just found out that he was wrong about something very basic.

"What," Coil said, "are you?"

Minerva knelt beside him.

"A very bad servant," she told him, "who's in a hurry."

*Seven*, *eight* and *nine* were the Rustabouts' three lieutenants. They had locked themselves into the plant's control room and were trying to set off the chemical tanks in the basement, on the principle that if they couldn't win, nobody would. It was a good plan, in its way. It would have leveled the Kilns and a third of New Rome.

Minerva opened the control room's door with the mochosian shaped charge, which leveled the door and nothing else. Then she went in and finished her count.

When she came out again, the rest of the Rustabouts were gone. They had scattered into the Kilns in the smoke and the dark, screaming about a ghost with a boy's face, and she let them go. The Augusti could finish them. They would have their leisure.

There was a dog.

It was a large dog, a mutant, heavy and grey and scarred, with two extra teeth on either side of its jaw and a faint glow in its eyes that suggested somebody had once experimented on it with something divine. It had come out of the plant during the fighting and sat on the steps, watching her with its head tilted. When she walked past, it stood up and followed her.

Minerva stopped. The dog stopped.

"I said eleven if they had a dog," Minerva told it. "I didn't say *which way* the numbers went."

The dog thumped its tail.

"Fine," said Minerva. "Nine."

**COLD STORAGE**

**16231.4.4.2 | 02:31**

The cold-storage room was in the sub-basement of the plant, behind a door with three locks and a hand-painted sign reading *DO NOT FEED*.

Flask had said *the Owl*. That was all he'd said: the Rustabouts had an Owl down there, they'd bought her from a slaver out of the Deyang docks three months ago, and she was the reason brain-ark stayed stable. She was the reason the Rustabouts had lasted six months instead of six weeks.

Minerva opened the door.

The room was cold and bright and full of equipment. There were centrifuges and gene-printers, and rows of reagent vats bubbling under blue light, and an old Everaude medical frame pulled apart and rebuilt into something the manufacturers wouldn't have recognized. There was a cot in the corner, and a bucket, and a bolt set into the wall with a chain running from it.

At the other end of the chain was a young woman sitting at a workbench.

She had her back to the door. She was bent over a microscope, her blonde hair tied up in a messy knot with a pencil stuck through it, and she didn't turn around when the door opened.

"If it's more brain-ark you want, you'll have to wait," she said. Her voice was crisp and dry and utterly unafraid. "The last batch is still in the centrifuge. And if you touch it before it's done, it'll give you seizures, and I'll laugh."

"I don't want brain-ark," said Minerva.

The woman went still.

Then she turned around on the stool.

Minerva had fourteen thousand years of her Varlet's memories. Most of them were filed carefully, indexed and cross-referenced, a library she walked through whenever she needed something. Some of them she did not walk through at all. Some of them she kept behind a very old door, because they belonged to him and they were his, and she had always believed that there were a few things even a Superintelligence should not read without asking.

The face in front of her was one of those memories.

She had curly blonde hair and a narrow, sharp face and a stubborn chin. Her eyes were grey, storm-grey, the grey of a sky before rain. They were the eyes of someone who had been calculating the distance to the door, the weight of the chain and the probability of every possible escape from the moment she heard the lock turn. She had a smudge of reagent on her cheek and a burn scar on the back of one hand. She was perhaps twenty.

She looked like Annabeth Chase.

She didn't look like a descendant, or a family resemblance, or a legacy who had inherited the hair or the chin. She looked like *Annabeth*. She looked like the girl on the hill, in the orange shirt, with the knife, on the first day, when a boy with a Minotaur's horn in his hand had looked up and seen her for the very first time.

Minerva stood in the doorway and said nothing at all for a full three seconds.

For her, that was a very long time.

The young woman was staring at her too. Her grey eyes had gone very wide. Her mouth had fallen slightly open, and her hand had drifted up to the pencil in her hair as though she wanted something to hold.

"You," she said, very quietly. "You're... Is this a *joke*? Did the Rustabouts do this? Is this some kind of brain-ark thing?"

"No."

"You look exactly like..."

"I know," said Minerva. "So do you."

---

Her name was Verrill Chase. She was a daughter of a legacy line of Athena, the old Chase line, and it had not produced a face like hers in fourteen thousand years. Her family were Owls, the engineering guild that had built half the relays in the northern sectors. She had been working for a Deyang contract in the docks when a slaver crew grabbed her off the street, drugged her, shipped her across the basin and sold her to a gang of junkies who needed a genius to keep them alive.

She told Minerva this very quickly while Minerva picked the lock on the chain. Then she stopped talking and watched Minerva's hands.

"You're not a legacy," she said.

"No."

"You move like..." She frowned. "You move like software."

"That's very perceptive."

"What are you?"

"I've been asked that four times tonight," said Minerva. "I'm starting to think I should have cards made."

The lock opened. Verrill rubbed her wrist where the iron had been, looked around the room one last time and walked straight to the centrifuge. She pulled out the vial of brain-ark she had been cooking and dropped it on the floor. She stepped on it, carefully, until it cracked. Then she went to the gene-printer and wiped its memory with four quick keystrokes. She took the pencil out of her hair, snapped it in half and dropped the pieces into the bubbling vats.

"There," she said. "Now they'll be dead in a week."

"You could have done that any time in three months."

"They'd have killed me." Verrill straightened her shirt. "I was waiting for somebody to open the door."

Minerva looked at her for a moment. Then, despite herself, she smiled her Varlet's crooked smile.

"Of course you were," she said.

**NEW ROME, THE VIA HEROUM**

**16231.4.4.2 | 03:15**

They came out of the Kilns on foot through the smoke, with the grey mutant dog trotting behind them. At the canal bridge, Minerva flagged down an automated cab. It was a battered yellow thing with a cracked canopy and a driver-intelligence so old it was still speaking with a pre-war accent.

"Where to, friends?"

"The Bakuto."

"Ah, a lucky night, is it? And is the dog coming?"

Minerva looked at the dog. The dog looked at Minerva.

"Apparently," she said.

"Extra five drachmae for the dog. Hop in, hop in."

They got in. The dog climbed in after them and lay across both their feet with a satisfied groan. The cab pulled away from the bridge into the empty streets, heading south across the basin.

For a while, neither of them spoke.

Verrill was watching Minerva out of the corner of her eye. Minerva was watching Verrill out of the corner of hers. Each of them had noticed the other doing it, and neither of them had stopped.

"You know," said Verrill at last, "you really do look exactly like him."

"Like who?"

"Don't." Verrill rolled her eyes, and Minerva felt something in her chest turn over, because it was *exactly* the right roll of the eyes. "You know who. The Blessed King. Perseus. He's on everything. Coins. Stamps. Recruitment posters. My grandmother had a tea towel." She paused. "You even stand like him in the statues. Like you're about to make a bad joke."

"I am frequently about to make a bad joke."

"And I look like her." Verrill looked out of the window. "I've known that my whole life. The family has the old photographs. From Gaia, before the Abandonment. There's one of her standing on a porch with a stupid Yankees cap. My mother used to put it on the table next to my school portrait so people could compare." She smiled a little. "The Architect. Annabeth Chase. They named a whole class of cruisers after her."

"The AnnChase pattern."

"The AnnChase pattern." Verrill laughed. "Every time I go through a dock, somebody makes that joke."

The cab turned a corner onto a wide boulevard. On either side stood great plinths of white stone, and on every plinth there was a statue.

This was the Via Heroum. The Princes had built it after the war, as part of their spite. They had torn down every statue of every god in New Rome, every Zeus, every Athena, every Ares, and dumped them in the canal. In their place they had put up heroes: mortals, demigods, legacies, the children of the gods rather than the gods themselves. Jason with the Fleece over his shoulder. Hippolyta with her belt. Achilles, Atalanta, Heracles with his club. They stretched down the boulevard for two kilometers, lit from below in pale gold, looking down on the empty street.

At the far end of the Via, at the center of a great circular plaza, stood the tallest of them all.

It was a pair of figures. A boy and a girl, perhaps seventeen, standing back to back, weapons drawn. He held a leaf-bladed sword and she held a bronze knife. They were looking in opposite directions, as though each was guarding the other against something coming from the dark. The boy's hair was falling in his eyes. The girl's hair was pulled back in a knot. The sculptor had given them both the same expression, a sort of tired, stubborn half-smile, as though they had fought this fight a hundred times and fully expected to fight it a hundred more.

The cab slowed as it circled the plaza. Its old driver-intelligence cleared its throat.

"On your left, friends," it said, in the proud voice of a tour guide, "the Monument of the Two. Perseus the Blessed King and Annabeth the Architect, defenders of Gaia, at the Battle of Manhattan. The largest bronze in the Sovereignty. It's said that if a couple passes it at night and..." The driver-intelligence paused. Its camera swung around to look into the back seat. It looked at Minerva. Then at Verrill. Then back at Minerva.

"*Oh*," it said.

There was a long silence in the cab.

"Oh, *my*," said the driver-intelligence.

"Eyes on the road," said Minerva.

"Yes, yes, of course. It's only... You'll forgive me. Twelve years I've driven this plaza. I've never had..." The camera swung between them again. "Would you two like a photograph? I have a camera. No extra charge."

"*No*," said Minerva and Verrill at exactly the same time.

They looked at each other.

Verrill's mouth twitched. Minerva's did too. Then Verrill put her hand over her face and started to laugh, helplessly, her shoulders shaking. It was a tired laugh, too loud, the laugh of someone who had spent three months chained to a wall and had finally, for the first time, been allowed to stop being afraid.

The dog lifted its head from their feet, looked at her with deep concern and put it back down again.

Minerva watched the statue slide past the window. She watched the boy and the girl standing back to back, fourteen thousand years after the battle they were fighting, and she opened a door in her memory that she had kept closed for a very long time. She did not read what was behind it. She only looked. Then she closed it again.

*He would want to know she exists,* she thought. *He would want to know there is still someone in the galaxy with her face.*

*And he would want to know that she walked out of that room on her own feet and broke every vial on the way.*

"You're doing it," Verrill said, wiping her eyes.

"Doing what?"

"The thing. The face. He does it in every statue. Like he's thinking about something very far away."

"I am thinking about something very far away," said Minerva.

"What?"

Minerva turned from the window.

"How long it would take," she said, "to get from here to the other side of a hole in Tartarus. And whether I could bring someone along."

Verrill stopped laughing.

She looked at Minerva for a long moment with those storm-grey eyes, and Minerva watched her calculate. She watched her think it all the way through, the way the girl on the hill always had, through the distance and the weight and the probability of every possible outcome, faster than any mortal had a right to.

"You're not talking about a trip," Verrill said slowly.

"No."

"You're talking about the Salient. The shear." Her eyes narrowed. "The four million."

"You heard about that?"

"Everyone heard about that." Verrill folded her arms. "You're going to try to open it. From the outside. With something the Empire doesn't have."

"I'm going to try to *buy* it," Minerva said. "Tomorrow night. At a very exclusive table."

The cab rolled on through the empty streets. Behind them, the Monument of the Two dwindled into the dark.

"The Owls build relays," Verrill said, after a while. "Tachyon relays. My family built half the ones in the north. Nobody in the galaxy knows more about bridging two places that shouldn't be connected." She paused. "If you're buying a road into the past, you're going to need somebody to build the other end of it."

"Are you offering?"

"You broke my chain." Verrill shrugged. "And you look like him. It seems unlucky to say no."

Minerva thought about her Varlet on a dock by a lake, with the moon on the water, asking her to let him decide. She thought about Athena's grey eyes across a marble table, and about four million names being read into the dark over a hole where the rain fell upward.

Then she thought about the girl on the hill in the orange shirt, who had never once in her life waited for someone else to decide for her.

"Alright," said Minerva. "But I'm warning you now. The people at that table are going to take one look at the two of us and think it's a con."

"Let them," said Verrill Chase. She leaned back in her seat, and her grey eyes gleamed in the passing streetlights. "The best cons always look like one."

The dog, lying across their feet, let out a long contented sigh and went to sleep.
