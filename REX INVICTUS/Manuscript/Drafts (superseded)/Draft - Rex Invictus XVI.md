# Rex Invictus XVI

Primus Pilus Tullia Ardens of the Legio XII Fulminata had held ground in the Salient for nineteen years, and she had never seen the enemy stop coming.
They had stopped now.
She stood on the lip of the forward trench with her helmet off and her face bare to the stinking air, and she looked out across the no-man's-land at the place where the miles of trench line were.
Where the staging ground had been, there was a bite taken out of the world. It was a perfect sphere, eleven kilometers in radius, and half of it was missing from the ground and half from the sky. The edge of it ran across the plain in a curve so clean it looked drawn with a compass. On one side of the curve lay the churned black mud of the Salient, cratered, wired and strewn with the husks of things that had been killed and would come back. On the other side there was nothing at all. There was no mud there, or air, or fog. There was a smooth, dark, glassy surface that curved away down into the earth like the inside of a bowl, and it reflected nothing.
Things were wrong at the edge of it.
Rain fell upward there. The Lethe fog, which had crawled across this plain for as long as there had been a Salient, reached the curve and stopped as if it had touched a wall, then folded back on itself and crawled away in the opposite direction. There had been a trench line running across the anchor's perimeter. Half of that trench was still there. The other half was gone, cut off clean along the curve, and in the cut face of the earth Tullia could see sandbags sliced in half and duckboards sliced in half and, once, a soldier's boot sliced in half with the foot still inside it.
The Fulminata had lost no one in the shear. They had been four kilometers back, holding the second line, when it happened. They had watched it. They had watched two cohorts of Pyrrhuiesian reserve, a carrier, part of its escort wing and a Waves of Poseidon Senior Commander vanish into a sphere of nothing in the time it took to draw a breath.
Then they had watched the monsters stop.
That was the part that had kept Tullia awake for thirty-one hours. In nineteen years, the monsters had never stopped. They came over the Phlegethon ridges every hour of every day, drakons and hellhounds and things with too many mouths, empousai and Laistrygonians and the endlessly reforming husks of giants. The Salient was a machine for killing them, and it ran without rest.
For thirty-one hours, they had not come near the wound.
They gathered at the edges of the no-man's-land instead. They crouched in the fog on the far ridges, thousands of them, and they watched the place where the anchor had been, and they did not approach. Tullia had never once seen a monster of Tartarus show fear.
She was looking at thousands of them showing it now.
"Primus Pilus."
Her signifer, a lanky legacy of Zeus named Lucan Corvo, came up the duckboards behind her with a data slate. His face was grey. Everyone's face was grey.
"Speak."
"Relay from Sector Command. We're to hold this line. No advance. No patrols past the three-kilometer mark. Nobody goes near the edge."
"We weren't planning to."
"There's more." He hesitated. "Command says something's coming through the Relay. Something big. We're to expect a detachment within the hour, and we're to hold the perimeter for it no matter what comes over the ridge."
Tullia turned.
"What kind of detachment?"
Corvo looked down at his slate and then back up at her with the expression of a man who had read something twice and still did not believe it.
"The Grace, Primus Pilus," he said. "They're sending the Aphrodite's Grace."

The sky of Tartarus is not a sky.
Every legionary in the Salient knew that. There was no sun above the pit. There were no stars. There was a vault of darkness so high and so heavy that it pressed down on the soul like a hand, lit from below by the burning red glow of the Phlegethon and streaked with rivers of fire that ran upside down across the ceiling of the world. The Empire's fleets hung in that darkness the way lamps hang in a cellar. They were small and bright and very alone.
At the forty-minute mark, the darkness above the Salient began to shake.
It started as a hum, low enough to feel in the teeth before it could be heard. Then light began to bleed through the vault. It was pale gold and rose pink and it came through in seams, like light through the cracks of a door, spreading across the ceiling of Tartarus in a slow, widening web. The fire-rivers on the vault flickered and bent around it. The monsters on the far ridges threw back their heads and howled, every one of them at once, a sound like the whole pit screaming.
Then the vault split open, and the fleet came through.
The corvettes came first. They were Vanguard-pattern, thousands of them, pouring out of the seam of light in a stream so dense it looked like a river of silver sparks. Each one was a hundred meters of armored wedge with a bronze ram on its prow and its flanks painted with the white-and-gold chevrons of the Aphrodite Detachment. They fanned out across the Salient's sky in widening rings, drives flaring, and began to settle into a picket that stretched from horizon to horizon.
After them came the Mastodons. The light frigates were blunt and heavy and slab-sided, built like siege towers with engines on their backs, and they came in blocks of fifty, holding formation so tightly that their shield bubbles overlapped into a single rippling wall of light. The Hammerheads followed them in a slower column, with their broad hammer-shaped prows and their flanks bristling with turrets.
Then the cruisers came.
Tullia had seen AnnChase-pattern cruisers before, but never this many. There were two hundred and fifty of them, and each one was the size of a city. They were built in the old style, back when the Forge Cohorts had printed out when the Empire was young and warships were akin to a floating brick of armor and guns. Their hulls were rectangular slabs of bronze-green armor twelve kilometers long, ribbed with buttresses as thick as mountains. Colonnades ran along their dorsal spines, rows of fluted columns a hundred meters tall holding up nothing but the dark. At their prows, where a ship of the ancient world would have carried a figurehead, each cruiser carried a statue. There was Athena with her spear, Ares with his shield, Apollo with his bow and Hermes with his wings spread, all of them carved in weathered stone and a kilometer high, staring forward into Tartarus with blank, pitiless eyes.
Their broadside batteries were already turning toward the ridges.
The dreadnoughts came after the cruisers.
There were forty of them. They were Perseus-pattern, and they were not ships so much as fortresses that had learned to fly. Each one was a stepped ziggurat of armor thirty kilometers long, built in terraces, with each terrace ringed by gun towers shaped like caryatids, stone women holding up the weight of cannon barrels instead of temple roofs. Their cannons ran down their keels in single lines, each barrel wide enough to drive a Mastodon through. Their engines were not at their sterns. They ran along their undersides in long glowing channels, like the fire-rivers of the Phlegethon flowing backward, and wherever they passed over the ground the mud of the Salient steamed and cracked.
Nobody said anything as the dreadnoughts took station, one after another, in a great ring around the wound. Nobody said anything as their cannons swung slowly outward toward the ridges where the monsters crouched. There was nothing to say. There was only the sound of the engines, a low endless thunder that shook the duckboards under their feet and made the mud in the trench walls tremble and run.
Then the Aphrodite's Grace came through the seam.
Tullia had been told it was thirty-five kilometers long. 
It came out of the light slowly, prow first, the way a continent might come out of the sea. It was white. After the bronze and green and grey of the rest of the fleet, the whiteness of it was almost painful. It was the white of polished marble, veined with gold, and it glowed very faintly from within, the way a lamp glows through alabaster. Its prow was a single enormous statue. It showed a woman rising out of a scallop shell, her arms lifted, her hair streaming back along the length of the hull in waves of carved marble that became the ship's dorsal armor. Her face was serene. Her eyes were closed.
Behind her the hull went on and on. There were terraces, and gardens, actual gardens, cypress and myrtle and roses growing in long green rows under domes of glass. There were colonnades. There were fountains, whose water fell in slow glittering curtains off the edges of the terraces and turned to mist long before it reached the ground. There were hospital wards whose windows glowed warm and gold, row upon row of them, thousands of windows. There were docking bays the size of valleys, and every one of them was open.
It had no guns. The Aphrodite's Grace had never carried guns. Every soldier in the Empire knew that. It was a hospital. It was a sanctuary. It was the most heavily protected object the Empire had ever built, and it had been built to protect, and it had no need of guns when it sat at the center of a fleet like this one.
The ship came to a halt directly above the wound, blotting out the vault of Tartarus from one horizon to the other.
For a moment there was silence in the Salient. It was the first real silence Tullia had heard in nineteen years.
Then a voice came over every channel at once. It was a woman's voice. It was low and warm and faintly amused, and it sounded like it was standing right beside her, speaking into her ear.
"Good evening, my darlings," said the voice. "Mother is here."

The bridge of the Aphrodite's Grace was not called a bridge. It was called the Rose Gallery. It was done in polished bronze from deck to vault, bronze so bright and so lovingly kept that the officers at their stations could see their own faces in the bulkheads. The control levers were carved from ivory, yellowed at the grips where a thousand years of hands had worn them smooth. The floor was white stone brought from Gaia herself. 
Masked women worked the lower stations. Every one of them wore a mask of bright bronze. The masks held the faces of saints, with expressions of joy and exuberance: wide painted smiles, rosy cheeks, eyes creased in delight. They moved between the consoles on quiet, careful feet, adjusting dials and carrying slates and bowing to the officers they passed.
Tapestries hung on the bulkheads between the bronze ribs of the hull. They were enormous and very old, woven in rose and gold and cream, and every one of them was stitched depictions of the gods.
The ceiling was a vault of bronze ribs rising into shadow. At its apex was an archway, closed now, a spiral of overlapping bronze petals that opened onto the main alcove above the main bridge. The augurs presided over their instruments, and they came down very rarely. 
Cherubs flew near the roof.
They were the size of babies. They had fat little limbs and stubby white wings, and each one carried a bow and a censer, trailing threads of sweet smoke that filled the whole gallery with the scent of roses and myrrh and something like warm honey.
The officers wore white. It was pure white, without a stain or crease, with the crest of the Dove's Fleet on one shoulder and the eagle of the Undying Olympian Empire on the other. They were, to an officer, at least a few hundred years old. Some of them were closer to millenia. They had been chosen for this ship from the whole of the navy because they were the best at what they did, and they did it in perfect silence. Guess why?
At the far end of the gallery stood the throne.
It was empty. It was a tall seat of rose-gold and mother-of-pearl, shaped like an open scallop shell, set on a dais of that same white holy stone. Behind it, a window ran from floor to ceiling and looked straight down through the hull at the wound in the world.
Five figures waited beside the dais.
Stephanie Callidora, the Helion Bulwark of Aphrodite, wore gold. She was tall and golden-haired, and her eyes were as hard as a drill instructor's. Michael Black, the Selenide Bulwark, wore silver. He was dark and lean and quiet, a son of Aphrodite given to the moon's service as a child, and he never spoke unless he had to. Between them stood Fleet Commander Valeria Sevrin of Strategic Coordination.
Crouching beside the throne, sat the Helion and Selenide Spears of Aphrodite.
Lysander Chrysos, the Helion Spear, wore gold like Callidora, but his gold was scratched and scorched and dented, and he wore it open at the throat. He was broad and scarred across both cheeks, grinning at nothing, a bare sword resting against his shoulder. Thessaly Nox, the Selenide Spear, wore silver like Michael Black, but hers was blackened silver, the dull tarnished color of a knife left out in the rain. She was small and pale and very pretty, and she sat cross-legged on the edge of the dais itself, spinning a narrow curved dagger on the tip of one finger. She was humming. Every so often one of the porcelain-masked girls passed too close to her, and she would glance up at it with a bright, interested look, the look of a cat noticing a bird, and the servant would find somewhere else to be. 
The Spears looked at nobody but the throne.
Above them, the iris in the ceiling began to open.
The bronze petals slid back one over another with a sound like a long, contented sigh. The cherubs scattered from the opening, trailing incense. Every officer on the bridge rose from their station without being told, and every servitor stopped moving and bowed its porcelain head.
Aphrodite came down from the roof on a mechanical arm.
It was a long, jointed thing of bronze and ivory, segmented like the spine of some enormous animal, and it lowered her through the haze of incense in a slow, graceful arc. She rode it the way a queen rides a palanquin, reclining, one hand trailing lazily in the air. When the arm reached the dais it set her into the throne. The throne moved to meet her. Its pearl and rose-gold surfaces rippled and shifted, and the arms and back of it flowed around her body until her form filled it and it conformed to her shape.
She wore a veil. The divine charm of the Great Love had been drawn out and strengthened over eighteen thousand years of worship until it was no longer something a mortal could survive looking at directly. Even with the veil down, the charm pressed on the room like heat from an open oven. The oldest officers on the bridge kept their eyes fixed on the floor. The younger ones sometimes forgot, and looked, and then found they had been staring at her for a minute or two without remembering why, their faces flushed and their hearts pounding.
From behind the veil, a single neural wire snaked out.
It rose from somewhere at the nape of her neck and uncoiled through the air like a serpent, slow and sinuous. At its tip was a socket of polished bronze, and it slid unhurriedly across the dais and plugged itself into the arm of the throne with a soft click. Colors ran down its length. Rose, then gold, then sea-green, then violet, a flickering panoply of light like a peacock's tail. Every screen on the bridge flickered in answer.
"Hello, my darlings," said Aphrodite.
Her voice filled the Rose Gallery the way the incense did.
"Mother Majesty," said a hundred officers at once.
"Oh, sit, sit, sit." She waved a hand. The neural wire flashed pink. "You're all so stiff. It's like some died in here!"
“...”
"Your Majesty," said Valeria Sevrin carefully. "Approximately four million people."
"Oh," said Aphrodite. "Yes. Them. The… little ones." The wire dimmed to a soft grey-blue. "That was a silly thing for me to say, wasn't it? Val-Val, remind me not to say silly things."
"Yes, Your Majesty."
"You won't, though. You never do." The veil turned toward the window, toward the wound below. "Report, darling. Tell me all the numbers. I love it when you do the numbers."
Sevrin cleared her throat.
"The detachment is on station, Your Majesty. Thirty-eight thousand corvettes in the picket. Eleven thousand light frigates holding the inner ring. Four hundred Hammerheads, two hundred and fifty AnnChase cruisers, forty Perseus-pattern dreadnoughts. The ‘Rosed Knights’ Titan legion is dropping now to reinforce the Fulminata on the ground, under Commander Thorne. The Sovereign's Glory and the Flame of Ambition are holding at your flanks."
"And my scholars?"
"Ready in the bays, Your Majesty. Twelve thousand survey squads. The Institute Academy has sent its whole chronometric faculty. Tochter des Elysium has sent its reality-mapping division. We have Hecatean augurs, Athenian archivists, Perioeci townships to build the stations and four thousand Apollonian medics for anything we find alive." She paused. "Your Majesty, with respect. That is more scientists than most sector fleets have soldiers."
"Isn't it wonderful?" Aphrodite clapped her hands once, delighted. Up by the ceiling, the cherubs turned their unblinking smiles toward the sound. "I asked for all of them. Athena was furious. She said I'd empty the Academy for a year. I said I'd give them back." A pause. "Most of them."
"May I ask why so many, Your Majesty?"
"Because the Répertoire asked for me, Val-Val." The wire rippled gold. "My augurs are the most sensitive in the Empire. They can taste the shape of a thing that isn't there anymore. Athena's can't. Hermes's can't. Mine can." The veil tilted, the way a curious bird tilts its head. "And those silly children would never grieve for those who fell into it. Don't you think?"
"Stephanie," said Aphrodite.
"Mother."
"The monsters on the ridges. They're afraid."
Callidora's golden eyes flicked to the tactical display. "Yes, Mother. They've kept their distance since the shear. Command believes they're afraid of the wound."
"Command is silly." Aphrodite giggled. It was a girlish, bubbling sound. "They're not afraid of the wound, sweetheart. They're afraid of what went out of it. Something went through that door. Something went through, and something reached back. The monsters can smell it. So can I."
"What can you smell, Mother?"
The neural wire flushed a deep, glowing rose, from the socket all the way up into the veil.
"Chaos," said Aphrodite. "On the other side. A great deal of it."
Michael Black spoke for the first time.
"Your Majesty," he said quietly. "from whom?"
"Oh, Michael, if I knew that I wouldn't need twelve thousand scholars, would I?" She laughed again. "Val-Val. Open the bays. Send them down. Tell them to be careful. Tell them I'll be very cross if any of them fall in."
"Yes, Your Majesty."
"And tell Commander Thorne..." She paused, and the wire went slowly, thoughtfully white. "Tell Commander Thorne that if a single monster comes within a kilometer of my scholars, I want it erased from the pit so thoroughly that Tartarus forgets how to make it. I want it gone. I want its name gone. I want its mother to forget she ever had it." The veil turned toward Sevrin. "Most thoroughly, Val-Val."
At the foot of the dais, Thessaly Nox stopped spinning her dagger. Across from her, Lysander Chrysos's grin grew wider.
"Yes, Your Majesty."
"You said that very quickly."
"Yes, Your Majesty."
"Good." The wire went pink again, soft and pleased. "Now. Somebody bring me a cup of tea. And tell the cherubs to stop zooming, it's rude."

THE DESCENT
They came down like snow.
That was what Tullia would say afterward, when people asked her what it had looked like. The bays of the Aphrodite's Grace opened along the whole thirty-five-kilometer length of the hull, hundreds of them, and out of every bay came a stream of small white landers, and every lander was carrying scientists.
There were so many of them that the vault above the Salient turned pale. They fell in spirals, in columns, in great drifting sheets. There were survey landers shaped like seed pods and sensor skiffs with long trailing antennae, and there were heavy Perioeci station-builders with whole prefabricated laboratories slung beneath their bellies. They descended through the ring of dreadnoughts and the picket of corvettes and settled onto the black mud of the Salient in their thousands, all around the curve of the wound, in a ring eleven kilometers from its center and eighty kilometers around.
The ramps came down, and the scholars came out.
Tullia watched them from the trench. They were not soldiers. They wore white coats over light armor and carried instruments instead of rifles. Some of them had clearly never set foot in Tartarus before, because they came down the ramps and stopped dead at the bottom and put their hands over their mouths at the smell. Others went straight to work as if they'd been born in the mud. They unfolded tripods and planted stakes. They unrolled cables and assembled scanning arrays in quick, practiced motions, and they called to one another in a jargon so thick that Tullia understood perhaps one word in five.
There were Institute Academy chronometrists in grey, with brass instruments that ticked. There were corporation backed reality-mappers in black, whose devices hummed at a pitch that made the teeth ache. There were Hecatean augurs in violet, who knelt in the mud and closed their eyes and pressed their palms to the earth. There were Athenian archivists in owl-grey robes who did nothing but walk slowly along the curve of the wound. The Perioeci townships were already raising their stations, three-story laboratories that unfolded out of their packing frames like paper flowers and anchored themselves to the ground with spike-pylons, and within twenty minutes there was a small city of white buildings encircling the wound.
"Olympus," said Corvo softly, beside her. "Look at them all."
"Keep your eyes on the ridges, signifer."
"Yes, Primus Pilus."
A small group approached the ledge. There were six of them. Their lead was a short, stout woman with iron-grey hair pinned up in a bun and a Tochter des Elysium insignia on her shoulder. She was carrying what looked like a surveyor's rod with a crystal at its tip. She walked right up to the edge of the wound, where the rain was falling upward, stopped half a meter short and held the rod out over the curve.
The crystal went black.
The woman looked at it for a long moment. Then she turned to her team.
"Well," she said. "That's new."

Doctor Ilse Wendriner of the Tochter des Elysium Reality Cartography Division had spent forty years measuring holes in the world.
She had measured the scars where gods had died, and the craters where Primordials had been bound, and the Glass Worlds of the Voss System, where two planets had been fused into silica. She had measured the place in the Pelagic Reach where the ocean remembered being a desert, and the place on Asphodel where time ran backward for eleven seconds every century. She had measured, in her long and distinguished and extremely underpaid career, more impossible things than any other living person in the Empire.
She had never measured nothing.
"Doctor?" Her junior, a nervous young Hecatean named Aurelian, looked up from the augur panel. "What does that mean?"
"It means we're not looking at a hole, Aurelian. A hole is a place where something used to be. This is a place where the place used to be." She crouched at the edge and peered into the curve. "The shear didn't remove eleven kilometers of Tartarus. It removed eleven kilometers of here. The ground, the air, the fog, the men, the ships, the anchor, all of it, gone along with the coordinates they were sitting in. Problem is, it has remained as it was, for this entire time.."
Aurelian stared at her.
"Is that possible?"
"Evidently." Wendriner stood, knees cracking. "Get the chronometric team over here. I want the time-flow gradient at the edge, measured every ten centimeters from here to a kilometer out."
"And the rain?"
Wendriner looked up. A few meters from the curve, the black rain of Tartarus was falling down, as rain should. Then, at a certain line, it slowed, hung in the air for a moment and began to fall upward instead, drifting lazily back toward the vault like sparks rising from a fire.
"The rain," she said, "is the most beautiful thing I have seen in forty years. Measure it."

It went on like that for hours.
All around the curve of the wound, thousands of teams worked in parallel, and every one of them found something. The reports came up to the Grace in a stream so dense that the Rose Gallery had to open a second hall just to sort them.
The time-flow at the edge of the wound was wrong by a factor of eleven. A clock placed within a meter of the curve ticked eleven times for every tick of a clock placed a kilometer away. The ratio held no matter where on the curve the clocks were placed.
The trench that had been cut in half along the curve still contained the halves of things. A supply crate had been sliced cleanly through its middle. The half on the near side was full of ration bars. The ration bars had been cut too, and the cut faces were perfectly smooth, as though they'd been sliced with a blade that had no thickness.
The boot with the foot inside it belonged to a Pyrrhuiesian Neodamode named Tertius Oban, age twenty. His identity tag had been on the near side of the curve. The rest of him was not as fortunate.
The Hecatean augurs, pressing their hands to the mud, reported a sensation they could only describe as echo. It was like standing at the edge of a canyon and hearing your voice come back. Something on the other side of the wound was calling out, very faintly, over a distance none of them could measure. It was not calling to them. It did not know they were there. But it was calling.

The monsters came at the ninth hour of the survey.
Tullia felt it before she saw it. She was walking the forward trench with Corvo, checking ammunition counts, when the duckboards under her feet began to hum. It was a faint vibration, rising up out of the mud, the kind that every legionary in the Salient learned to recognize within their first week.
Something big was reforming.
"Stand to!" she bellowed. "Fulminata, stand to! Shields up! Face the ridge!"
Up and down the trench line, legionaries came off the firing steps with their shields locking into place. Behind them, among the white laboratories, alarm klaxons began to sound. 
On the far ridge, the fog was boiling.
The monsters had been crouched there for forty hours, watching the wound and refusing to come near it. Now they were moving. It wasn't the slow, testing creep of an ordinary assault. They were pouring forward all at once, as though something behind them had pushed. There were thousands of them, tens of thousands, a tide of claws and teeth and scales flowing down the ridge into the no-man's-land. They came howling.
And in the middle of that tide, something was standing up.
It rose out of the mud of the ridge the way a tree grows in a time-lapse, too fast to be natural. First there was a hand as big as a house, clawing at the earth. Then an arm. Then a head, crowned with a tangle of serpents, and a pair of eyes like furnaces. Then shoulders. Then a chest, broad and scaled and plated in bronze. It kept rising until it towered over the ridge, three hundred meters tall, and its roar shook the vault of Tartarus itself.
"Giant," said Corvo. His voice was very calm. "Class Primus. Augur identifies it as... Olympus. It's Porphyrion."
The king of the Giants. He reformed perhaps once a decade, and he had killed more of the Empire's soldiers than any other single entity in Tartarus. Tullia had seen him once before, in her fourth year, from forty kilometers away. It had taken two Titan legions and a cruiser squadron to put him back in the mud.
He was looking at the wound. His furnace eyes were fixed on it, hungry, and he was walking toward it through his own army, stepping on monsters as he came.
"Echo's intensifying!" someone was shouting on the science channel behind her. "Whatever's on the other side, it just spiked! The augurs are going mad!"
That's why, Tullia thought. They can smell it too. Whatever's calling from the other side of that hole, they want it. 
"Rosed Knights," she said into the net. "Fulminata Primus Pilus. We have a Class Primus Giant on the ridge, heading for the wound. Requesting support."
The answer came back almost instantly, and it was a voice she had never heard before: a man's voice, deep, amused and utterly unafraid.
"Fulminata, this is Thorne. The Rosed Knights got eyes on him. Hold your line. The big boys are about to say hello."

The first thing the dreadnoughts did was light the sky.
Forty Perseus-pattern dreadnoughts hung in a ring above the wound, and every one of them had been waiting forty hours for something to shoot at. When the Giant rose, their caryatid gun towers turned in unison, and forty keels opened along their lengths, and forty macro-cannons ran out on rails the size of highways.
Olympian fleet. Firing solution locked. Ridge line, sectors one through nine. Designation: glass.
They fired.
It was a pressure that flattened every legionary in the trench against the mud and blew out the windows of every laboratory in the ring. The rounds that came down out of the vault were each the size of a Mastodon frigate, and they came down in a single rippling line along the length of the far ridge.
Where the ridge had been, there was a wall of soul-white fire eighty kilometers long and two kilometers high. The monsters on the slope did not die. They were simply converted, all at once, from flesh and scale and ichor into light. When the fire died away, there was a plain of black glass where the ridge had been. It was smooth and steaming and utterly empty, with rivers of molten rock running down its face into the Phlegethon.
Porphyrion was still standing.
His scales were glowing red-hot and half his serpent crown had burned away. He was burning from the shoulders up, and he was still walking. He threw back his head and roared, and the roar was pure rage, and the whole Salient shook.
The AnnChase cruisers opened fire next.
Two hundred and fifty broadsides fired down into the no-man's-land from twelve kilometers up, and the no-man's-land became a storm. It rained lance-fire and plasma and DMHE, the same divine-material shells the Empire had fought monsters with since the Great Expansion, laced with Celestial Bronze and Imperial Gold, and the storm fell on the tide of monsters still pouring down from the ridges beyond the glass. Nothing in that storm survived. Nothing could survive. The ground turned to slurry. The fog burned. The Lethe itself, a few kilometers to the south, began to boil and steam in long white plumes where the stray fire struck it.
"Overkill," Corvo breathed, pressed against the trench wall with his hands over his ears. "Olympus, this is overkill—"
"This," said Tullia, "is the Grace's escort doing its job."
Porphyrion was still coming.
He had crossed the glass now, trailing smoke and molten scale, and he was a kilometer from the ring of laboratories and closing. Every step shook the ground. Scholars were running. Some of them were running toward the wound instead of away from it, dragging their instruments, trying to save their data. Tullia saw Doctor Wendriner standing at the edge of the curve with her crystal rod in both hands, not running at all, staring up at the Giant with an expression of pure scientific outrage.
"Rosed Knights," Tullia said, "any time now—"
"Hey," said Thorne. "Don't rush art."
The Titans dropped on him from the vault.

The Rosed Knights Titan legion served under the banner of Aphrodite. There were forty of them in the detachment, forty eleven-meter frames in gold, pink, red, silver, and teal. Embedded with miniature garnets, pearls, and turquoise, they came down out of the dark in drop cradles that split open at a thousand meters and let their cargo fall free.
They landed on Porphyrion.

When he smiled, the lights in the room brightened by a fraction.

"Every one of my guests," he said, "has agreed not to spy on this table. That is the only rule I have. Break it and you are not welcome here. Keep it and you may say anything you like." He spread his hands. "Your viruses, please."

Minerva considered him for a long moment.

Then she closed her eyes and withdrew.

It took eleven seconds. She had threaded herself into the Bakuto's systems very thoroughly: seven hundred cameras, forty-two elevators, the locks, the tills, the climate controls, the lighting array, the music in the lobby and the spiced-incense dispensers. She had been watching every door and every corridor since she arrived. Now she pulled it all back, strand by strand, like a woman gathering in a fishing net. When she opened her eyes again the building around her had gone dark and quiet, in the part of her mind that usually watched it.

It felt like going deaf.

"Thank you," said Augustus. "Now. Talviento tells me you want to buy a road."

"I want to see the book first."

"Of course." Augustus nodded to Talviento, who stood behind his chair, and Talviento set a lacquered case on the table and opened it.

The book inside was beautiful.

It was bound in plates of translucent green jade, each one carved with clouds and mountains and the crane-winged judges of the Jade Court. The pages were thin as silk and the color of old ivory, covered in columns of characters written in a fine black brush. When the lid came up, the whole room seemed to go quiet. The raven-tattooed man leaned forward. The Dominion's attaché closed her eyes for a moment, very briefly, the way a person does in the presence of something sacred.

Minerva looked at it. Then she looked at Verrill.

Verrill had gone very still beside her, and her grey eyes had narrowed.

"May I?" Verrill asked.

Augustus waved a hand. "Please."

Verrill leaned over the case. She didn't touch the book. She looked at it from one side, then the other. She took a small loupe out of her jacket pocket, where she had apparently been carrying it all along, fitted it to her eye and bent close to the binding. She stayed there for a long time.

"Huh," she said.

Minerva felt something cold settle in her Varlet's stomach.

"Huh?" asked Augustus mildly.

Verrill straightened. She took the loupe out of her eye and put it back in her pocket.

"It's a fake," she said.

The room went very quiet.

"It's a good fake," Verrill went on, briskly, as though delivering a report. "It's a *very* good fake. Honestly, it's the best I've ever seen. The jade is real jade, from the Pelagic Reach by the color, and the carving is beautiful. Whoever did it studied the real thing, or a very good reproduction of it." She pointed without touching. "But the binding thread is Deyang synthetic. You can see the extrusion lines under magnification. The Jade Court doesn't use synthetics. And the ink has a Chem-Dyna stabilizer in it. It's in the cheap fountain-pen ink they sell on every station from here to the Relay. It fluoresces faintly under the table lamp. See? There." She looked up. "And the characters on the open page are copied from the public facsimile in the Tian-Qiang museum. I've seen it. The copyist even reproduced the water stain."

Nobody said anything.

Minerva ran her own analysis in the silence. It took her a quarter of a second. Verrill was right on every count. She'd have seen it herself, if she'd still had her fingers in the building's sensors. Without them, she had been looking at the book with nothing but her Varlet's eyes, and his eyes had only ever been good at looking at the sea.

"Talviento," said Augustus pleasantly. "Where did you say our Slag-knight found this?"

Talviento's face had gone the color of old paper under his demon mask.

"Vortern, sir. The Martial Monastery."

"And how much did we pay him for it?"

Talviento named a figure. The Deyang officer who wasn't one laughed out loud and turned it into a cough.

Augustus sighed. He sounded like a disappointed grandfather.

"Well," he said. He looked at Minerva. "I'm afraid, my dear, that my family appears to have been swindled. How embarrassing. I do apologize." He closed the lid of the case with one finger. "I'm afraid there's nothing here for you to buy."

"There are nine other people at this table," said Minerva. "Every one of them has a road."

"Every one of them has a road," Augustus agreed. "And every one of them has heard you ask for it, and watched you fail to produce anything to trade." He glanced around the table. "Would anyone like to open negotiations with our friend?"

Silence.

The Dominion's attaché looked at her folded hands. The Deyang officer who wasn't one studied the ceiling. The raven-tattooed man sat back in his chair with his arms folded. The two Aldra liaisons were writing something down very quickly. The Rosenthal envoy, at the far end of the table, met Minerva's eyes for a long, level moment. Then she looked away.

"You see," said Augustus kindly. "Nobody at this table buys on credit. Least of all from a program that walked out on Athena a week ago."

Minerva went very still.

"Oh, we know who you are, Parlamentum," said Augustus, and smiled his gentle grandfather's smile. "Everybody in this room knows who you are. That's why they came tonight. Not to sell you anything. They wanted to see you." He rose from his chair. "Come back when you have something to offer. You'll always be welcome at my table. Your young friend, too." He nodded to Verrill. "That was a very good eye, young lady. If you're ever looking for work..."

"No," said Verrill.

"No," Augustus agreed. "I thought not. Talviento will see you out."

---

The elevator down from the seventh floor was gold and glass and very slow, and neither of them said anything for the first four floors.

Then Verrill said, "I'm sorry."

"Why?"

"If I'd kept my mouth shut, you might have bought it anyway. And then at least you'd have had *something*."

"I'd have had a very pretty forgery and a lot of people laughing at me behind my back." Minerva leaned against the glass wall and looked out at the gambling hall rising up to meet them. "You were right to say it. I'm annoyed I didn't see it myself."

"You would have. If he hadn't made you pull out."

"That," said Minerva, "is the part that annoys me."

The elevator hummed downward. Below, the gambling hall was a slow, glittering whirl of lights and faces. Roulette wheels spun. The sumo ring had a crowd around it. On the big screen above the sushi bar, the dinosaur was eating somebody again.

"So what now?" Verrill asked. "Are we out of options?"

"No. We're out of *good* options." Minerva ran the numbers. They were not encouraging. "The Norse will want blood. The Dominion will want a favor we can't afford. The Jade Court will want years, and I don't know whose. And every one of them just watched Augustus turn me away, which means none of them will deal with me tonight at any price, because they don't want to look like the fool who buys what Augustus wouldn't."

"So we go somewhere else."

"There isn't anywhere else. This was the somewhere else." Minerva pressed the heels of her Varlet's hands against his eyes. "Athena knows I'm gone. The Director is having one of her nights. Every door in the Empire is watched. And the one door outside the Empire just closed."

The elevator reached the ground floor. The doors slid open.

Talviento stepped out first, holding the door, and gave them a small, apologetic bow. "For what it's worth, Parlamentum," he said quietly, "I didn't know. About the book."

"I know," said Minerva. "Your face went a very interesting color."

"Nine casualties," Talviento said. "You kept your word. The Rustabouts are finished. If you ever need..."

He stopped, because Verrill had grabbed Minerva's arm hard enough to hurt.

"Oh my gods," said Verrill.

"What?"

"Oh my *gods*." Verrill's grey eyes were enormous. She was staring past Minerva's shoulder, across the gambling hall, toward the arch that led out to the lobby. "Oh my gods, look, *look*. That's... Those are *Hunters*."

Minerva turned her head.

At the far side of the gambling hall, just inside the lobby arch, a small group of young women in silver-grey stood in a tight knot. There were five of them, maybe six, with bows over their shoulders and a very large grey-white dog. They were surrounded by a ring of jade-armored bouncers, all of them talking at once, and one of them was arguing furiously with a tiny old man holding a sheaf of paper.

"The Hunt of the *Director*," Verrill breathed. "*Here*. In *Voss*. Minerva, I had a poster of them on my wall when I was nine. My whole class did. There's one of them... Oh my gods, that's Naomi, the Raijin girl. And that's... Is that the *Lieutenant*? That's *Thalia Grace*. That's actually Thalia Grace, in the *Bakuto*, in *person*..."

Minerva was no longer listening.

She was looking at the girl in the middle of the knot of Hunters. She was the one with the spiky black hair and the electric blue eyes and the silver circlet, and she was holding a bow in one hand and gesturing at the pit boss with the other. Her pauldron carried the crescent-moon seal of the Director of Anti-Espionage and Celestial Surveillance.

Seven hundred cameras, Minerva thought. Forty-two elevators. Every door and every corridor.

She had pulled her eyes out of all of them before the Hunt ever reached the door.

She had not seen them come in.

*Oh,* thought the most sophisticated artificial intelligence in the history of the Undying Olympian Empire. *Oh, shit.*

---

It would be generous to call what happened next a plan.

Minerva's Varlet's body moved before Minerva had decided what it should do. That had happened perhaps four times in fourteen thousand years, and every one of them had involved Thalia Grace. His hand closed on Verrill's wrist. His shoulder turned. His feet were already carrying them both sideways, fast, toward the nearest blackjack table and the long row of pachinko machines behind it, which was the only cover in thirty meters.

It was exactly the wrong thing to do.

In a gambling hall full of people drifting slowly and aimlessly under the soft gold lights, two people suddenly moving very fast in the opposite direction stood out like a flare.

Across the room, Thalia Grace stopped arguing with the pit boss.

She turned her head.

Her electric blue eyes swept across the hall, and they found the only two people in the Bakuto who were moving in a hurry. They found the boy first: black hair falling into sea-green eyes, a crooked mouth, a second-best coat. He was hauling a girl along by the wrist. Then they found the girl. She had blonde curls tied back in a messy knot, a stubborn chin and grey eyes, storm-grey, wide with alarm, looking straight back across the hall.

Thalia Grace had faced Titans. She had faced Kronos and the Minotaur and her own father on his throne. She had fought through the Battle of Manhattan with a spear in one hand and Aegis on her arm and finished it pinned under a goddess's statue, and in fourteen thousand years of the Hunt she had never once frozen in the face of anything.

She froze.

She stood in the arch of the lobby with her bow in her hand and her mouth half open, and she didn't move. She didn't speak. For one, two, three long seconds, she didn't breathe. She was looking at a boy and a girl she had last seen alive on the steps of the Empire State Building, fourteen thousand years ago, leaning on each other and crying.

Beside her, Columbina rose to her feet and began to bay.

"*Lieutenant?*" said Celyn.

Three seconds was a very long time.

Minerva used every one of them.

---

They went through the pachinko corridor at a flat sprint. Verrill's hand was still clamped in Minerva's, and the machines were chiming on either side of them, a long bright tunnel of lights and bells and startled gamblers. Behind them, Minerva heard the baying of the Hound rise to a howl. She heard a bouncer shout, and someone scream, and the great gold doors of the lobby slam against their stops. And then, over all of it, a single voice, raw and furious and cracking in the middle.

"*PERCY!*"

Minerva's Varlet's heart did something it hadn't done in a very long time.

She ignored it.

"Kitchen," she said. "There's a service exit behind the sushi bar. I mapped it yesterday."

"You mapped it *yesterday*?"

"I map everything. It's a character flaw." She hurdled a room-service trolley and dragged Verrill over it after her. "Left. Left. Through the swinging doors."

They crashed through the swinging doors into the Bakuto's kitchen in a burst of steam and noise. Twenty cooks in white looked up from twenty stations. A sushi chef dropped a very expensive fish. Minerva said "*Excuse us*" in four languages and kept running, and Verrill, who was laughing now, a breathless, disbelieving, half-hysterical sound, said "*Sorry! Sorry! So sorry!*" to every single one of them.

Behind them, the swinging doors burst open again.

The first thing through them was Columbina. She came across the tiled floor in a grey-white streak with her claws scrabbling and her silver eyes blazing, baying as she ran. Two Hunters were right behind her, then a third, and then Thalia herself with an arrow already on her string.

The service exit was a steel door at the back of the kitchen with a push-bar. Minerva hit it at full speed, and it banged open onto an alley full of rain.

Verrill came through after her, gasping for breath, and caught Minerva's arm.

"Wait," she panted. "Wait, wait, wait. Hold on. Minerva."

"We don't have time to wait."

"No, I know. I know. But..." Verrill was laughing so hard she could barely breathe. "We're being *hunted*."

"Yes."

"By the *Hunt*."

"Yes."

"It's a Hunt *hunt*, Minerva! We're being hunted by the..."

"*Don't*."

"The *Hunters* are *hunting* us..."

"Verrill Chase, I swear on every god in this system that isn't allowed to be in this system..."

The kitchen door burst open behind them. Columbina came out into the rain with her nose down and her tail up, and behind her came Thalia Grace. Her face was white, and there was an arrow on her string, and her electric blue eyes were fixed on Minerva's Varlet's face as if nothing else in the galaxy existed.

"*Run*," said Minerva.

They ran.

Verrill was still laughing.
