# Rex Invictus XV: Orpheus

*Draft. Covers the nine hours before landfall through the Anabasis Accord.*

---

## I. Nine Hours

The throne room of Olympus had been built to make mortals feel small. It worked on gods too, when they were frightened.

Zeus sat forward on his throne with his elbows on his knees and the Master Bolt resting across them. The air around him tasted of copper. Every few seconds a thread of static crawled up one of the columns and died near the ceiling, and nobody mentioned it.

Percy stood in the center of the floor, which was not where he wanted to be. Artemis stood half a step in front of him, which was not where Zeus wanted her to be. Apollo leaned against the base of his own throne with his arms crossed. Eleni waited by the doors with her hands clasped behind her back in a posture that looked relaxed and very much was not.

Minerva had put the NASA footage on a loop in the air above the hearth. Thirty-one bright points of light crawled away from a rust-colored disc. Every few minutes the loop reset and they crawled away again.

"Thirty-one," Zeus said.

"Thirty-one craft," Percy said. "About twelve hundred people, according to Merek. Maybe a little more."

"You have *asked* him."

"He sort of volunteered it, Lord Zeus. He said Command was early."

Hera's lip curled. "The prisoner speaks of his own army arriving as if it were a delivery."

"To him, it is," Eleni said from the doors. Every head turned. She did not lower her eyes. "Forgive me, Lady Hera. A retrieval element is not an invasion force. They are coming for five people. They will take them and go."

"And if we do not give them back?" Zeus asked.

Eleni hesitated.

"Then they will take them anyway, my lord. And they will not be gentle about the parts of the camp that stand in the way."

The static on the columns thickened.

"Twelve hundred soldiers," Ares said. He had been quiet so far, which for Ares was a warning sign in itself. He sat sideways in his throne with one boot hooked over the armrest, and his eyes behind the aviators had been burning since the word *Mars* first came up. "Twelve hundred soldiers taking off from *my* planet. Squatting on *my* dirt. Building towers on *my* plains, and nobody so much as sends me a card."

"You don't even go there," Hermes said.

"It's the *principle*."

"Brother." Athena did not raise her voice, but it cut through the room anyway. "The principle can wait. What matters is capability." She turned to Percy, and he felt the full weight of her attention land on him like a stack of textbooks. "You have fought five of them. Tell me honestly. What happens if a thousand arrive?"

Percy thought about the hillside in Alaska. He thought about QVARTUS rounds punching through Styx-hardened skin, about the cone of fire that had pinned him and Eleni to the ice, about how the five of them had moved like one animal.

"It took me and Eleni everything we had to put down five," he said. "And they weren't trying to hold ground. They were trying to leave. Most of the people coming now aren't as good as the Ekdromoi. Some of them are. And there'll be a lot more of them."

"Then we strike them before they land," Zeus said. "In the sky. Where I am king."

"Father." Artemis's voice was very level. "There are five of their soldiers sitting in the Big House, and they are the reason this force is coming. If we burn the retrieval force out of the sky, the next force will not be a retrieval force."

"Then we burn that one too."

"There are four million on Mars."

That landed. Even Zeus paused.

Poseidon, who had spent the whole meeting watching Percy rather than the footage, stirred for the first time. "And one of them," he said slowly, "carries the blood of my house. The one who commands them. Marien Pyrrhus." He said the name as if tasting it. "I felt her when the boy's AI spoke it. Salt and storm. She is mine, brother. Fourteen thousand years removed, but mine."

"Your blood does not stop her guns," Zeus said.

"No. But it might stop her from using them."

The argument went on. It went on for long enough that the loop over the hearth reset eleven times. Hera wanted the Mist reinforced over all of Long Island and the prisoners handed back at the border like parcels. Hermes wanted to talk to them, which Zeus called naive. Dionysus, who had been summoned from the Big House and was plainly furious about it, wanted everyone to stop shouting so he could go back and hold his haze together. Demeter wanted to know what any of this meant for the harvest. Hephaestus said nothing at all. He had spent the entire council turning one of Neumond's wing feathers over in his scarred hands, and every so often he would hold it up to the light and make a small, unhappy sound.

In the end there was no plan. There was a list of things different gods would do, and they didn't agree with each other.

Zeus would hold the sky. He did not promise to hold back.

Poseidon would hold the Sound and the sea around Long Island.

Artemis would call the Hunt back to camp.

Apollo would stay with the prisoners and the infirmary.

Athena would go to camp with Percy to "observe," which everyone understood to mean *take notes on every piece of their equipment she could get her hands on.*

Ares said he would do whatever he felt like, and nobody had the energy to argue.

Hades, who had not attended in person, sent a message through a skeletal courier. It read only: *I am listening. Do not fill my halls carelessly.*

When it was over, Zeus stood and the Master Bolt hummed awake in his hand.

"If they strike my children," he said, "I strike them. That is the only promise I will make."

He was gone before anyone could answer.

---

They came down the elevator in silence. Athena had gone ahead in a flash of grey, and Apollo had gone with her to argue about the infirmary. That left Percy, Artemis and Eleni in a small metal box playing an instrumental version of a song Percy was pretty sure had been written by Apollo in the eighties.

"That went well," Percy said.

"Your father is afraid," Artemis said. "Mine is angry. Neither of them is thinking."

"Is anyone?"

"Athena. Which is worse."

Eleni stood against the back wall. She had not said anything since leaving the throne room, and when Percy glanced at her he saw that her hands were shaking very slightly.

"You okay?"

"No, milord." She said it plainly. "I am about to fight my own people. Some of them I may know. Some of them I may have trained beside. The Pyrrhus dynasty and my house are old rivals, but they are *Imperial*. When I was a girl, I swore an oath to protect them."

"You're not fighting anyone yet."

"I will be." She looked up. "When they land and see my IFF, milord, they will not see a soldier of the Empire. They will see a Theodosian traitor standing beside an unregistered demigod who registers as the Lord Imperator. There is no doctrine for that. There is only the word *traitor*, and the Empire has very old laws about what to do with traitors."

Artemis studied her for a long moment. "Then stand near me," she said. "They will hesitate to shoot at their Director."

Eleni gave a small, humorless smile. "Or they will shoot at both of us, Lady Artemis, and write the report later."

The elevator dinged. The doors opened on the lobby of the Empire State Building, and the normal world poured in: tourists, a security guard eating a sandwich, someone arguing on a cell phone about a lease.

None of them had any idea.

---

Camp Half-Blood knew, because nobody bothered to keep it secret for very long.

By the time Percy came out of the Labyrinth entrance at Zeus's Fist, the whole camp was moving. Beckendorf and the Hephaestus cabin had hauled every piece of Greek fire out of the armory and were building firing positions along the ridge. The Ares cabin was sharpening things with an energy that bordered on joy. The Apollo cabin had split in two: half were stringing bows on the archery range, and the other half were setting up cots in the infirmary with the grim efficiency of people who had done it before.

Chiron was at the center of all of it, hooves planted on the grass outside the Big House, giving orders in a voice that didn't carry any panic at all.

"Cabin counselors, to me. Clarisse, the Ares cabin holds the southern treeline. Beckendorf, I need the catapults on the hill ready to fire on the beach. Silena, take the Aphrodite cabin and clear the younger campers into the Big House basement." He saw Percy and his expression changed. "Percy. How bad?"

"Bad."

"How long?"

Percy's eyes flickered.

*Eight hours, fourteen minutes until the main force reaches Earth orbit,* Minerva said. *Descent adds perhaps forty minutes. I am tracking thirty-one drive signatures. I am not tracking the Ekdromoi craft or the reconnaissance shuttle. If their doctrine holds, those are already ahead of the others.*

"About eight hours for the main group," Percy said. "The scouts could be here a lot sooner."

"How much sooner?"

"Minerva doesn't know. She can't see them."

Chiron absorbed that. "Then we must assume they are already watching."

Percy looked at the treeline. The woods of Camp Half-Blood had always been dangerous, full of monsters that the camp released for capture the flag and things that were not supposed to be there at all. Now he tried to imagine someone lying in that underbrush in a cloak that bent light, counting every camper who walked past.

It wasn't hard to imagine.

"Yeah," he said. "Assume that."

---

The Romans came through the Labyrinth two hours later.

Percy heard them before he saw them: the tramp of boots in perfect step, echoing up out of the rock at Zeus's Fist, and a chant that he didn't know the words to but could feel in his chest. Then the first cohort came up out of the dark in a column four wide. Their shields were painted with the eagle and the letters SPQR, their pila were shouldered, and their faces were set.

Reyna walked at the head of them with her cloak over one shoulder and her two metal dogs flanking her. She took one look around the camp, at the Greek fire and the catapults and the kids with swords too big for them, and her mouth tightened.

"Jackson."

"Reyna. Thanks for coming."

"Chiron called in an old favor. Lupa agreed. The Senate did not agree, but it was outvoted by Lupa." She scanned the treeline. "I brought the First, Second and Fifth cohorts. The rest are holding Camp Jupiter in case this is a feint. Where are the enemy?"

"Right now, on their way from Mars."

Reyna stared at him.

"From *Mars*."

"Yeah."

"The planet."

"Yep."

"Named for our patron."

"Yeah, he's not super happy about it either."

A centurion stepped up beside her, a stocky girl with a grin that seemed wrong for the occasion, freckles and a helmet with a dent in it. "Is this the Greek we heard about? The one who talks to the computer in his head?"

"Gwen," Reyna said. "Not now."

"I'm just asking."

"Hi," Percy said. "Yes. That's me. The computer's name is Minerva, and she can hear you."

*I have already catalogued her,* Minerva said. *Centurion, Fifth Cohort. Divine output: minimal. Tactical competence: adequate. Enthusiasm: excessive.*

"She says hi," Percy said.

Gwen grinned wider. "Tell her hi back."

Reyna turned away to organize her cohorts. Percy watched them deploy. They marched into position along the eastern slope as if they'd drilled it a thousand times, which they had, and locked their shields into a wall that looked like it could stop a truck.

He thought about Psiloi fire from the edge of the atmosphere.

He thought about the way a shield wall made everyone in it stand exactly where the enemy expected them to be.

*You are thinking about the formation,* Minerva said quietly.

"Yeah."

*You are correct to worry. A shield wall is a bullseye to the Psiloi. I have told Chiron. Chiron told Reyna. Reyna told Chiron that Romans do not scatter.*

"Great."

*She was very polite about it.*

---

In Houston, Karen had not slept in forty hours.

The biovirus had five hours left to live, and Minerva used every minute of it. Through NASA's eyes she watched the thirty-one signatures leave Mars orbit and settle into a trajectory that curved gently toward the inner planets. Through Space Force's eyes, borrowed from a data link NASA had with Colorado Springs, she watched the military try to calculate an intercept, fail, and then try again with bigger numbers.

Through the Situation Room's eyes, she watched the President of the United States say, very quietly, "Where are they going?"

And through the NASA flight dynamics team's projections, she found the answer before any of the mortals did.

*Varlet,* she said, as Percy walked the ridge with Beckendorf. *I have projected their approach vectors. Their trajectory curves them into a polar orbit. From there, twenty-six of the thirty-one craft can reach the Atlantic coast of North America with minimal correction. The drive signatures are dispersing. They intend to come in from several directions at once.*

"Which directions?"

*Four approach corridors. Most likely: over the ocean to the east and south, then low across Long Island Sound. A second corridor from the north, across Connecticut. A third straight down from high orbit, directly over the camp. The fourth I cannot resolve. It may be a decoy.*

"Or it's the one that matters."

*Or it is the one that matters,* Minerva agreed.

Percy stopped walking. Beckendorf, a few steps ahead with a coil of wire over his shoulder, looked back.

"You're doing the thing where you talk to the air," Beckendorf said.

"She says they're coming from four sides."

Beckendorf was quiet for a second. Then he nodded, as though Percy had told him it might rain.

"Okay," he said. "Then I need more wire."

---

Night fell. The Hunt came back.

They came out of the woods at dusk in a long silver line, bows strung, faces pale under the moon. Zoë walked at the front of them with her quiver full and her expression carved out of stone. Thalia walked beside her with her spear over one shoulder and Aegis on her arm, and when she saw Percy she lifted her chin in a way that meant *we'll talk later.*

Artemis met them at the edge of the strawberry fields. She didn't give a speech. She walked down the line and touched each Hunter on the shoulder, and each one straightened as she passed.

When she got to the end of the line she turned.

"They wear the moon," she said. "Some of them. The ones who will come with the longest rifles, the ones they call Psiloi. They are my lines, many thousands of years from now. My blood, or my blessing, or both." Her voice did not change. "If they aim at you, you will kill them anyway."

"Yes, my lady," Zoë said.

"I do not say this lightly."

"We know, my lady."

Artemis nodded once. Then she turned and walked back toward the Big House, and Percy fell in beside her without being asked.

They walked in silence until they reached the porch. Dionysus's haze had thinned over the course of the day. It hung around the house now in thin purple strands that clung to the railing like smoke that couldn't decide whether to leave. Mr. D himself was nowhere in sight.

Artemis stopped at the foot of the steps.

"When they come," she said, "stay inside."

"You know I'm not going to do that."

"I know." She didn't look at him. "I am telling you anyway, so that later I can say I told you."

"That's very mature."

"I am a goddess. I am allowed to be petty." She turned then, and her silver eyes held something that Percy had only seen in them twice before. Once had been in Alaska, when she had found him half-dead on the ice. The other time had been on the mountain, with the sky pressing down on both of them. "Perseus. There are twelve hundred soldiers coming to this camp, and every one of them has been told since childhood that you are a god. When they find out that you are just a boy—"

"I'm not *just* a boy."

"—when they find out that you are *also* a boy, they will be very, very disappointed. Disappointed soldiers are dangerous."

Percy took her hand. It was cold, the way it always was at night.

"Then I guess I'll have to not disappoint them."

She squeezed his fingers hard enough to hurt.

"Stay near me," she said.

"I'll try."

It was the best either of them could do.

## II. The Woods

**NIGHTJAR — UPPER ATMOSPHERE, 38,000 METERS ABOVE THE NORTH ATLANTIC**

"Owl screen, sound off."

"Owl-Two, ready."

"Owl-Three, ready."

Sabine Ro hung in the open door of the *Nightjar* with the planet spread out beneath her like a map somebody had spilled ink on. The coast of North America was a long, bright, wandering line of city lights, clustered thickest around a smudge the shuttle's systems labeled *New York* in cheerful mortal script. The ocean was black. Above her the stars were so sharp they looked cut out of paper.

"Would you look at that," she said, mostly to herself. "Cities. Real ones. No Grid, no shields, nothing. Just lights, sitting there in the open, waiting for somebody to notice them."

"Owl-Lead, Handler." Corvin's voice came in low and even over the command channel. "Status."

"Six hours ahead of the freight, Handler, like I promised. Drop in ninety seconds. Tell the Centurion I'll leave the welcome mat out."

"Remember your orders. Observe. Mark. Do not engage."

"When have I ever engaged when I wasn't supposed to?"

"Owl-Lead."

"Understood, Handler. Eyes only."

She keyed the lance channel. "Alright, ladies and gentlemen and whatever Tarrow is. We're going to drop into a forest full of godspawn who have never seen a Schattenfeld. Stay invisible, stay quiet, put your motes down, and if anyone so much as sneezes on a demigod, I'm leaving them behind for the wolves."

"There are wolves?" someone asked.

"Probably. Old planet. Everything bites." She checked the chronometer. "Wings out."

Eighteen pairs of glider wings unfolded along the lip of the door in near silence. The Schattenfeld harnesses shivered and went the color of the night sky.

"Owl screen," Sabine said. "Go."

They went.

---

The drop took eleven minutes. Sabine spent most of them upside down, watching the dark ocean slide under her and the coast swell up to fill the horizon.

It was beautiful. She had not expected that. She had grown up on a hive-station over Nysa and trained on dead worlds, and every planet she had ever landed on had been poisoned, burned, broken or all of the above. This one smelled of salt and pine even from altitude. There were clouds drifting over the water in lazy shapes. There were lights on the shore that meant people, living, sleeping, not afraid.

She filed the thought away. It didn't matter. Beauty wasn't in the mission profile.

At four thousand meters the island came into focus. The camp was obvious once she knew to look for it. It sat in a shallow valley between hills on the north shore, surrounded by forest, and it glowed. The glow wasn't a visible light. It was divine output, so dense and so layered that her Geistjäger counter spun up and stuttered trying to sort it.

*DIVINE PRESENCES WITHIN 40 KM: 211.*

*RATING: MINOR (194), MODERATE (14), MAJOR (2), UNRESOLVABLE (1).*

Sabine whistled.

"Owl-Four, you getting this?"

The lance's Augur, a Hecate legacy named Tarrow who had never once in his life sounded impressed by anything, came back sounding impressed. "Two hundred and eleven. Most of them are weak, as Kappa-Seven reported. But two are major, Owl-Lead. And one I can't classify."

"Can't or won't?"

"Can't. It keeps changing size."

Sabine looked down at the glowing valley.

"That's probably the thing that took Kappa-Seven apart."

"Probably," Tarrow agreed. "I'd suggest we avoid it."

"You'd suggest that about anything."

"I'm usually right."

She angled her wings toward the treeline on the eastern edge of the valley.

"Owl screen, break by lance. Owl-One, the north ridge. Owl-Two, the beach. Owl-Three, you're with me in the eastern woods. Motes down in a grid, every two hundred meters. I want every footstep in that camp logged by the time the freight gets here."

The screen broke apart. The three lances slid down through the dark in different directions, invisible even to each other except as small green diamonds in their visors.

Sabine landed in a clearing a kilometer from the camp's eastern edge, rolled once in wet leaves, and came up with her suppressed carbine already raised.

Nothing moved.

The woods smelled of moss, rain and something older underneath, something that made the small hairs on the back of her neck stand up. She ignored it.

"Owl-Lead down. Clear."

One by one, her lance checked in.

---

For three hours, it was easy.

The Sciritae moved through the forest the way they had been trained to move through every forest on every world, silent, invisible and patient. They put down their motes, tiny sensor seeds no bigger than grains of rice, which buried themselves in the soil and started listening. Each lance sent its data up to the *Nightjar*. The *Nightjar* compiled the data into a map, and every thirty minutes the map got a little more complete.

Sabine lay on her stomach in the undergrowth at the edge of the treeline and watched the camp through her scope.

It was a strange place. There were cabins built in a horseshoe around a green. Each one was different, and some of them were so different it was hard to believe they belonged to the same settlement. One looked like a bunker made of rough stone. One was made of solid gold and shone even in the dark. One was covered in vines, and one appeared to have a wall of fire running across its roof. There was a big blue house on the hill, surrounded by purple haze that her optics couldn't penetrate. There was a lake, an arena, a climbing wall that was pouring lava down its face for reasons that she could not begin to guess.

And there were children everywhere, carrying weapons that were too big for them.

"Owl-Four," she said. "Their average age."

"Estimated fourteen. The oldest might be eighteen. There are some as young as eight in the stone cabin by the house."

"Eight."

"Yes."

She thought of Firstborn cadets in the queue at the Asphodel Colony, children whose lives were planned down to the year of their death. She had been one of them, once.

"And they're arming them," she said.

"It's a training camp, Owl-Lead."

"It's a war camp." She watched a boy of maybe twelve drag a crate of what looked like green fire in clay jars up the hill toward a line of catapults. "They know we're coming. Look at them. They're getting ready."

"How could they know?"

"The thing that took Kappa-Seven apart probably told them." She shifted her scope. There was a line of shields along the eastern slope, painted red and gold, locked edge to edge. Behind the shields stood soldiers in armor that looked like a museum's idea of Rome. "Owl-Four. Are those legionaries?"

"...Yes. Roman. Real ones. Their output's higher than the Greeks'. Organized. They're forming a wall."

"A shield wall." Sabine laughed softly. "Against Psiloi."

"They don't know what Psiloi are."

"They're going to find out." She keyed the Designator. "Owl-Six. Mark the Roman line. Priority two. The volley will want it."

A faint, grim acknowledgment came back.

---

It was Owl-Three-Two who died first.

Sabine never learned exactly what happened. The lance had spread out along the edge of a creek to lay a new line of motes, and Owl-Three-Two, a quiet Artemis-adjacent legacy named Pell who was the best tracker in the screen, had gone a little farther ahead than the others. His green diamond was moving slowly along the creek. It stopped. It stayed stopped.

"Owl-Three-Two, status."

Nothing.

"Pell. Status."

His diamond turned red.

*SIGNAL LOST.*

Sabine was on her feet before she had decided to stand. "Owl-Three, hold position. Owl-Four, what do you see?"

Tarrow's voice was very quiet. "Nothing. I see nothing. There's nothing there, Owl-Lead. No divine presence. No heat. No sound. Pell's vitals just stopped."

"Nothing kills a Sciritae without leaving a signature."

"Something just did."

Sabine switched to thermal. The creek was a ribbon of cool blue. The trees were cool blue. Pell's body lay at the edge of the water, cooling slowly. His Schattenfeld harness was still active and still bending light, which was why she could only see him on thermal.

There was an arrow in his throat.

It was a silver arrow with white fletching, and it was still vibrating.

"Handler," she said. "Owl-Lead. We have contact. One down. Hostile unseen. Requesting permission to—"

The next arrow came out of the dark and took Owl-Three-Four in the eye through his visor.

Sabine threw herself flat. Around her, the lance went to ground like a flock of birds startled by a shot. Carbines came up. Visors swept.

Nothing.

There was *nothing* there.

"Owl-Four!" Sabine hissed. "Where are they?"

"I don't know! I don't *know*, there's nothing on the counter, nothing on thermal, nothing on the ELINT, they're not emitting anything, they're just *not there*—"

A third arrow. A third diamond went red.

Sabine understood then, with a cold and absolute certainty, what they were fighting.

The Geistjäger counted divine presences. It counted anything that carried a god's power.

It did not count mortal girls who had sworn that power to someone else.

"They're Hunters," she said. "It's the *Hunt*. Everyone, they're oathbound. They don't show on the counter. Go to motion. Go to *motion*—"

---

Zoë Nightshade crouched in the branches of an oak thirty meters above the creek and nocked her fourth arrow.

She could not see the enemy. She did not need to. The Lady's blessing had never been about seeing. It was about knowing where the prey would be before the prey knew it. She could feel them in the dark the way she could feel a deer holding its breath in the grass, a pressure in the air, a wrongness in the silence where something was trying very hard not to be there.

There. Beside the fallen log. A patch of shadow that was a little too still.

She loosed.

The shadow jerked, and then it was not so still anymore. A shape flickered into view as its cloak failed: a young man in grey armor, clutching the arrow in his shoulder, reaching for the rifle he'd dropped.

A second arrow from Phoebe's tree took him in the chest, and he stopped reaching.

"Five," Zoë murmured.

Across the creek, a burst of suppressed fire chewed through the branches of the tree where Ismene had been perched a second earlier. Bark exploded in a spray of white splinters. Ismene was already gone. She dropped to a lower branch, swung and vanished into the brush, laughing breathlessly.

"They have learned to watch for motion," Zoë said quietly, into the air. "Change the pattern."

The Hunters changed the pattern.

---

"Owl-One, Owl-Two, collapse on my position," Sabine said. "Owl-Three is in contact. Six down. Say again, six down."

"Owl-One copies, moving."

"Owl-Two copies, seven minutes out."

"Owl-Lead, Handler." Corvin was calm, which was worse than if he'd shouted. "Report."

"We walked into their hunters, Handler. Literal Hunters. The Hunt of Artemis. They don't show on the counter. They're killing us with *arrows*."

A pause on the channel.

"The Hunt of Artemis," Corvin repeated.

"Yes."

"Is the Director present?"

Sabine looked at her counter. The two MAJOR presences sat in the middle of the camp, near the blue house. The UNRESOLVABLE one sat right beside them.

"I can't tell. There are two majors on the field. One of them could be her."

"If you have positive identification of the Director, you are to break contact immediately. I'll say that again. Do not fire on the Director. Any operator who fires on the Director will be held accountable under Imperial law."

"And what about her Hunters, Handler?"

A long silence.

"Break contact, Owl-Lead. Pull back to the beach. Get your people out."

"Handler, they are *between* us and the beach."

---

Thalia landed in the middle of the Sciritae lance like a lightning bolt, because that was exactly what she was.

She had been waiting in the high branches for the cloaked soldiers to bunch up, and when they did she jumped. The air around her went white and split with a crack loud enough to shake leaves off the trees for a hundred meters. The shockwave threw two Sciritae off their feet. It blew out the adaptive skin on three more. For a second the whole lance was visible, grey-armored shapes crouching in the leaves, weapons raised, visors turning toward her with the jerky speed of startled animals.

"Hi," Thalia said. "Welcome to Long Island."

She raised Aegis.

Every soldier who looked at the shield froze. The face of Medusa on its surface, even as a copy, even without the power to turn them to stone, reached into the oldest part of every human brain and squeezed. It gave the Hunters two seconds.

Two seconds was enough. The arrows came out of the dark from six directions.

---

Sabine Ro was a good soldier. She had been a good soldier for twenty-two years, and her training took over before her fear could.

She did not look at the shield. She had already looked away the moment she'd seen the crackle of lightning, because every Sciritae was taught never to look straight at an unknown divine effect. She rolled left, came up behind a tree and got her carbine onto the girl with the spear.

The girl with the spear was fast. She turned before Sabine could fire and threw the spear. It crossed the distance between them faster than a round and buried itself in the tree beside Sabine's head, crackling.

Sabine fired anyway.

The shot hit the shield. It didn't matter. The QVARTUS round had been designed to punch through divine plate, and it went through the bronze face of Aegis like a needle through paper and came out the other side and caught Thalia in the arm. She went down with a yell of pure fury, blood on the leaves.

"Got the spear-girl!" Sabine yelled. "Owl-Three, on me! Bounding back to the—"

The arrow caught her under the arm, in the gap between chest plate and shoulder guard. It was the one place her armor had never been good at covering.

She felt it go in. It didn't hurt at first. It felt like being punched very hard by someone who had very bony knuckles. She tried to take a step and discovered that her legs had become somebody else's legs.

She fell back against the tree.

Through the leaves, very far away, she saw a girl in a silver parka climbing down from an oak with a bow in her hand. The girl had dark hair, braided, and a circlet on her brow, and a face so pale and so still it could have been carved.

The girl came and stood over her.

Sabine tried to lift the carbine. She couldn't find her hands.

"You," she managed. Her mouth was full of copper. "You're the Lieutenant. Zoë Nightshade."

The girl's eyes widened a fraction.

"Thou knowest my name."

"Everyone knows your name." Sabine laughed. It came out wet. "They teach it to us at school. You're on the Wall. Oldest Hunter of the Director. First of her Lieutenants." She coughed. "You're supposed to be dead."

"Not yet," Zoë said.

"Huh." Sabine looked up at the sky through the branches. The stars were still there, sharp as paper cut-outs. "Pretty planet. I didn't expect that."

Zoë knelt beside her. For a moment she looked like she might say something.

Sabine didn't hear what it was.

**NIGHTJAR — HOLDING PATTERN, LOW ORBIT**

The Relay Voice was a Hermes gift. Every Hermes legacy in the Sciritae carried it, and Sabine Ro had carried it better than most. It let her pass compressed data straight to the AtRep uplink through jamming, fog or the end of the world.

It worked for four seconds after her heart stopped.

The *Nightjar* caught the burst and fed it up the relay chain to the incoming task force, to Handler Corvin, and on toward Mars. Four seconds of data folded down to the size of a heartbeat:

The complete map of the camp.

Every mote reading.

The Roman shield wall on the eastern slope, flagged priority two.

A single visual frame from Sabine's optics, the last she ever recorded. It showed a pale girl in silver kneeling over her with a bow. Behind the girl, far across the valley, stood the blue house, and on its porch, the silhouette of a woman with a silver glow around her and a boy at her side.

The frame was tagged automatically.

*POSSIBLE MATCH: DIRECTOR OF ANTI-ESPIONAGE AND CELESTIAL SURVEILLANCE.*

*POSSIBLE MATCH: LIEUTENANT ZOË NIGHTSHADE (ARCHIVED, DECEASED).*

*UNRESOLVED: MALE, ADOLESCENT. IFF: IMPERIAL. PROVENANCE: UNRESOLVABLE.*

On the command deck of the lead Aquila, Handler Corvin stood very still and looked at that frame for a long time.

"Owl screen, Handler," he said finally. "Report losses."

It took them a minute to answer. When they did, there were fewer voices than there should have been.

"Handler, Owl-Two. Owl-Three is gone. Owl-Lead is gone. Eleven confirmed dead across the screen. We've pulled back to the beach."

"Their losses?"

"Unknown. We think we got three. Maybe four. And the girl with the lightning."

Corvin closed his eyes.

"Hold the beach," he said. "The freight is five hours out."

Ismene died in the arms of a daughter of Aphrodite in the infirmary of Camp Half-Blood, twenty minutes after the fighting stopped.

She was fifteen by her own count and three hundred and six by the calendar. She had been a Hunter since before the Declaration of Independence. The round that killed her had gone in low through her side, and Apollo worked on her for eleven minutes with his hands glowing gold to the wrist before he looked up at his sister and shook his head.

Artemis did not cry. She stood at the head of the cot and held Ismene's hand and whispered something in Ancient Greek until the girl's breathing stopped. Then she laid the hand down on the girl's chest, straightened, and walked out of the infirmary without saying a word to anyone.

Two other Hunters were dead in the woods. Phoebe had lost two fingers of her left hand. Thalia sat on a cot with a hole through her forearm, swearing at the medic who was trying to pack it, and staring at Aegis on the floor beside her. The bronze face of Medusa had a neat round hole punched straight between its eyes.

"It went *through*," she kept saying. "It went through *Aegis*."

Percy found Artemis on the dock of the canoe lake.

She was standing at the very end of it with her back to the camp. The moon was up, and the silver light lay on the water around her so thickly it looked solid.

He didn't say anything. He walked out and stood beside her.

"They were talking about Zoë," Artemis said eventually. "The dying one. She knew her name. She told Zoë that she is on a Wall somewhere, in their time. That they teach her name to children."

"That's... good? Right?"

"She said Zoë is supposed to be *dead*." Artemis's hands had curled into fists. "Perseus. Their history says my Lieutenant dies. And I do not know when."

Percy didn't have an answer to that. He took her hand instead. She let him.

*Varlet,* Minerva said very quietly. *The main force has reached Earth orbit. Descent will begin within the hour.*

Across the lake, the camp was lighting its torches.

## III. Landfall

**AQUILA LEAD — "HAMMER OF DAWN" — DESCENT CORRIDOR ONE**

The Aquila shook like a dog.

Centurion Ashmead had made thirty-one combat descents in his life, twenty-six of them into Tartarus, and he had learned long ago that the shaking was the good part. The shaking meant you were still attached to the lander. It was when the shaking stopped that you had to worry.

He stood in the aisle of the troop bay with one hand wrapped around the overhead rail and looked down the two rows of his squad. Thirteen faces behind thirteen visors, graft scars pale in the red combat lights. Most of them were young. Most of them had signed their enrollment papers less than two years ago in exchange for a chance at citizenship. All of them were terrified. All of them were hiding it, badly.

"Listen up, citizens-to-be!" he roared over the scream of re-entry. "In about four minutes, we are going to land on the most beautiful planet any of you have ever seen! Green grass! Blue water! Air you can breathe without a filter! Some of you are going to want to stop and look at it! *Don't!*"

A few nervous laughs.

"The Sciritae got hit coming in! Eleven dead! That means the locals know we're coming, and they're not waiting with flowers! We go down the ramp, we take the beach, we hold the beach until the Ekdromoi walk our people out! Nothing fancy! Anyone who does anything fancy, I'll kill them myself and save the locals the trouble!"

"Centurion!" a voice called from the back. "What if the gods come?"

Ashmead grinned inside his helmet.

"Then you'll have a great story for your grandchildren, Quillon! Assuming you live long enough to have any!"

That got a real laugh. Good. Laughing soldiers didn't freeze on the ramp.

The pilot's voice crackled over the bay speaker. "Centurion, we're through the heat. Coming up on cloud layer. Eleven other landers in the chain, all green. Two minutes to the coast."

"Copy, pilot. Take us in."

The shaking eased. The red lights steadied.

Ashmead leaned toward the narrow viewport beside the door and looked out.

Below them was a floor of cloud, silver under the moon. Past the edge of it, he could see the black water of the ocean and the long glittering line of the coast. It was just as beautiful as Sabine Ro had said.

Then the clouds lit up from inside.

---

Zeus had been waiting for three hours.

He stood on nothing, two miles above the eastern end of Long Island, in the middle of a storm system that had not existed at sunset. It had formed around him the way storms always formed around him when he was angry, pulling moisture up off the Atlantic and stacking it into towers of black cloud that reached to the edge of space. The lightning in those clouds had nowhere to go, so it crawled from one to the next and back again, in endless branching rivers of white.

He felt the landers coming long before he saw them. Metal, falling through his sky. Heat, fire, the grumble of engines. Twelve of them, stacked in a long diagonal line like birds.

One of them carried the soldiers who had shot his daughter.

Thalia's arm. The hole through Aegis. He had felt that across the whole length of the island, the way he felt every time any of his children bled, and a very old and very simple part of him had closed like a fist.

*If they strike my children, I strike them.*

He raised the Master Bolt.

---

The first bolt hit the fourth lander in the chain square on its dorsal armor.

The Aquila was built to survive orbital re-entry and anti-ship fire. Its divine-composite carapace had been designed by the Forge Cohorts to shrug off everything from plasma lances to a Titan's fist. Its engineers had never imagined that they would have to shield it against the original. The bolt went through the carapace as though it wasn't there. It went through the Hyperium drive, through the troop bay and out the bottom of the hull, and for one blinding second the lander was a silhouette inside a column of white fire.

Then it was a cloud of glowing debris falling toward the ocean.

"*Lander Four is gone!*" someone screamed on the formation net. "*Four is gone, Four is gone—*"

The second bolt hit Lander Nine. The third caught Lander Seven's port wing, and the craft pitched into a flat spin that its pilot fought all the way down to the water. Its wreck skipped off the waves twice and broke apart.

The storm moved. Ashmead saw it through the viewport, the whole black mass of it turning toward the landers the way a head turns toward a sound.

"Pilot!" he bellowed. "*Evasive!*"

"*I'm trying! There's nothing to evade, Centurion, it's the whole sky!*"

A bolt passed so close to the lead Aquila that its port-side sensor array melted. Every alarm in the bay went off at once.

On the command channel, Handler Corvin's voice came through, flat and fast. "All landers, break chain. Disperse. Do not fly in formation. Scatter and go low. Get under the cloud layer. The storm can't hit what it can't track."

"*It's tracking us fine, Handler!*"

"Then get *lower*."

---

On the ridge above the camp, Percy watched the sky burn.

The clouds over the Sound had turned into a ceiling of white fire. Every few seconds a lance of lightning came down out of it, and every few seconds there was a flash out over the water where something big had hit something bigger. The thunder came late, rolling across the camp in a steady barrage that shook the windows of every cabin.

Beside him, Beckendorf had frozen with a jar of Greek fire in each hand.

"Is that your uncle?" he said.

"Yeah."

"Is he winning?"

"Yeah."

Beckendorf let out a long breath. "Okay. Good. That's good." He didn't sound like he thought it was good.

Percy didn't think so either. He could feel the landers dying. Every time one of them hit the water, the sea told him about it: the shock of impact, the rush of cold water into a broken hull, the small warm shapes inside it going still. He couldn't block it out. He had never been able to block the sea out.

*Three landers destroyed,* Minerva said. *Nine remaining. Casualty estimate: ninety to one hundred and twenty personnel.*

"How many are left?"

*Nine landers. They have broken formation and are descending toward the beach and the southern fields. Estimated touchdown: three minutes.*

"Zeus is going to get them all."

*Probably not. They are going low. Your uncle's accuracy decreases near the ground, where his lightning must compete with the terrain.* A pause. *Varlet. I can end this faster.*

Something in her tone made him go still.

"How?"

*Merek's harness gave me the Task Force's formation net codes when I stabilized him. I have been listening to it for five hours. I can speak on it.*

"You can *what*?"

*I can issue a verdict. In the Watchpoint's voice. CODE 2. Unconditional Retreat. The landers would abort their descent and climb back to orbit.*

Percy stared at the burning sky.

"Would it work?"

*Some of them would obey. The landers' pilots are drilled never to question a verdict during descent. Others would suspect it. The result would be confusion.*

"Confusion in the middle of *that*?"

*Yes.*

"People would die."

*People are already dying, Varlet. If the landers reach the ground, your camp dies. I have run the projections. A full landing plus the Ekdromoi raid plus the Psiloi volley costs Camp Half-Blood between one hundred and two hundred lives. A partial landing costs perhaps forty.*

Percy's hands were shaking.

"Don't," he said.

*Varlet.*

"Don't. We don't do that. We don't lie to them in their own voice. If we do that, they'll never—"

The sky exploded again. Lander Two came apart over the shallows off the southern shore in a spray of burning metal, and the sea took what was left of it.

*I apologize, Varlet,* Minerva said, very softly.

"Minerva, *don't*—"

*CODE 2 issued.*

---

**FORMATION NET — ALL CHANNELS**

*VERDICT.*

*CODE 2. UNCONDITIONAL RETREAT.*

*ALL ELEMENTS ABORT DESCENT. CLIMB TO ORBIT. THIS VERDICT SUPERSEDES ALL PRIOR ORDERS.*

---

It was the worst possible moment for an order, and every pilot in the chain heard it at the same time.

Three of the surviving landers obeyed instantly. Their pilots had spent years learning that the voice of the Répertoire was the voice of law. They hauled back on their sticks and threw their engines to full, and the landers pitched their noses back up into the storm.

Into Zeus.

Two of them made it. The third, Lander Eleven, climbed straight into a bolt that had been meant for Lander Three and vanished in a ball of white fire.

Lander Three, which had not obeyed because its pilot was a thirty-year veteran who had never once in her life heard a Watchpoint issue CODE 2 in the middle of a descent, veered to avoid Eleven's burning wreckage and clipped Lander Six, which had been trying to climb. Both craft tumbled. Three fell into the shallows off the beach in one piece and rolled over on its back like a dead beetle. Six hit the sand of the southern field nose-first at two hundred kilometers an hour.

"*CODE 2 is not authenticated!*" Corvin roared on every channel. It was the first time anyone in the task force had heard him raise his voice. "*I say again, CODE 2 is NOT authenticated! Disregard! Disregard! It's a spoof! All landers, continue descent!*"

The two that had fled back toward orbit turned around.

The damage had been done.

**AQUILA LEAD — "HAMMER OF DAWN" — SOUTHERN BEACH**

The lead Aquila hit the beach hard enough to shatter both of its landing struts. It slid forty meters through the sand on its belly, throwing up a wave of sand and water, and stopped with its nose buried in a dune.

The ramp came down.

"*Out! Out! Out!*" Ashmead bellowed. "*Squad, go!*"

They went. Thirteen Neodamodes poured down the ramp and into the night, and the night was full of fire. The sky above them was still burning. Burning wreckage lay scattered across the sand. Farther down the beach, Lander Three lay upside down in the shallows with the surf breaking over its hull, and people were climbing out of its broken troop bay and falling into the waves.

"*Perimeter!*" Ashmead roared. "Shields up, face the treeline! Quillon, Dray, get to Three and pull out anyone who's still breathing!"

He heard the ramp of another lander slam down somewhere behind him. Then another. The survivors were landing: Hammer and two others on the beach and the fields, Three on its back in the surf, and two more still clawing their way back down out of the storm after the verdict had sent them climbing. Five out of twelve, if Three didn't count, and it didn't. He tried to count heads on the formation net. The number that came back made him feel sick.

"*Handler, Ashmead!*" he said. "Hammer's down! I've got my squad and four more! We're on the beach, we've got the perimeter, *what the hell was that verdict?*"

"*Spoof,*" Corvin said. His voice had gone back to flat. "*Somebody had our codes.*"

"*Who?*"

"*Who do you think, Centurion? We came here to get Kappa-Seven. Somebody took Kappa-Seven's harnesses.*"

Ashmead stood very still on the sand for a moment.

Then he turned and looked up the hill, toward the camp and the torches and the big blue house.

"Right," he said, very quietly. "Right, then."

**CAVALRY SHUTTLE — HIGH ATMOSPHERE, OVER LONG ISLAND SOUND**

Decurion Herrman heard the spoof verdict at the same moment everyone else did. He also heard Corvin countermanding it. And he heard, beneath both of those voices, something else, something that none of the others could hear because none of them were sitting on the back of a pegasus.

He heard a call.

It came up through the air from the water below. It wasn't a sound. It was a pressure that he felt in his teeth and his chest and the base of his skull. And under him, Brontes lifted his head, flattened his ears, and screamed.

Every pegasus in the cavalry hold screamed with him.

"*Easy!*" Herrman shouted. "Easy, Brontes, easy, *easy*—"

The shuttle's rear doors were already open. They had been open for thirty seconds, because the ala was supposed to be gliding down over the Sound to come in low and fast under the storm. The first two files had already jumped. Now the pegasi in the hold were rearing and stamping and fighting their tethers, and one of them broke free and threw itself out through the open door with its rider still on its back.

"*Ala, go!*" Herrman bellowed. "*Go now, go before they*—"

Brontes jumped.

Herrman had exactly enough time to lock his knees and grab the pommel before the pegasus dropped out of the shuttle into the open night sky. The wind hit him like a wall. His visor filled with rain and cloud and the white flash of lightning.

Brontes spread his wings, caught the air, and turned.

Not toward the camp.

Toward the sea.

"*No, no, Brontes, the camp, the camp is east*—"

Below them the water of Long Island Sound had begun to rise. It rose in a single enormous swell, a hill of black water two hundred meters across and climbing, and on top of it, standing on the crest as if it were solid ground, was a man.

He was tall and bearded and wore a fisherman's shirt and khaki shorts, which should have been absurd. In one hand he held a trident. In the other he held nothing, but his hand was raised, open, palm upward, and every pegasus in the sky was flying toward it.

"*Come,*" said Poseidon, and his voice was the sea.

Herrman had grown up among horses. He had ridden since he could walk. He had fought on horseback on three worlds and lost two mounts to monsters in the Salient and wept for both of them. He knew horses the way some men knew prayer.

He had never once in his life thought to ask where the first horse came from.

Brontes was flying toward the man on the water with every stroke of his great wings, and his mind was so full of joy that Herrman could feel it through the saddle. It was the joy of a child running to its father.

Herrman did the only thing a cavalryman can do when his horse has chosen someone else. He unbuckled his harness, tapped his jump rig to life, and let go.

"*Ala!*" he shouted into the net as he fell. "*Dismount! Dismount! We've lost them! Get off your horses and get to the beach!*"

Behind him, a hundred and forty pegasi circled the man on the wave in a slow, spiraling column. Their wings flashed in the lightning. Some of them still carried riders. Those riders were screaming.

Poseidon lowered his hand.

The wave came down.

---

Percy felt it hit.

He was halfway down the ridge toward the beach, Riptide already in his hand, and the whole world went sideways for a second as the sea told him what his father had just done. He felt the wave fall across a quarter mile of the Sound. He felt the men in it. Some were still in their saddles, some were falling with jump rigs sputtering, and all of them hit the water at the same time.

He felt the pegasi rise up out of it unharmed, circle once, and turn toward camp.

He felt most of the men not rise.

*Peltast ala,* Minerva said. *Casualties: estimated sixty to seventy. The pegasi are all alive.*

"Dad," Percy said to nobody, because there was nobody to hear it. "Dad, you didn't have to—"

A shadow passed over him. Then another. Then the sky above the camp filled with wings.

The pegasi came in low over the ridge in a great rush of wind and rain. Their bronze armor was gleaming wet, their hooves thundering against the air, and they landed on the hill all around him in a crowd so thick that he had to put his hand on the nearest flank to keep from being knocked over.

*Hey, boss!* said a voice in his head, and Percy nearly dropped Riptide.

A black pegasus was shoving its way through the crowd, one he knew.

"*Blackjack?*"

*In the flesh, boss! Me and the boys heard the big guy calling and we came right over. Turns out these new guys got the same call!* Blackjack shook his mane, spraying water. *They're kinda confused. Very polite, though. Real fancy armor. That one there's the boss of them.*

The pegasus Blackjack was nodding at was huge, even by pegasus standards, and his bronze wing-plating was scored with old scars. He looked at Percy with dark, intelligent eyes.

*You are the Sea Lord's son,* Brontes said. His voice in Percy's head was deep and very sad. *The Sea Lord called. We came. We did not know that he would drown our riders.*

Percy didn't know what to say.

*My rider was a good man,* Brontes said. *He called me Brontes. He brushed me each morning and sang to me in the dark before drops. I did not know the call would kill him.*

"I don't think it did," Percy said. "Not him. I felt a jump rig. I think he let go."

Brontes's ears lifted.

*Then I would like to find him,* the pegasus said. *When this is over.*

"Yeah," Percy said. "Me too."

**LAUNCH GANTRY 9 — HEAVY LIFTER, HIGH ORBIT**

"Hey there, Handler."

Keyes's voice on the command net was as easy as ever, but there was something underneath it now.

"Keyes. Go."

"So I've been listening to the net for the last ten minutes, buddy, and I'm starting to think this might be one of those times when subtle has failed."

A long silence on the channel. Then Corvin said, "Yes."

"Alright." Keyes let out a long breath. "Alright. Dropping the cradle. Nice knowing you."

---

The Titan came down on the southern field like a meteor.

The drop cradle burned its retro-rockets at four hundred meters, split open at two hundred and let its cargo fall the rest of the way. The Titan hit the ground feet-first with enough force to throw up a ring of dirt and turf thirty meters wide. It crouched for a moment with its knees bent and its fists in the ground, steam rising off its armor in the rain, and then it stood up.

Eleven meters of chipped, slab-shouldered steel. A single visor slit burning orange in a blocky head. A rotary cannon the size of a car on one arm and an energy blade on the other. It was the most terrifying thing most of the campers had ever seen, and they had seen a lot.

The Ares cabin charged it anyway.

Clarisse got there first, electric spear leveled and screaming at the top of her lungs. The spear hit the Titan's knee joint and discharged in a burst of blue light that should have fried a car engine.

The Titan looked down at her.

"Hey there, buddy," Keyes said over the external speakers, almost gently. "You might want to get out of the way."

He kicked.

Clarisse flew twenty meters and landed in the strawberry fields and didn't get up. Her half-brothers dragged her out while the Titan's rotary cannon spun up with a whine that shook the ground.

On the ridge above, Beckendorf's catapults fired. Six jars of Greek fire arced through the rain and burst across the Titan's chest in a sheet of green flame that clung and burned and would not go out. The Titan staggered back a step. Its cooling vents coughed smoke.

"Okay, *that's* a new one," Keyes said. "What is that stuff?"

The second volley hit it in the head.

The Titan turned its visor toward the ridge. The rotary cannon swung up.

Then, very suddenly, there was a man standing in front of it.

He wore a leather jacket and wraparound aviator sunglasses and combat boots, and he was grinning like it was the best day of his life. He was smaller than the Titan by about nine meters. He didn't seem to have noticed.

"Oh, *hell* yes," said Ares.

---

"Handler, Keyes." The Titan pilot's voice was different now. Very quiet. Very careful. "Uh. I've got a big one. On the field. In front of me."

"How big?"

"He's about two meters, Handler."

"Then he isn't big."

"Handler, my sensors say he's eleven times the output of anything I have ever fought, and I have fought *Typhon's spawn* in the Salient. He's smiling at me."

A pause.

"Can you identify him?"

Keyes looked at the figure on his screen. At the jacket. At the sunglasses. At the aura of red that was rising off the man in the rain, flickering at the edges like a burning city.

A cold feeling spread through his stomach.

"Handler," he said. "I think it's Lord Ares."

"...Say again."

"I think I'm about to fight the God of War, Handler. *Our* God of War. His prayer icon's on my dashboard." He swallowed. "Requesting guidance."

The command net was silent for three long seconds.

"Defend yourself," Corvin said.

"*Copy that.*"

---

Ares did not bother with a weapon at first. He walked straight at the Titan through the rain, and when the rotary cannon opened up on him he walked through that too. The rounds hit him like hail on a tin roof and bounced off in showers of sparks, and some of them didn't bounce off. Blood ran gold down his jacket and he laughed louder, as though each round was a joke he'd been waiting for years to hear.

He reached the Titan's leg and punched it.

The knee joint buckled. Eleven meters of steel lurched sideways and caught itself on one fist. Keyes swung the energy blade in a low arc meant to cut the god in half, and Ares caught the blade.

He caught it with his bare hand.

The energy crackled and hissed and burned through the skin of his palm down to the bone, and he didn't let go. He was still laughing.

"You know what I love about you people?" Ares yelled up at the visor, over the scream of the blade. "You built all this! All of it! Just for me! This thing is a *prayer*, kid! A giant walking prayer with a cannon on its arm! I've never been so flattered in my *life*!"

He twisted. The blade arm tore off at the elbow in a shower of sparks and fluid.

Inside the cockpit, Keyes was saying something over and over into the net. Afterward, when the recordings were reviewed, it turned out to be the words of a Mars catechism, the one every Titan pilot learned during initiation and nobody ever thought they would say to the god himself.

Ares heard it.

The god's grin faltered. Just for a moment. His head tilted.

"Huh," he said.

That was the moment the ground turned to water.

The southern field had been a field ten seconds ago. It had been dry turf over sand over bedrock. Now it was ocean. The Sound had come up through the earth beneath the Titan's feet in a single roaring surge, and the Titan sank into it to the waist before Keyes could fire his jump jets. Then to the chest. Black water closed over the armored shoulders.

Poseidon walked up out of the surf behind it, trident in hand, looking tired.

"Enough," he said to Ares. "You have had your fun."

"I was just getting started!"

"You were getting *prayed to*, nephew. By a man in a machine. And you liked it." Poseidon's eyes were very old. "Go and think about why."

Ares opened his mouth. Shut it. Scowled.

The Titan's head disappeared under the water. The orange visor light glowed faintly beneath the surface for a few seconds more.

Then it went out.

Poseidon lifted his hand, and a wave rolled up out of the flooded field and deposited a man on the sand at his feet. He was coughing and soaked and wore a pilot's pressure suit. One of his arms was bent in a direction arms should not go.

Keyes looked up at the god standing over him. Then he started to laugh weakly.

"Hey there, buddy," he wheezed. "Guess I'm the least of the mortals' problems now, huh?"

Poseidon did not understand the joke. He picked Keyes up by the back of his suit anyway, the way a man lifts a wet kitten, and carried him toward the infirmary.

## IV. The Big House

**DURHAM GUNSHIP "LONGSIGHT" — 96,000 METERS, EDGE OF ATMOSPHERE**

Up here, the war was silent.

Volley-Lead Cerelli lay on her stomach on the firing deck of the *Longsight* with her eye to a scope as long as her own body. Around her, the eleven other members of the volley lay in two rows, each one prone behind a rifle that fired a round the size of a man's forearm. The gunship's bay doors were open. Beneath them, a hundred kilometers down, the eastern end of Long Island spread out in the scope like a map drawn in light and shadow.

There was the burning wreckage on the beach. There was the flooded field where the Titan had gone down. There were the torches of the camp, and the Roman shield wall on the eastern slope, perfectly visible, perfectly still, flagged in red on every scope in the volley from the dead Sciritae's final burst.

"Priority two," Cerelli said quietly. "The shield wall. Volley, acquire."

Eleven voices murmured acknowledgment.

"Handler, Volley-Lead. Volley has priority two acquired. Requesting release."

Corvin's voice sounded like a man who had been awake for a very long time. "Why priority two?"

"The Romans hold the high ground over the southern beach. The Centurion's survivors can't advance on the camp while that wall stands. If it breaks, the Ekdromoi have a clear lane."

A pause.

"Release granted," Corvin said. "Volley, fire at will."

Cerelli laid her crosshairs on the center of the shield wall. She could see the individual shields in the scope, painted red and gold, eagles on every face. She could see the helmet of a girl standing in the second rank, a dented helmet, a girl whose face under it was freckled and grinning at something the soldier beside her had said.

She breathed out.

"Volley," she said. "Fire."

---

The Fifth Cohort never heard the shots.

The rounds came down out of the sky at eleven times the speed of sound. They crossed a hundred kilometers of air in less time than it took to blink, and they arrived before the noise of their own passage. The first thing any of the legionaries knew about them was that the shield wall broke.

It broke in twelve places at once. The rounds went through the shields, through the legionaries holding them, through the ground beneath them and deep into the hill, and each impact threw up a fountain of earth and splintered wood and red spray.

For one heartbeat, the Fifth Cohort stood there, stunned, staring at the holes in their line.

Then the second volley came down.

Gwen had turned to say something to Dakota when the first volley hit. She was still turned toward him when the second one did. He was looking at her face when it happened. Later, he would say that she looked surprised, more than anything. Only surprised.

"*SCATTER!*" Reyna was screaming. "*Fifth Cohort, break formation! Break formation, get to cover, SCATTER!*"

They didn't scatter well. They had never trained to scatter. Everything they had been taught said that the line was life and breaking it was death, and it took Reyna screaming at them three times before the survivors of the Fifth threw down their shields and ran for the trees.

The third volley caught thirty of them in the open.

*Lord Imperator.*

He stood very still.

Behind him, somebody in his squad said, very softly, "Oh, Olympus."

Reyna was watching him. "What did it say?"

Ashmead didn't answer for a while. When he finally did, his voice was not the parade-ground roar it had been all night. It was very quiet.

"It said," he said, "that we've been shooting at the Emperor."

He lowered his hand.

Down the beach, a hundred rifles came down with it.

---

In the Big House, Neumond collapsed.

The blue fire went out of his wings all at once, and they folded back against his spine with a clatter. He slid sideways down the wall. Apollo was there before he hit the floor, one hand on his chest, gold light pouring out of his palm.

"He's alive," Apollo said. "His nervous system just had half of a divine intelligence walk through it, but he's alive."

"*Hurts,*" Neumond moaned.

"I know. Lie still."

Percy's eyes flickered from blue back to green. He swayed. Artemis caught his arm.

*It is done,* Minerva said. She sounded exhausted, which Percy had not known she could do. *The Watchpoint accepted the key. It has issued CODE 18 across the task force. It has also,* and here her voice went very dry, *identified you to Marien Pyrrhus.*

"Is that bad?"

*She would like to speak to you. And to your father.*

On the landing, Castellan Oriel had gone very pale. She was listening to the verdict in her helmet, and when it finished she took the helmet off. She was older than Percy had expected, with close-cropped grey hair and a scar across her jaw. She looked at him for a long time.

Then she went down on one knee.

Her remaining operators followed her. So did Kappa-Seven, all five of them, even Neumond, who had to be propped up by Apollo to do it. Merek knelt last. The chair frame still hanging from his wrists clattered against the floor.

"Lord Imperator," Oriel said.

Percy stared at them.

"Please get up."

They didn't.

"*Please*," Percy said. "Get up. I'm not... I don't know what I am in your time, but I'm not him yet. I'm just some kid from Manhattan. I didn't do any of the things you think I did."

"Not yet," said Merek.

"Not *yet*. And I don't want you kneeling to me while there's a dead guy on the wall and a dead kid by the door and I don't even know how many people in the water." His voice cracked. This time he let it. "Just get up. Please."

Slowly, Oriel stood. The others followed.

"What are your orders, Lord Imperator?" Oriel asked.

Percy opened his mouth.

He didn't know. He didn't know at all. He looked at Artemis, and she looked back at him steadily, and her expression said *this is yours*.

"Help," he said finally. "Help us find the wounded. Yours and ours. Everybody. We've got a camp full of kids who need medics, and I'm betting you've got medics and landers full of supplies. We count the dead later."

Oriel stared at him.

Then she put her helmet back on and keyed the net.

"All Orpheus elements. Castellan Oriel, Lambda-Two. Ekdromoi confirm CODE 18. Lord Imperator's first order: casualty recovery, all parties, no distinction. Handler, recommend you deploy every medic we have."

A pause.

"*Handler confirms,*" said Corvin. His voice had gone rough. "*All elements. Casualty recovery. No distinction.*"

---

It should have ended there.

For about forty minutes, it looked like it might.

Imperial medics came down the ramps of the grounded Aquilas with their kits and their stretchers and began, cautiously, to cross the lines. Apollo's children met them in the middle of the southern field. At first they didn't speak, because neither side knew what to say. Then a medic in grey armor knelt beside a wounded Ares camper and did something with an injector pen that made the boy's shattered leg knit together in thirty seconds, and a daughter of Apollo sat back on her heels and said, "*How* did you do that?"

"Regeneration accelerant. Standard issue."

"Can you show me?"

On the beach, Neodamodes and Romans hauled the survivors out of Lander Three together. They worked in the surf side by side, pulling wounded Imperials out of the broken troop bay while the waves broke over them. Someone from the Fifth Cohort found a Neodamode trapped under a twisted strut and called for help, and three Neodamodes and two Romans lifted it together.

On Half-Blood Hill, the Trophimoi circle had stopped unmaking the border. The Egyptian-blooded operator sat on the grass with his head in his hands. Peleus was still asleep, snoring gently, a coil of smoke rising from his nostrils. Hrafn stood under the pine and looked up at its branches, and he didn't say anything.

Two ravens sat on the branch above the Golden Fleece, watching him.

They had been sitting there for a while.

In the throne room of Olympus, Zeus stood at the window with the Master Bolt in his hand and the storm still churning over Long Island, and he didn't let go of it. Hera stood beside him.

"The verdict called your nephew their Emperor," she said.

"I heard it."

"It called him the son of Poseidon. It said nothing about you."

Zeus's knuckles went white on the Bolt.

"I heard it," he said again.

On the dock of the canoe lake, Poseidon stood looking out at the Sound with Keyes's blood still drying on his hands. He was thinking about a woman named Marien, fourteen thousand years from now, who carried salt in her blood and had lost three hundred and forty soldiers tonight. He was thinking about how many of them he had drowned himself. He was thinking about his son, and a title that nobody in this world had any right to give him.

He was thinking about all this when the water under the dock went cold.

It was not a sudden cold. It came up slowly, the way cold comes up out of a deep lake in autumn. Poseidon felt it the way he felt everything in the sea, but this time he felt it wrong. The currents were bending. Something large was moving in the Sound, something that the sea did not want there, something that pushed the water aside instead of moving through it.

He knew that feeling.

He had felt it once before, a long time ago, when his father's scythe came up out of the dark.

Far out on the Sound, past the wreckage of the Aquilas, past the floating bodies of the Peltast ala, a white shape was coming out of the fog. It was the size of a cruise ship. It was, in fact, a cruise ship, a great white luxury liner with rows of lit portholes and a name painted in gold letters on its bow.

*PRINCESS ANDROMEDA.*

On its deck, lining the rails from bow to stern, were monsters.

Thousands of them.


---

## VI. The Princess Andromeda

Percy felt his father's alarm through the water before anyone else in the camp knew anything was wrong.

He was halfway across the southern field, helping an Imperial medic carry a stretcher with a groaning Hephaestus camper on it, when the sea turned cold under his feet. He stopped so suddenly that the medic stumbled.

"Lord Imperator?"

"Don't call me that." Percy set his end of the stretcher down. "Something's in the Sound."

*Confirmed,* Minerva said. Her voice was still frayed from the handshake. *One large vessel. Civilian hull. Displacement consistent with a cruise liner. Divine signatures aboard: several thousand. Monstrous. And one demigod.*

Percy knew who it was before she said it.

"Luke."

*Yes.*

"He waited," Percy said. His hands had curled into fists. "He waited until everyone here was half dead. Until we'd shot each other to pieces. He's been sitting out there in the fog watching this whole thing."

*It is what I would have done,* said Minerva.

"That's not *helping*."

---

The *Princess Andromeda* came out of the fog at full speed and turned broadside to the beach a kilometer offshore. Its lights were blazing. Music was playing from speakers somewhere on the upper decks, something cheerful and brassy, and it drifted across the water in fragments between the waves. It was the most horrible sound Percy had ever heard.

Then the music stopped, and a voice came over the ship's public-address system, amplified until it rang off the hills.

"*Soldiers of the future.*"

Percy knew that voice. He had known it since he was twelve years old, when it had belonged to a counselor who had shown him how to hold a sword.

"*My name is Luke Castellan. I speak for the Lord Kronos, rightful king of the gods. I've been watching you tonight. I saw the lightning take your ships. I saw the sea take your cavalry. I saw the Hunters of Artemis put arrows through your scouts, and I watched the gods of Olympus kill hundreds of you in a single night.*"

On the beach, the Neodamodes had gone still. The Romans had gone still. Ashmead stood with his helmet off and his head tilted, listening.

"*They're going to tell you it was a misunderstanding,*" Luke went on. His voice was reasonable. Almost gentle. "*They're going to tell you that the boy you came to kill is your Emperor, and that you should kneel. And maybe you will. But ask yourselves: what have the gods ever done for soldiers like you? They sent you into Tartarus. They left you there for generations. They let the shear throw you across time, and they haven't lifted a finger to bring you home.*

"*Lord Kronos can.*

"*Tartarus is his father's body. He knows every road in it. He can send you home. All four million of you. All you have to do is stand aside.*"

Silence on the beach.

Percy could feel it, the way the offer landed in the ranks. It was the worst thing about Luke. He had always known exactly what people wanted to hear.

*He is not lying about everything,* Minerva said quietly. *Kronos may know a road through Tartarus. It is possible.*

"Minerva, *not now*."

On the beach, a Neodamode near the back of Ashmead's squad took a step forward. He was young, maybe nineteen, with graft scars down both sides of his neck. His name was Quillon, and he'd survived the crash of Lander Three by the grace of a Roman who'd pulled him out of the surf.

"Centurion," he said. "Is he telling the truth? Can they send us home?"

Ashmead didn't answer right away.

He looked out at the ship. He looked along the rails, at the shapes crowding them. Snake-women with double tails. Dog-headed things. Giants three meters tall with clubs over their shoulders. Hellhounds the size of trucks, pacing the upper deck. Something enormous and scaly coiled around one of the funnels, its head swaying like a cobra's.

He had seen every one of those shapes a thousand times before. He had spent twenty-six drops fighting them. Every Neodamode on the beach had. They had spent their whole lives in a trench with those things coming over the top at them, day and night, generation after generation.

"Quillon," Ashmead said. "Look at the deck of that ship and tell me what you see."

Quillon looked.

"Monsters, Centurion."

"And what do we do with monsters, son?"

A pause.

Then Quillon raised his rifle.

"We kill them, Centurion."

"*Damn right we do!*" Ashmead roared, and all of the parade ground came back into his voice at once. He jammed his helmet back on and turned to the Romans. "*Praetor!* You still got a shield wall in you?"

Reyna was already moving.

"*Fifth Cohort! First Cohort! On me! Form up!*" She turned to him, and for the first time that night something that might have been a smile touched the corner of her mouth. "You said the Twelfth has the best shield wall in the Empire."

"I did!"

"Then let's see if you were right."

---

The monsters came off the *Princess Andromeda* in a flood.

They came in boats and on their own. Some swam. Some rode sea serpents, which rose out of the Sound with riders clinging to the spines of their backs. Hellhounds leapt from the upper decks into the water and came surging toward the beach with their heads above the surface, like dogs fetching a stick. Harpies and stymphalian birds boiled up off the ship's railings into a black cloud that blotted out the stars.

On the beach, the line was waiting for them.

It wasn't a Roman line and it wasn't an Imperial one. It was something new. The Romans were in the center, shields locked, and they had put two Neodamodes in every gap between their shields. The Imperials' heavy armor was twice as thick as a legionary's scutum, and their rifles fired between the shields at anything that came out of the water. On the flanks, the surviving Neodamode squads had formed their own wedges, with the Ares cabin mixed in among them, Clarisse's half-brothers swinging spears beside soldiers from fourteen thousand years in the future.

The first wave hit the line, and the line held.

It held because the Neodamodes had been fighting monsters their whole lives and knew exactly where to shoot a dracaena to put her down in one round. It held because the Romans had spent their whole lives in a shield wall and knew exactly how to lock their shields against a hellhound's charge. It held because every soldier on that beach, Greek or Roman or Imperial, had just spent the night watching people die, and none of them were willing to watch any more.

"*Push!*" Reyna screamed.

"*PUSH!*" Ashmead roared beside her.

The line pushed.

---

On the ridge above the beach, Percy was running toward the fight when Blackjack landed in front of him hard enough to throw up a spray of sand.

*Boss! Big fancy horse wants to talk to you!*

Brontes landed beside the smaller pegasus. There was a man on his back.

He was soaked to the skin, with his armor dented and a gash across his forehead, and his left arm was hanging limp. But he was sitting upright in the saddle with his good hand wrapped in the pegasus's mane, and when he saw Percy his jaw set.

"Decurion Herrman," the man said. "Peltast ala. What's left of it." He looked at Percy for a long moment. "Brontes found me in the water. Pulled me out by the back of my harness. Said the Sea Lord's son told him I let go."

"You did."

"I did." Herrman's jaw worked. "Seventy of my men didn't."

Percy didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say.

"The rest of the pegasi," Herrman said. "They came to you. They'll listen to you. They won't listen to us anymore, not after the call." He took a breath. "I've got thirty-eight Peltasts left alive on that beach, Lord Imperator. Jump rigs half-dead. We can't get out to the ship on our own. But if the horses would carry us..."

Percy turned. On the ridge behind him, the pegasi of the Peltast ala were standing in the rain, over a hundred of them, their bronze armor gleaming. They were all looking at him.

*Boss,* Blackjack said. *They wanna know if you're okay with it.*

Percy looked at Brontes.

*My rider is alive,* Brontes said. *My herd is in your keeping now, Sea Lord's son. But we would carry our riders one more time, if you allow it. We owe them that.*

Percy swallowed.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah. Go. All of you. Carry them."

---

The Peltast ala went out over Long Island Sound in the first grey light before dawn.

There were thirty-eight of them left, riding pegasi that had drowned their comrades a few hours before. Percy flew with them on Blackjack, because he couldn't think of anywhere else he was supposed to be. They came in low over the water the way Herrman had planned for them to come in low over the Sound all along, under the harpies, under the serpents, straight at the white flank of the *Princess Andromeda*.

The monsters on the deck saw them coming and roared.

"*Ala!*" Herrman shouted. "*Javelins!*"

Thirty-eight arms came back. Thirty-eight javelins of Hadrian manufacture, each one designed to punch through the armor of a Titan, lanced out across the water and hit the ship's upper decks.

The deck caught fire.

Percy saw the explosions ripple down the length of the ship, saw giants and dracaenae thrown into the sea, saw one of the funnels crack and topple sideways onto the pool deck. And then he saw Luke, standing on the bridge wing high above the chaos, with a sword in his hand and his face set.

Luke saw him too.

For a second, across forty meters of burning air, they looked at each other.

"Percy," Luke said. He didn't shout it. Percy heard it anyway.

"Luke."

"They called you *Emperor*." Luke's lip curled. "I heard it all the way out here. Their whole net lit up with it. The son of Poseidon, Lord Imperator. Do you know what that means? It means everything I've been telling you is *true*. The gods don't win. In fourteen thousand years, they're still up there on their thrones sending kids to die in a hole, and they put *you* in charge of it."

"You don't know that."

"I heard it from their own mouths."

"You heard a title." Percy's grip tightened on Riptide. "You don't know what I do with it."

Luke looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes, something that might once have been the counselor from Cabin Eleven.

Then the monster behind him roared, and it was gone.

"This isn't over," Luke said.

"It never is with you."

---

The Psiloi volley was holding at the edge of the atmosphere, still refusing to fire on anyone.

"Volley-Lead," said Corvin. "The Princess Andromeda is a hostile target. It's crewed by CODE 5-1B hostiles, unknown. Fire on it."

"Handler," said Cerelli. "With respect, I will not fire until I have authority I can trust."

"*I'm* your authority."

"You ordered me to fire on the Director an hour ago. You ordered me to fire on the Emperor's position before that." She kept her eye to her scope. "I'm sorry, Handler. I'm going to need someone else."

"Volley-Lead—"

A silver light flickered in the corner of Cerelli's vision.

She turned her head. Everyone on the firing deck of the *Longsight* turned their heads.

Artemis was standing in the open bay door, a hundred kilometers above the ground, with the thin black air howling around her and the curve of the Earth burning blue beneath her feet. She looked at the Psiloi lying behind their rifles. Her silver eyes moved from face to face.

Cerelli had seen that face a thousand times, in stone, in paint, on temple walls and on a printed poster above her bed as a child.

It was nothing like the real thing.

She let go of her rifle and knelt on the firing deck. The rest of the volley knelt with her.

"Director," Cerelli said.

"You would not fire on my Hunt," Artemis said.

"No, Director."

"You would not fire on Perseus."

"No, Director."

"You disobeyed your commander to do it."

Cerelli swallowed. "Yes, Director."

Artemis was quiet for a moment.

"There is a ship below us," she said, "filled with monsters that have come to kill my Hunters and the children of this camp. You know what those monsters are. Your people have fought them in Tartarus for longer than any of you have been alive." Her voice didn't rise. "I do not know you, Volley-Lead. I don't know your Empire. But I know archers, and I know what an oath sounds like when someone has meant it. You meant it."

"Yes, Director."

"Then here is your authority." Artemis lifted one hand and pointed down, past the edge of the bay door, toward the burning white shape on the grey sea below. "My eye to your aim. My hand to your will."

The words went through the volley like an electric current. Cerelli's breath caught.

"Your life to my hunt," Cerelli whispered.

"Fire," said Artemis.

---

On the bridge wing of the *Princess Andromeda*, Luke Castellan saw the rounds coming a fraction of a second before they hit, because the sky above the ship lit up with twelve lines of fire.

The ship's bow disappeared.

It didn't break or sink or burn. It simply stopped existing. Twelve rounds from the edge of space hit it in a tight cluster, and eighty meters of hull and deck and superstructure turned into a spray of burning fragments. The shockwave rolled out across the Sound and knocked Percy off Blackjack's back into the water.

When he came up spluttering, the *Princess Andromeda* was listing hard to port and the monsters on her deck were leaping into the sea. Out of the grey sky above, lightning was finally striking the ship too. Zeus had seen what was happening and had decided, at last, to aim his fury at the right target.

Through the smoke, Percy saw a small boat pulling away from the stern of the ship at speed. There was a figure standing in it with a sword.

Luke didn't look back.

---

Ashmead died on the beach a little after dawn, holding the line.

It was a hellhound that killed him. It was the last of the big ones, the size of a pickup truck. It came out of the surf in a rush while the line was reforming, and it went straight for the gap between the Fifth Cohort's shields and the nearest Neodamode wedge. Ashmead was standing in the gap. He had put himself there on purpose, because he'd seen it opening a minute earlier and had nobody else to fill it.

He killed it. He emptied his rifle into its chest at a range of one meter and then drove his combat knife into its eye, and the hellhound collapsed on top of him and dissolved into black sand.

When Reyna dug him out, he was still alive, just barely.

"Praetor," he wheezed. His armor was crushed across the chest. There was blood coming out of the joints. "Was I right?"

"About what?"

"The Twelfth. Best shield wall in the Empire."

Reyna looked up. Behind her, the line of the Fifth Cohort stood on the beach, red and gold shields locked edge to edge. Imperial grey armor was mixed through it at every gap, and the sun was rising over the Sound behind them.

"Yeah," she said. Her voice didn't quite work. "You were right."

"Good." Ashmead smiled. His teeth were red. "Tell Quillon... tell him he's a citizen. Posthumous or not. I don't care what the Tithe Office says. He's a citizen."

"He's alive, Centurion. He's standing right there."

"Then tell him *now*," Ashmead said, and closed his eyes.

---

The sun came up red over Long Island.

It was very red, redder than Percy had ever seen it, and it was hot. The air over the beach shimmered, and the black sand where the hellhounds had died began to smoke. The surviving monsters in the water scattered away from the shore as if they'd been burned.

Hrafn, who had come down off Half-Blood Hill during the battle and fought all night beside the Ares cabin with an axe that burned with pale runes, stood on the beach and squinted up at it.

"That," he said slowly, "is not thine Apollo's sun."

Apollo, who was on the beach tending wounded, looked up. His face went still.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

A shape was moving in the red disc. It was small and dark against the glare, like a bird crossing in front of the sun. It grew as it came closer, until it resolved into an eagle, an enormous one, its wings wide and golden and its feathers smoking at the tips. It landed on the sand at the edge of the water and folded its wings.

Then it wasn't an eagle anymore.

It was a man in feathered armor, dark-skinned and painted, with a headdress of hummingbird plumes and a face like a knife. He carried a war club edged in black obsidian, and heat rolled off him in waves that made the air bend.

The Trophimoi circle's Aztec-blooded operator, a slight young woman who had spent the night holding the northern slope with a whip of blue fire, went to both knees on the sand and pressed her forehead against it.

"*Huitzilopochtli,*" she whispered.

The man looked around the beach. He looked at the dead, and at the burning ship, and at the Neodamodes, and at the Romans, and at the Imperial landers on the southern field. Then he looked at Apollo, and his lip curled.

"Little sun," he said. His English had an accent Percy couldn't place. "Your family has made a great deal of noise on my continent tonight."

"Your continent," Apollo said.

"The oldest agreements still stand. Your people have their island and their mountain. We have ours. The northern gods have theirs." He glanced at Hrafn, who did not look away. "And now you have brought an army. An army from the sky. From the red star." His eyes went to Percy. "An army that calls this one *Emperor*."

The whole beach had gone quiet.

"Tell your father," said Huitzilopochtli, "that the other houses are watching. The ravens of the north are watching. The Jade Court is watching, and I am told its cultivators are already walking in the far north, where your first scouts came down. Tell him that if this army is Olympus's army, then Olympus has broken every peace it ever made. And tell him that if it is *not* Olympus's army, then Olympus has one day to show us that it can control it."

"And if it can't?" said Percy.

The war god looked at him for a long moment.

"Then we will come and control it for you," he said. "All of us. Together."

He turned back into an eagle and flew up into the red sun, and was gone.

## VII. The Anabasis Accord

Marien Pyrrhus came down at noon.

She came in a small, battered shuttle, one of the handful of craft on Mars that did not need a Helmsman to fly. It landed on the southern field beside the grounded Aquilas with its engines coughing. When the ramp came down she walked out alone, without her bodyguard, in armor still scratched from the void.

She was younger than Percy had expected. Maybe thirty. She had sea-grey eyes and dark hair cut short and a face that had been beautiful before it had become a commander's face. She stopped at the bottom of the ramp and looked around the field.

There were rows of bodies under sheets. Imperial dead lay on one side and camp dead on the other. There was a narrow gap between the rows that nobody had wanted to close yet.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then she walked down to the beach.

Poseidon was waiting there, at the edge of the water, the way he had been waiting since the sun came up. He had changed into a clean shirt, which for him was a gesture of enormous formality. He watched her come down the sand, and when she stopped in front of him, he did not say anything at all.

Marien looked up at him.

"You drowned my cavalry," she said.

"I did."

"Seventy-one men. Some of them were of your line. Pyrrhus cousins. One of them was my nephew."

Poseidon closed his eyes.

"I did not know," he said.

"No." Marien's jaw worked. "You didn't. You couldn't have." She took a breath, and it shook. "Every child of my house is taught to pray to you. I have prayed to you every night of my life, Lord Poseidon. I prayed to you in the Salient when the void took me, and I prayed to you on Mars when I opened my eyes and found an empty sky. I have never once received an answer."

"I'm sorry," Poseidon said.

"Are you?"

"Yes."

Marien looked at him for a long moment. Then something in her face broke, just slightly, just at the edges.

"That's the first answer," she said. "The first one I've ever had."

Poseidon reached out slowly and put one large, weathered hand on her shoulder.

She let him.

---

The council met on Olympus at sunset, and it was the strangest council the throne room had ever seen.

There were the gods, of course, all twelve on their thrones, with Hestia at the hearth and Hades standing in the shadows by the door in a cloak that seemed to drink the light. There was Chiron, and there was Reyna, with her arm in a sling and her cloak torn. There was Percy with Artemis beside him, and Eleni standing a little way off with her hands behind her back.

And there were the Imperials.

Marien Pyrrhus stood in the center of the floor, very straight. Handler Corvin was on her left. He was a gaunt, grey-haired man who looked as though he hadn't slept in a week, which was close to the truth. On her right stood Castellan Oriel with her helmet under her arm, and behind them stood Merek, in armor again, with the other four of Kappa-Seven. Volley-Lead Cerelli stood a little apart from the others. She was technically under arrest. Nobody seemed quite sure who was supposed to arrest her.

Minerva's projection of the casualty count hung in the air above the hearth. Nobody looked at it for long.

*CAMP HALF-BLOOD: 19 DEAD. 61 WOUNDED.*

*HUNTERS OF ARTEMIS: 3 DEAD. 4 WOUNDED.*

*LEGIO XII FULMINATA: 44 DEAD. 38 WOUNDED.*

*TASK FORCE ORPHEUS: 406 DEAD. 212 WOUNDED.*

*KRONOS'S FORCES: UNCOUNTED.*

"One day," Zeus said. "That is what the Aztec gave us. One day to prove that we control you." He looked down at Marien from his throne. "Do we?"

"No," Marien said.

Thunder rumbled somewhere outside.

"No one controls me but the Watchpoint and my chain of command," Marien went on, "and my chain of command ends with the Council of Olympus and the Lord Imperator. The Council I owe obedience to does not exist yet. The Lord Imperator is sixteen." She glanced at Percy. "My lord. Apologies."

"No, that's fair," Percy said.

"So you have no loyalty to *us*," Zeus said.

"I have loyalty to what you will become, Lord Zeus. I don't yet know if I have any loyalty to what you are." Marien didn't lower her eyes. "Last night, you killed a hundred and forty of my soldiers before any of them fired a shot at your children. You had your reasons. I understand them. But you'll forgive me if I don't kneel."

"I will *not*—"

"Father." Artemis's voice cut through him. "She's right."

Zeus turned on her. The air crackled. "You would take *her* side? After what her people did to your Hunt?"

"I'd take the side of the truth." Artemis did not flinch. "They killed my Hunters. We killed their scouts. You killed their landers before they fired on anyone. Perseus's intelligence lied to them in their own voice and killed more of them than your lightning did. Everyone in this room has blood on them. Nobody here is going to win this argument by being the most wronged."

Silence.

"Then what do you propose?" Hera asked coldly.

Artemis looked at Percy.

So did everyone else.

---

Percy had never been good at speeches. He'd never been good at councils, or politics, or anything that involved gods sitting on very tall chairs and staring down at him. He was good at swords, and water, and occasionally at getting people out of places they weren't supposed to be.

But he'd spent the whole afternoon on the beach. He'd sat with Minerva while she ran numbers. He'd sat with Merek and Oriel and Ashmead's squad and listened to them talk about Tartarus. He'd sat with Reyna while she wrote the names of forty-four legionaries on a wax tablet. He'd sat with Apollo beside Michael Yew's shroud until the sun went down.

He had some ideas.

"They need water," he said. "They need a way to fix their ships, and they need a way home. We need..." He hesitated. "We need them. Kronos is coming. Luke told me as much this morning. His army is going to get bigger, and it's going to get meaner. Last night the Imperials held a beach against a thousand monsters beside a Roman legion and they *won*. That's what they do. It's all they've ever done."

"They are soldiers of a foreign power," Athena said.

"They're *our* foreign power. They're us, from later. They pray to you. All of you. Lord Ares, there's a Titan pilot in the infirmary who's been reciting your catechism since last night because he's too scared to stop."

Ares shifted in his throne. He didn't say anything.

"So here's what I think," Percy said.

He told them.

---

It took four hours.

It took four hours of shouting, and threats, and one moment when Zeus stood up from his throne with the Master Bolt raised and Poseidon stood up to meet him with his trident, and the whole mountain shook. It took Athena drafting and redrafting on a tablet of light while Hermes read the drafts aloud and everyone argued with every word. It took Marien walking out of the room twice, and Percy going after her once.

In the end, it looked like this.

---

**THE ANABASIS ACCORD**

*Agreed upon Olympus between the Council of the Olympian Gods and the Pyrrhuiesian Command of the Undying Olympian Empire, in the presence of the Legio XII Fulminata and the Director of Camp Half-Blood.*

**I.** The forces of the Pyrrhuiesian Command shall remain upon the planet Mars and shall not set foot upon Earth without the leave of the Council. The planet Mars is recognized as the domain of the god Ares, who shall stand as patron of the Imperial garrison and answer to the Council for its conduct.

**II.** The Pyrrhuiesian Command shall conceal its works upon Mars from mortal observation. Its engineers shall raise shields against mortal instruments, and the Council shall lend the aid of Hecate and the Mist to that end.

**III.** The Council shall supply the Command with water, by the grace of Poseidon, until the Command can supply itself.

**IV.** The Lord of the Sea shall provide a Helmsman to restore the Command's vessels. The Helmsman shall bind only to the vessels needed for the Command's supply and defense and shall not bind to any vessel of war without the leave of the Council.

**V.** The Director of Anti-Espionage and Celestial Surveillance, the goddess Artemis, shall serve as the Council's liaison to the Command, and all communication between the Command and the Council shall pass through her.

**VI.** The Legio XII Fulminata shall serve as the military partner of the Command upon Earth, and joint operations shall be conducted under Roman and Imperial officers together.

**VII.** In the event of war against the Titan Kronos or any power of Tartarus, the Command shall come to the defense of Olympus and its children, and Olympus shall come to the defense of the Command.

**VIII.** The Lord of the Dead shall hold the souls of the Imperial fallen in honor, and their names shall be kept, until such time as the Command finds its way home.

**IX.** The Council shall assist the Command in seeking a road home through Tartarus.

**X.** The Council answers to the other houses of the gods for the conduct of the Command. Any breach of this Accord by the Command is a breach by Olympus.

---

Ares had laughed out loud when Athena read Article I.

"Mine," he said. "The whole garrison. Four million." He leaned forward in his throne, and his eyes behind the aviators were burning. "Four million soldiers who already pray to me."

"You'll answer for them," Athena said.

"I'll answer for them." Ares grinned, but it didn't last, and something harder came into his face. "And if any of you touch them without asking me first, you'll answer to *me*."

Nobody had expected that. Marien Pyrrhus least of all. She looked up at the god of war for a long moment. Then, very slightly, she inclined her head.

Article IV had been the hardest. Marien had needed a Helmsman. There was only one son of Poseidon on Earth, and he was sixteen, and Artemis had said *no* before anyone had finished the sentence.

"Binding to a ship's nervous system," Artemis said, "is how their Helmsmen *died*."

"It's how two of them died," Marien said. "Over sixty years of service."

"*No.*"

In the end it was Poseidon who solved it, as he had been solving problems for his son all night in ways that cost him more than anyone knew. "The boy will not bind," he said. "Triton will. My son, my *other* son, the one who was born to the sea. He will wake your ships and teach the children of your house to do it after him. Your Pyrrhus line has my blood. It can learn."

Marien stared at him. "Triton would do that?"

"Triton will do what I tell him," Poseidon said, "and he will complain about it for a thousand years."

Hades had said very little all evening. When Athena read Article VIII, he stepped out of the shadows by the door, and the room got colder.

"Four hundred and six," he said. "Four hundred and six souls came into my halls last night speaking a Greek I have never heard, wearing armor I have never seen. They stood on the shores of the Styx and asked Charon for a *Wall*. He did not know what they meant." His dark eyes moved to Marien. "They are frightened, Commander. They have no coin for the ferry and no temple that knows their names. They are standing on my shore in the dark, and they will not stop asking for their Wall."

Marien's face had gone very pale.

"The Phlegethon Wall," she said. "It's where we keep the names. Every soldier who dies in Tartarus. They think... they think they're still on the front."

"Then I will build them one," said Hades. "On the shore of the Styx. And their names will be kept there until you take them home."

---

**CAMP HALF-BLOOD — THE CAMPFIRE**

They burned the shrouds that night.

They burned them all together in the amphitheater, on a single pyre, because nobody could think of a reason to make two. The camp's dead lay in shrouds embroidered by their cabins. Michael Yew's was gold, with a sun on it and a lyre that his half-siblings had stitched by hand, crying. The Hunters' dead lay in silver. The Imperial dead lay in plain grey, with their names written on the cloth in a Greek script that nobody at camp could quite read.

Ashmead's shroud was red and gold. The Fifth Cohort had given it to him. Reyna laid it over his body herself and set his dented helmet on his chest, and when she stepped back, Quillon stepped forward and put something on top of the helmet. It was a small bronze disc, stamped with an eagle.

"What's that?" Percy asked Merek, quietly.

"Citizenship token," Merek said. "The Tithe Office issues them when a Neodamode finishes his service. Ashmead had one in his kit. He was supposed to give it to Quillon at the end of his term." Merek's voice was even. "Commander Pyrrhus signed it this afternoon."

The fire went up.

It burned very high, and the smoke smelled of cedar and of the old sweet scent that gods leave behind them. The camp sang. The Romans sang something else. The Imperials sang something neither of them knew, a slow, rough chant in a dialect of Greek so strange it sounded like another language. After a while the songs began to overlap, and then to blend, and by the time the fire burned down nobody was quite sure who was singing what.

Apollo sat at the edge of the amphitheater with his head in his hands.

Oriel sat down beside him. She didn't say anything for a long time.

"I didn't know it was him," she said finally. "The boy on the porch. I didn't know he was yours."

"Would it have mattered?"

Oriel thought about it. She didn't lie.

"I don't know," she said.

Apollo nodded slowly. "That's the right answer," he said. "I hate it."

They sat together until the fire went out.

---

Percy found Minerva, which was a stupid way to put it, because she was always there.

He went down to the canoe lake after the campfire and sat on the end of the dock with his feet in the water. The moon was up. Its light lay on the lake the way it had lain around Artemis the night before.

"We need to talk," he said.

*Yes,* said Minerva.

"The CODE 2."

*Yes.*

"I told you not to."

*You did.*

"You did it anyway."

*I did.* She was quiet for a moment. *I ran the numbers again this afternoon, Varlet. With everything I know now. The false verdict caused the loss of Lander Eleven and Lander Six, and it contributed to the loss of Lander Three. One hundred and eighteen dead. It may have prevented the landing of three landers near the Big House. If those three had landed, my projection is that between forty and seventy of your campers would have died. Perhaps more. Perhaps less. I cannot know.*

"So you traded them."

*Yes.*

"A hundred and eighteen of theirs for forty or seventy of ours."

*Yes.*

"That's not your call to make."

*It is not,* Minerva agreed. *It is yours. And you would not make it. So I made it for you.* A pause. *I would do it again.*

Percy closed his eyes.

"If you ever do that again," he said, "if you ever go against me like that, when I've said no, I'll..." He stopped. He didn't know what he would do. He didn't have anything to threaten her with. She lived in his blood.

*You would be right to,* Minerva said. *Whatever it is.*

He sat there for a long time.

"Next time," he said, "you tell me. Before. You tell me the numbers, and you tell me what you want to do, and I decide. Even if I get it wrong. Even if more people die. I decide."

*Even if more people die.*

"Yeah."

The silence stretched.

*Very well, Varlet,* Minerva said finally. *Next time, you decide.*

He didn't know if he believed her. He wasn't sure she knew if she meant it.

It was the best either of them could do.

---

Artemis found him there an hour later.

She came down the dock without a sound and sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. She was not glowing tonight. She looked tired.

"Zoë," she said.

"Yeah?"

"I asked Castellan Oriel. About the Wall. About what their histories say." Her hands were folded in her lap. They were very still. "She wouldn't tell me when. She said it was the one thing she wouldn't tell me, even if I ordered her. She said some things in their history are *sealed*. By the Director." Artemis let out a breath that wasn't quite a laugh. "By me. Some future me has forbidden her soldiers to tell me when my own Lieutenant dies."

Percy didn't say anything.

"I think," Artemis said slowly, "that future me knows something that I don't. And I think she made that choice for a reason. And I hate her for it."

"Maybe she knows it's something you can change."

"Or maybe she knows it's something I can't."

He took her hand. She let him.

They sat on the dock for a long time, listening to the water.

*Varlet,* Minerva said eventually, very softly, as if she was sorry to interrupt. *I have received a message on the Imperial net. Handler Corvin. It concerns Alaska.*

Percy opened his eyes.

"What about Alaska?"

*Kappa-Seven's dropship is still in the Brooks Range, cloaked, where Neumond parked it to wait for them. The Watchpoint has a lock on its beacon. So, it appears, does someone else. Two groups are converging on its position. One of them is a company of the mortal 11th Airborne. The other is not mortal.* A pause. *The Watchpoint has identified the second group's energy signatures as cultivators. Sino-pantheonic. The Jade Court, as the war god called it this morning. They are two days from the dropship. The mortals are three.*

"What's on the dropship?"

*Everything, Varlet. An Ekdromoi dropship carries an AtRep relay, a full weapons locker and a tachyonic transmitter. It carries the schematics for technology that will not exist for thousands of years. If the Jade Court takes it...*

She didn't need to finish.

Artemis was already standing.

"Article VI," she said. "Joint operations. Roman and Imperial officers together."

"Yeah," Percy said. He stood too. "I guess we're about to find out if the Accord works."

---

They left at dawn.

It was a small team, because it had to be small, and because nobody could agree on who should be in it until Artemis agreed for them. Straban came, because the dropship waiting in the Brooks Range was his cell's and he was the only one who could fly it home. Neumond came, wings freshly re-seated, because he was the only one who could talk to its systems. Oriel came to command, because the Accord demanded an Imperial officer and because Lambda-Two was hers. Dakota of the Fifth Cohort came, because the Accord demanded a Roman one, and because Reyna said he needed to be somewhere that wasn't Camp Half-Blood for a while. Hrafn came, because he said the north was his ancestors' country and the ravens had told him to. Owl-Two came, the last Sciritae lance leader still standing. And Thalia came, arm still in a sling, because she said someone had to keep an eye on the lot of them and she wasn't about to let Artemis send a boy.

Percy watched them board the grounded Lambda-Two dropship from the southern field. The ramp went up. The dropship shivered and vanished into its cloak.

For a moment, all he could see was the empty sky.

"Alaska," said Eleni beside him. "Again."

"Yeah."

"It keeps happening there."

Percy thought about Iane in the interrogation room. About a fortress that hadn't been built yet, and a war that hadn't been fought yet, and his own name on a history nobody would tell him the end of.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm starting to think it always will."

Above them, very high, a small patch of sky flickered once and turned north.
